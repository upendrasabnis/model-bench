19. **1. What Is an Acquittal Report?**  
20. An acquittal is the formal verification that a grant award has been spent in accordance with the approved budget, program objectives, and applicable regulations.  
21. It serves as the funder’s “receipt” that the money was used for the intended purpose.  
22. Acquittal reports can be narrative, tabular, or a combination of both, depending on the funder’s specifications.  
23. The report typically includes:  
24. • A summary of expenditures by budget category  
25. • A description of activities completed with the funds  
26. • Evidence of outcomes or outputs (e.g., number of beneficiaries served)  
27. • Any unspent balances and the reason for retention (if allowed)  
28. • Certification signatures from authorized officials

29. **2. Why Acquittal Reporting Matters**  
30. Demonstrates stewardship of public or philanthropic dollars.  
31. Maintains or improves the organization’s credibility with current and prospective funders.  
32. Fulfills legal and contractual obligations, avoiding penalties or claw‑backs.  
33. Provides data for internal performance measurement and future budgeting.  
34. Enables transparency for stakeholders, board members, and the public.  
35. Facilitates continuity of funding by showing compliance history.

36. **3. Core Components of an Acquittal Submission**  
37. 3.1. Cover Sheet / Submission Header  
38. • Grant number, award title, reporting period, and contact information.  
39. 3.2. Budget Reconciliation Table  
40. • Line‑item spending versus approved budget (often in an Excel or spreadsheet format).  
41. • Columns for “Original Budget,” “Revised Budget,” “Actual Expenditures,” and “Variance.”  
42. 3.3. Narrative Summary  
43. • Description of activities carried out during the reporting period.  
44. • Highlights of achievements, challenges, and lessons learned.  
45. 3.4. Output/Outcome Metrics  
46. • Quantitative results (e.g., “Delivered 1,200 meals to 300 seniors”).  
47. • Qualitative outcomes (e.g., “Improved community awareness of mental‑health resources”).  
48. 3.5. Supporting Documentation  
49. • Receipts, invoices, contracts, and vendor statements.  
50. • Timesheets, payroll records, and sub‑grantee reports (if applicable).  
51. 3.6. Certification Statement  
52. • Signed declaration that the information is true, complete, and compliant with the award terms.

53. **4. Common Requirements Across Funders**  
54. Government (Federal, State, Local)  
55. • OMB Circular A‑133 (now part of the Uniform Guidance, 2 CFR 200) for federal awards.  
56. • State-specific grant statutes (e.g., California’s “Grant Management Manual”).  
57. • Mandatory electronic submission via Grants.gov, SAM.gov, or state portals.  
58. • Minimum retention period of 3‑7 years for financial records.  
59. • Specific language for “allowable costs” and “unallowable costs.”  
60. • Requirement to report on “sub‑award” or “sub‑grantee” activities.  
61. Private Foundations & Philanthropic Funders  
62. • More flexible formatting but often require a narrative plus budget reconciliation.  
63. • May request outcome data, case studies, or qualitative testimonials.  
64. • Some foundations require a “grant close‑out” letter when the award ends early.  
65. • Reporting frequency can be quarterly, semi‑annual, or annual, depending on the grant size.  
66. • Foundations may impose “restricted use” clauses that dictate how unspent funds must be handled.

67. **5. Record‑Keeping Best Practices**  
68. Maintain a master grant file for each award, containing:  
69. • Award letter, amendment letters, and award terms.  
70. • Approved budget and any budget revisions.  
71. • All invoices, receipts, and payment vouchers.  
72. • Timesheets and payroll records for personnel directly charged to the grant.  
73. • Sub‑grantee agreements and their own financial reports.  
74. • Correspondence with the funder regarding budget changes or spending questions.  
75. Use a standardized coding system (e.g., object‑class, function‑class) to tag each expense.  
76. Store electronic files in a read‑only, backed‑up repository with version control.  
77. Implement a “grant ledger” that mirrors the accounting system’s chart of accounts.  
78. Conduct quarterly internal reconciliations to catch discrepancies early.  
79. Keep a “paper trail” for any cost that is borderline in terms of allowability.  
80. Document the rationale for any unspent balance and the plan for its use.

81. **6. Internal Controls and Documentation Strategies**  
82. Establish a “Grant Management SOP” (Standard Operating Procedure) that outlines:  
83. • Who is authorized to approve expenditures.  
84. • Required documentation for each type of expense.  
85. • Review and sign‑off workflow (e.g., staff → finance → director → board).  
86. • Escalation path for unallowable cost identification.  
87. Use a “grant compliance checklist” for each reporting cycle.  
88. Conduct periodic training for staff on allowable vs. unallowable costs.  
89. Maintain a “cost‑allowability matrix” that maps common expense types to funder policies.  
90. Keep a log of all “budget reallocations” and the justification for each.  
91. Store signed certifications and audit trails in a secure, access‑controlled system.  
92. Perform annual self‑audits to simulate external audit conditions.  
93. Create a “post‑audit action plan” for any findings and track remediation.

94. **7. Navigating Audits and Site Visits**  
95. Understand the audit scope:  
96. • Pre‑award (application) review, post‑award (expenditure) audit, and close‑out audit.  
97. • Audits can be “desk” (document‑only) or “on‑site” (field visit).  
98. Prepare a “Documentation Package” that includes:  
99. • Original award letter and any amendments.  
100. • Full set of financial records for the reporting period.  
101. • Copies of all acquittal submissions and related correspondence.  
102. • Evidence of compliance with programmatic requirements (e.g., service logs).  
103. • Policies and procedures that demonstrate adherence to Uniform Guidance.  
104. During an on‑site audit, be ready to:  
105. • Provide original receipts or electronic scans.  
106. • Walk auditors through the grant ledger.  
107. • Explain any variances between budget and actual spend.  
108. • Answer questions about sub‑grantee oversight.  
109. If audit findings are issued, respond within the funder’s deadline with:  
110. • A corrective action plan (CAP).  
111. • Evidence of implementation (e.g., revised invoices, updated policies).  
112. Document all communications with the audit agency for future reference.

113. **8. Frequently Cited Compliance Failures**  
114. 8.1. Misclassification of Costs  
115. • Charging personal expenses to the grant.  
116. • Using “indirect cost” rates without proper justification.  
117. 8.2. Inadequate Documentation  
118. • Missing receipts for small purchases.  
119. • Failure to retain sub‑grantee invoices.  
120. 8.3. Unallowable Activities  
121. • Using grant funds for lobbying or political campaigning.  
122. • Paying for capital equipment not approved in the budget.  
123. 8.4. Late or Incomplete Submissions  
124. • Missing the reporting deadline.  
125. • Submitting a narrative that does not align with the budget reconciliation.  
126. 8.5. Over‑reliance on Estimates  
127. • Reporting projected spend as actual without proper documentation.  
128. 8.6. Lack of Segregation of Duties  
129. • One person both approves and processes payments, increasing fraud risk.  
130. 8.7. Failure to Report Unspent Balances  
131. • Not disclosing remaining funds when required.  
132. 8.8. Ignoring “Matching” Requirements  
133. • Not providing the required cash or in‑kind match.  
134. 8.9. Inadequate Outcome Measurement  
135. • Reporting activities without tying them to measurable outcomes.  
136. 8.10. Poor Communication with Funders  
137. • Not responding to funder queries in a timely manner.

169. **10. Step‑by‑Step Checklist for a Clean Acquittal**  
170. 1. Retrieve the original award letter and any amendments.  
171. 2. Pull the approved budget and any budget revisions.  
172. 3. Gather all invoices, receipts, and payment vouchers for the reporting period.  
173. 4. Reconcile expenditures against the budget line‑by‑line.  
174. 5. Prepare the budget reconciliation table (Excel or required format).  
175. 6. Write the narrative summary, aligning activities with outcomes.  
176. 7. Compile supporting documentation (photos, service logs, outcome metrics).  
177. 8. Review the draft acquittal for completeness and accuracy.  
178. 9. Obtain required signatures (authorized official, finance director, board chair).  
179. 10. Submit the acquittal through the funder’s portal before the deadline.  
180. 11. Archive all supporting documents in the grant file for the required retention period.  
181. 12. Monitor for any funder follow‑up questions and respond promptly.  
182. 13. If an audit is announced, prepare the audit documentation package.  
183. 14. Conduct a post‑submission debrief to capture lessons learned for the next grant.

184. **11. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)**  
185. Q1: *Do I need an acquittal if the grant is fully expended?*  
186. A: Yes. Most funders require a final acquittal even if the award is closed early, to confirm that all expenditures were allowable.  

---  

187. Q2: *Can I use the same acquittal template for every funder?*  
188. A: While a master template is useful, each funder may have unique formatting or content requirements; always tailor the submission accordingly.  

---  

189. Q3: *What if I discover an error after submission?*  
190. A: Submit a corrected acquittal as soon as possible, accompanied by an explanation and supporting documentation; many funders accept a “correction” if communicated promptly.  

---  

191. Q4: *How long should I retain grant records?*  
192. A: Federal awards: at least 3 years after final close‑out, but many agencies recommend 7 years. State and private foundations often require 3‑5 years; check the specific award terms.  

---  

193. Q5: *Do I need a certified public accountant (CPA) to sign the acquittal?*  
194. A: Not always, but many funders require a CPA‑reviewed financial statement for awards above a certain dollar threshold (e.g., >$500,000).  

---  

195. Q6: *What is the difference between “allowable” and “unallowable” costs?*  
196. A: Allowable costs are those that directly support the grant’s purpose and are explicitly listed as permissible in the award terms. Unallowable costs are prohibited (e.g., lobbying, entertainment, personal expenses).  

---  

197. Q7: *Can unspent grant funds be carried over to a new award?*  
198. A: Only if the funder explicitly permits carry‑over or if the new award includes a “carry‑over” provision; otherwise, unused funds must be returned.  

---  

199. Q8: *How do I handle sub‑grantee reporting within an acquittal?*  
200. A: Include sub‑grantee invoices and their own financial reconciliations; ensure that sub‑grantee expenditures are reflected in your overall budget reconciliation.
