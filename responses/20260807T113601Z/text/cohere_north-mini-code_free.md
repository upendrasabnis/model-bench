# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*An In‑Depth Guide from Research to Post‑Award Reporting*  

---

## 1. Introduction  

Grant writing is both an art and a science. It requires a deep understanding of funder priorities, a crystal‑clear articulation of your organization’s mission, and the ability to translate impact into measurable outcomes. This guide walks you through every stage of the grant lifecycle, offering concrete examples, practical checklists, and tips you can apply immediately to increase your success rate.  

---  

## 2. Understanding the Grant Landscape  

### 2.1 Why Research Matters  

Before you even open a word processor, you need to know **who** you are writing to, **what** they fund, and **how** they evaluate proposals. Research eliminates wasted effort, aligns your proposal with funder language, and helps you avoid “spray‑and‑pray” submissions that dilute your resources.  

### 2.2 How to Research Funders  

| Step | Action | Resources |
|------|--------|-----------|
| **Identify** | List all potential funders (foundation directories, government grants, corporate giving). | Foundation Directory Online, Candid, GrantStation |
| **Read** | Review the funder’s website, annual reports, and recent grant announcements. | Funder’s “About Us”, “Grant Guidelines”, “Recent Awards” pages |
| **Analyze** | Extract key themes: priority issues, geographic focus, funding amounts, eligibility, application timeline. | Spreadsheet or grant management tool |
| **Network** | Attend funder conferences, webinars, or meet‑the‑grant‑maker events. | Local nonprofit associations, LinkedIn groups |
| **Document** | Capture notes in a “Funder Profile” template (see Checklist 2.1). | Internal database (e.g., Airtable, Google Sheet) |

#### Checklist 2.1 – Funder Research Checklist  

- [ ] Organization name, mission, and core values recorded  
- [ ] Funding focus areas (e.g., education, health, arts) noted  
- [ ] Geographic or demographic restrictions identified  
- [ ] Typical grant size range documented  
- [ ] Application deadlines and submission portals listed  
- [ ] Required documents (e.g., budget template, letters of support) captured  
- [ ] Contact person and communication preferences noted  
- [ ] Recent awarded projects and outcomes summarized  

### 2.3 Example: Funder Profile  

| Funder | Focus | Typical Grant Size | Deadline | Contact |
|--------|-------|-------------------|----------|---------|
| **The Jane Doe Foundation** | Youth STEM education (ages 8‑14) | $25,000‑$75,000 | 15 Oct | grants@janedoe.org |
| **United Way of County X** | Basic needs (food, housing, employment) | $10,000‑$50,000 | 1 Sep | grantinfo@uwcounty.org |

## 3. Developing the Proposal Core  

### 3.1 Aligning with the Funder’s Mission  

Your proposal must speak the funder’s language. Start each section with the funder’s priority phrase (e.g., “addressing food insecurity in underserved communities”).  

### 3.2 Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

A strong needs statement answers three questions: **What** problem exists, **Why** it matters now, and **Who** is affected.  

#### Example Needs Statement  

> *In County X, 1 in 5 children lives below the federal poverty line, and 28% lack access to reliable internet connectivity. This digital divide hinders academic achievement, limiting these students’ ability to complete homework, conduct research, and participate in online learning. Our program will directly address this gap by providing low‑income families with devices and subsidized broadband.*  

#### Checklist 3.2 – Needs Statement Checklist  

- [ ] Quantify the problem with current statistics or data  
- [ ] Explain why the problem is urgent (trends, gaps, or policy changes)  
- [ ] Identify the specific population(s) served (age, geography, ethnicity)  
- [ ] Highlight any existing service gaps or unmet needs  
- [ ] Connect the need to the funder’s stated priorities  

### 3.3 Writing the Mission Alignment Narrative  

- Begin with a concise statement of how the project supports the funder’s strategic goals.  
- Use the funder’s exact terminology.  
- Provide a “bridge” sentence that links your mission to theirs (e.g., “Your mission to empower underserved youth aligns with our goal to increase STEM literacy…”)

## 4. Program Description & Logic Model  

### 4.1 Why a Logic Model?  

A logic model is a visual roadmap that shows **Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes**. It helps you and the funder understand the causal chain and ensures you can measure impact.  

### 4.2 Core Components  

| Component | Description | Example (STEM Program) |
|-----------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Inputs** | Resources you bring to the project (staff, funding, materials). | $30,000 grant, 2 part‑time instructors, 20 laptops |
| **Activities** | Concrete actions you will take. | Conduct 8 weekend workshops, mentor students weekly |
| **Outputs** | Immediate, tangible products of activities. | 120 students participated, 20 project kits built |
| **Outcomes** | Short‑ and long‑term changes you aim to achieve. | Improved test scores (+15% average), increased confidence in coding |
| **Impact** | Broader, long‑term effects on the community. | Higher college enrollment rates in STEM fields |

### 4.3 Example Logic Model (Plain‑Text)  

```  
Inputs  
- $30,000 grant  
- 2 part‑time STEM instructors  
- 20 refurbished laptops  

Activities  
- Design curriculum aligned with state standards  
- Deliver 8 weekend workshops (4 hours each)  
- Provide after‑school tutoring sessions (2 hrs/week)  

Outputs  
- 120 student participants  
- 20 project kits completed  
- 15 teacher training hours  

Outcomes (Short‑term)  
- 15% increase in students’ pre‑post assessment scores  
- 80% participant satisfaction rating  

Outcomes (Long‑term)  
- 30% of participants enroll in STEM college majors (tracked 2 years later)  

Impact  
- Increased STEM literacy in underserved communities  
- Reduced achievement gap in local school district  
```  

#### Checklist 4.2 – Logic Model Checklist  

- [ ] List all resources (financial, human, in‑kind) under **Inputs**  
- [ ] Define specific, measurable activities under **Activities**  
- [ ] Quantify deliverables under **Outputs**  
- [ ] Identify both short‑term and long‑term outcomes with metrics  
- [ ] Explain how outcomes lead to broader impact

## 5. Objectives, Indicators, and Evaluation  

### 5.1 Setting SMART Objectives  

- **Specific** – Clear focus  
- **Measurable** – Quantifiable  
- **Achievable** – Realistic given resources  
- **Relevant** – Aligned with funder priorities  
- **Time‑bound** – Defined deadline  

#### Example Objective  

> *By June 2025, increase the number of low‑income high‑school students completing a basic coding bootcamp from 0 to 50, achieving a 90% satisfaction rating.*  

### 5.2 Choosing Indicators  

| Indicator Type | What It Measures | Data Source |
|----------------|------------------|-------------|
| **Input** | Number of participants enrolled | Registration forms |
| **Process** | Workshop attendance rate | Sign‑in sheets |
| **Output** | Projects completed | Project checklists |
| **Outcome** | Knowledge gain (pre/post test) | Standardized assessments |
| **Impact** | College enrollment in STEM (2‑year follow‑up) | School records (with consent) |

### 5.3 Evaluation Plan Overview  

1. **Design** – Choose quantitative (surveys, tests) and qualitative (interviews, focus groups) methods.  
2. **Timeline** – Align data collection with project phases (baseline, mid‑point, end‑point).  
3. **Budget** – Allocate funds for data collection tools (e.g., SurveyMonkey license, participant incentives).  
4. **Reporting** – Prepare concise reports for funder (see Section 8).  

#### Example Evaluation Timeline  

| Phase | Dates | Data Collected | Deliverable |
|-------|-------|----------------|-------------|
| Baseline | Month 0 | Pre‑survey, demographic info | Baseline report |
| Mid‑point | Month 3 | Quarterly progress survey | Mid‑term progress report |
| End‑point | Month 6 | Post‑survey, project artifacts | Final evaluation report |
| Follow‑up | Month 24 | College enrollment data | Impact assessment |

#### Checklist 5.3 – Evaluation Plan Checklist  

- [ ] SMART objectives documented  
- [ ] Indicators defined for each objective  
- [ ] Data collection methods selected (surveys, tests, observations)  
- [ ] Timeline with specific dates established  
- [ ] Budget line items for evaluation activities noted  
- [ ] Confidentiality and IRB considerations addressed

## 6. Budget Development  

### 6.1 Direct vs. Indirect Costs  

| Cost Type | Definition | Typical Examples |
|-----------|------------|------------------|
| **Direct Costs** | Expenses directly tied to project activities. | Staff salaries, supplies, travel for workshops |
| **Indirect Costs** | Organizational overhead allocated to the project. | Rent, utilities, administrative support (often a % of direct costs) |
| **Capital Costs** | One‑time purchases of equipment. | Laptops, projectors, software licenses |

### 6.2 Building a Clear Budget Narrative  

- **Explain each line item**: Why is it needed? How does it support the project?  
- **Use consistent formatting**: Align dollars, include a brief description, and note any in‑kind contributions.  
- **Show the math**: If you’re calculating a percentage of indirect costs, display the calculation.  

#### Example Budget Narrative (excerpt)  

> *Salaries for two part‑time instructors ($15,000) are budgeted to deliver the 8 workshops and ongoing tutoring. Supplies (software licenses, printed materials) total $2,500, directly supporting participant learning. A 10% indirect cost allocation ($3,250) covers office space and administrative oversight.*  

### 6.3 Sample Budget Table  

| Category | Item | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total |
|----------|------|----------|-----------|-------|
| Personnel | Part‑time instructor (20 hrs/mo × 6 mos) | 2 | $750 | $18,000 |
| Supplies | Laptops ( refurbished ) | 20 | $300 | $6,000 |
| Travel | Workshop venue rental (per day) | 8 | $150 | $1,200 |
| Indirect | 10% overhead | — | — | $2,700 |
| **Total** | | | | **$28,000** |

#### Checklist 6.3 – Budget Checklist  

- [ ] All expenses are justified with a brief narrative  
- [ ] Direct costs are itemized; indirect costs are clearly marked as a percentage or flat amount  
- [ ] Currency and date consistency (USD, fiscal year)  
- [ ] In‑kind contributions valued and noted  
- [ ] Budget aligns with funder’s allowable cost guidelines  
- [ ] Calculations are double‑checked and formatted for readability

## 8. Sustainability & Legacy  

### 8.1 Planning for Long‑Term Impact  

- **Funding diversification**: Identify additional revenue streams (individual donations, fee‑for‑service, in‑kind support).  
- **Program replication**: Document processes so other organizations can adopt the model.  
- **Community ownership**: Engage local stakeholders (schools, businesses) to take over components after grant ends.  

### 8.2 Example Sustainability Plan  

> *After the 2‑year grant concludes, we will transition the tutoring component to a parent‑led “Learning Circle” funded by a combination of community fundraisers and a modest participation fee. The curriculum will be open‑source, allowing any district to implement it at no cost.*  

#### Checklist 8.2 – Sustainability Checklist  

- [ ] Identify post‑grant funding sources (grants, donations, earned income)  
- [ ] Create a “handover” document for staff, volunteers, and community partners  
- [ ] Set milestones for achieving self‑sufficiency (e.g., 30% of operating costs covered by fees)  
- [ ] Establish a monitoring schedule to track progress toward sustainability

## 9. Writing the Narrative  

### 9.1 Storytelling Techniques  

- **Start with a hook**: A vivid anecdote that illustrates the problem.  
- **Use the “problem‑solution‑impact” framework**: State the issue, describe your solution, then show measurable results.  
- **Keep it human**: Include quotes from beneficiaries, photos (if allowed), and personal stories.  

### 9.2 Clear, Concise Language  

- **Avoid jargon**: If you must use technical terms, define them.  
- **Use active voice**: “We will train teachers” vs. “Teachers will be trained by us.”  
- **Limit sentence length**: Aim for 15‑20 words per sentence.  

#### Example Narrative Paragraph  

> *When 12‑year‑old Maya arrived at our after‑school program last fall, she struggled to complete her math homework because she lacked a reliable computer. With your support, we provided her a laptop and a weekly tutoring session. Six months later, Maya’s grades rose from a C to an A, and she now leads a peer coding club. This transformation illustrates how access to technology and mentorship can break the cycle of educational inequity.*  

#### Checklist 9.1 – Narrative Writing Checklist  

- [ ] Hook that grabs attention within the first two sentences  
- [ ] Clear problem statement with data  
- [ ] Detailed description of your solution (activities, staff, resources)  
- [ ] Specific, measurable outcomes linked to objectives  
- [ ] Beneficiary quotes or stories included (with permission)  
- [ ] Language is plain, active, and free of unnecessary jargon  
- [ ] Consistent tone and formatting throughout the proposal

## 10. Review & Editing  

### 10.1 Internal Review Process  

1. **Initial draft** – Write the first version without editing.  
2. **Peer review** – Have a colleague or mentor read for content, logic, and compliance.  
3. **Compliance check** – Verify that all required sections (budget narrative, letters of support) are present.  
4. **Formatting** – Ensure fonts, headings, and page numbers follow funder guidelines.  

### 10.2 External Validation  

- **Funder feedback**: If the funder provides a pre‑review or “questionnaire” stage, respond promptly.  
- **Letters of support**: Collect from partners, schools, or community leaders, confirming they have read the proposal.  

#### Example Review Timeline  

| Day | Activity |
|-----|----------|
| 1‑2 | Draft complete |
| 3‑5 | Peer review & comments |
| 6‑7 | Incorporate revisions |
| 8 | Final compliance & formatting check |
| 9 | Submit (or send for external review) |

#### Checklist 10.2 – Review Checklist  

- [ ] Draft reviewed for content completeness (mission, needs, logic model, budget, evaluation)  
- [ ] Grammar, spelling, and punctuation checked (consider using Grammarly or similar)  
- [ ] All required attachments included (letters of support, IRS status, organizational chart)  
- [ ] Formatting matches funder’s template (font, margins, page length)  
- [ ] Contact information for the principal investigator verified  
- [ ] Submission portal accessed and deadline confirmed

## 11. Submission Process  

### 11.1 Online Portals  

- **Create an account** early (many portals lock after deadline).  
- **Upload each required document** as a separate file (PDF, Word) with exact naming conventions.  
- **Complete all fields** in the portal’s “project summary” section; this may be used for internal scoring.  

### 11.2 Hard Copy Submissions  

- **Print two copies** (original + duplicate).  
- **Use professional binders** with tabbed sections.  
- **Include a cover letter** that references the funder’s RFP number and highlights your alignment.  

#### Example Cover Letter (excerpt)  

> *Dear Members of the Jane Doe Foundation Grants Committee, I am writing to propose a partnership that will address the digital divide in our community. Our “Tech for Teens” initiative aligns with your strategic goal of expanding STEM literacy among underserved youth. Enclosed you will find our full proposal, budget, and letters of support.*  

#### Checklist 11.1 – Submission Checklist  

- [ ] Application deadline noted on calendar and tracked with reminders  
- [ ] All required documents uploaded or printed  
- [ ] File names follow funder’s naming conventions (e.g., “Organization_Project_Title.pdf”)  
- [ ] Cover letter attached (electronic or physical)  
- [ ] Confirmation receipt obtained (email receipt, tracking number)  
- [ ] Backup copy stored securely (cloud and local)

- **Regulatory compliance**: Follow any specific funder requirements (e.g., procurement policies, sub‑grant rules).  
- **Reporting compliance**: Submit all reports on time, with required attachments.

## 13. Reporting & Communication  

### 13.1 Types of Reports  

| Report | When Due | Content Focus |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| **Progress Report** | Quarterly | Activities completed, interim outcomes, challenges |
| **Financial Report** | Quarterly or Annual | Budget vs. actuals, receipts, expenditures by category |
| **Final Report** | At project end | Full narrative, outcomes, lessons learned, sustainability plan |
| **Evaluation Report** | As per evaluation plan | Data analysis, findings, recommendations |

### 13.2 Writing Effective Reports  

- **Start with executive summary**: 1‑2 paragraphs highlighting key achievements.  
- **Use consistent formatting**: Mirror the proposal’s headings for easy comparison.  
- **Include visuals**: Charts, graphs, or photos that illustrate impact.  
- **Be transparent**: Note any challenges, risks, or deviations from the original plan.  

#### Example Progress Report Excerpt  

> **Quarterly Progress Report – January–March 2024**  
>   
> **Program Activities**: Conducted 3 of 8 planned workshops, reaching 45 students. Developed curriculum modules for two new subjects.  
>   
> **Outcomes**: Pre‑post assessments show an average 12% gain in problem‑solving scores. Participant satisfaction rating: 92%.  
>   
> **Challenges**: Limited internet bandwidth at one partner school slowed digital activities; we secured a temporary broadband loan from a local ISP.  
>   
> **Next Steps**: Complete remaining workshops, finalize evaluation data collection, and begin sustainability planning with community partners.  

### 13.3 Sample Report Template (Plain Text)  

```  
[Organization Name] – Grant #[Grant ID] – [Funder Name]  

Quarterly Progress Report – [Month Year]  

1. Executive Summary  
   - Total budget: $[X]  
   - Expenditures to date: $[Y] (ZZ%)  
   - Key achievements: [list]  

2. Activities Completed  
   - Workshop #1: Date, location, participants  
   - Curriculum development: Modules completed  
   - Community outreach: Partnerships secured  

3. Interim Outcomes  
   - Indicator A: Baseline [X] → Current [Y] (Δ%)  
   - Indicator B: Survey results – [percentage] satisfied  

4. Financial Summary  
   - Personnel: $[a]  
   - Supplies: $[b]  
   - Indirect: $[c]  
   - Total spent: $[d]  

5. Challenges & Mitigations  
   - [Describe]  
   - Action taken: [Explain]  

6. Next Quarter’s Priorities  
   - [List]  

7. Attachments  
   - Budget reconciliation spreadsheet  
   - Photos of workshop  
   - Letters of support (updated)  
```  

#### Checklist 13.2 – Reporting Checklist  

- [ ] Review funder’s reporting guidelines for format, length, and required attachments  
- [ ] Ensure all data is up‑to‑date and accurate  
- [ ] Include visual aids (charts, photos) to enhance readability  
- [ ] Note any deviations from the original plan and explain reasons  
- [ ] Attach supporting documentation (receipts, letters, evaluation data)  
- [ ] Proofread for grammar, consistency, and brand voice  
- [ ] Submit well before the deadline to allow time for feedback

## 14. Continuous Improvement  

### 14.1 Lessons Learned  

- **Collect feedback** from beneficiaries, staff, and partners after each reporting period.  
- **Document successes** (what worked, why it worked) and **challenges** (what didn’t, why).  
- **Update logic models** and **evaluation tools** based on new insights.  

### 14.2 Data‑Driven Decision Making  

- **Use key performance indicators (KPIs)** to monitor progress in real time.  
- **Adjust activities** if KPIs are off‑track (e.g., increase outreach if enrollment lags).  
- **Reallocate resources** to high‑impact areas, staying within funder‑approved parameters.  

#### Example KPI Dashboard (simple)  

| KPI | Target | Current | Status |
|-----|--------|---------|--------|
| Workshop attendance | 120 participants | 85 | Below target |
| Post‑test score gain | +15% | +10% | Below target |
| Budget variance | ≤5% over | 3% under | On target |
| Stakeholder satisfaction | ≥90% | 88% | Slightly low |

### 14.3 Updating the Grant Writing Process  

- **Create a “grant library”**: Archive successful proposals, budgets, and reports.  
- **Develop templates** for common sections (needs statement, logic model, evaluation plan) to speed future writing.  
- **Hold quarterly “grant review” meetings** to refine processes and share best practices.  

#### Checklist 14.3 – Continuous Improvement Checklist  

- [ ] Conduct post‑grant debrief with all team members  
- [ ] Gather quantitative and qualitative feedback  
- [ ] Update logic model and evaluation tools based on findings  
- [ ] Refine templates and checklists for future cycles  
- [ ] Set goals for next funding cycle (e.g., secure 2 additional grants)  
- [ ] Train new staff on standardized grant writing procedures

## 15. Conclusion  

Writing nonprofit grants is a disciplined, iterative process that blends strategic research, clear storytelling, meticulous budgeting, and rigorous evaluation. By following the best‑practice framework outlined above—researching funders, crafting compelling needs statements, building robust logic models, managing budgets responsibly, and delivering transparent post‑award reports—you position your organization for both immediate success and long‑term sustainability.  

Remember: each grant is an opportunity to demonstrate your mission’s impact, build relationships with funders, and refine your capacity to serve the community. Use the checklists, examples, and templates as tools, but always tailor your narrative to the unique priorities of each funder.  

With diligence, attention to detail, and a commitment to learning from every cycle, your grant writing will become more effective, efficient, and impactful—fueling the transformative work your nonprofit strives to achieve.
