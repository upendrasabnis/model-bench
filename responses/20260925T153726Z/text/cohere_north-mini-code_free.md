## 1. Introduction  

Grant compliance is the backbone of trustworthy public and private funding ecosystems. When a grantee receives money—whether from a federal agency, a state department, or a major philanthropy—they enter into a contractual relationship that imposes legal, financial, and programmatic obligations. **Acquittal reporting** is one of the most critical compliance mechanisms: it is the formal process by which a grantee certifies that grant funds have been used in accordance with the award terms, that any unspent balances have been returned or justified, and that all reporting requirements have been satisfied.  

Failure to meet compliance obligations can result in:  

* **Financial penalties** (e.g., recoupment, debarment)  
* **Loss of future funding** (agencies often place grantees on “watch lists”)  
* **Reputational damage** that spreads quickly through peer networks, professional societies, and social media.  

The cost of non‑compliance is not abstract. According to the U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO), federal agencies recovered over **$1.5 billion** in FY 2022 through audit findings, penalties, and grant terminations. Philanthropic foundations, while less punitive, still enforce strict compliance clauses and can terminate multi‑year funding relationships.  

This article provides a **step‑by‑step roadmap** for understanding acquittal reporting, navigating the overlapping requirements of government and philanthropic funders, establishing robust record‑keeping systems, preparing for audits, and avoiding the most common compliance pitfalls. Real‑world case studies illustrate how lapses unfold and how organizations have turned crises into learning opportunities.

## 2. What Is Acquittal Reporting?  

### 2.1 Definition & Legal Basis  

*Acquittal* (sometimes called “final accounting” or “closeout”) is the formal certification that a grantee has fulfilled all obligations associated with a grant award. In the federal context, the term appears in statutes such as **31 U.S.C. § 1301** (availability of appropriations) and agency‑specific regulations (e.g., 2 CFR Part 200 – Uniform Administrative Requirements). Philanthropic funders typically embed acquittal requirements in their grant agreements under headings like “Final Report,” “Closeout,” or “Accountability.”  

### 2.2 Purpose and Stakeholders  

| Stakeholder | Why Acquittals Matter |
|-------------|-----------------------|
| **Funding Agency** | Verifies fiscal responsibility, ensures proper use of taxpayer/donor dollars, and satisfies audit requirements. |
| **Grantee** | Provides legal closure, protects against future liability, and enables future funding applications. |
| **Auditors** | Use acquittal reports as primary evidence when testing expenditures and compliance. |
| **Regulators** | May request acquittal documentation during compliance reviews or investigations. |

### 2.3 Core Elements of an Acquittal Report  

| Element | What It Includes | Typical Format |
|---------|------------------|----------------|
| **Executive Summary** | High‑level overview of project outcomes, budget status, and compliance status. | Narrative + tables |
| **Financial Certification** | Statement that all expenditures are allowable, allocable, and reasonable; that any surplus funds have been returned or justified; and that the final expenditure report matches the accounting records. | Signed attestation by authorized official |
| **Narrative Description** | Description of project achievements, challenges, and lessons learned; alignment with funder’s strategic goals. | 2–5 pages, often with charts/graphs |
| **Supporting Documentation** | Audited financial statements, bank reconciliations, contract copies, sub‑award reports, and any required certifications (e.g., Single Audit, FAR Subpart 31.2). | Appendices |
| **Closeout Checklist** | Confirmation that all funder‑specific requirements have been met (e.g., publications, data management plan, dissemination activities). | Checklist format |
| **Signatures** | Authorized signatory (e.g., CEO, CFO) and, where required, an independent auditor or certified public accountant (CPA). | Physical or electronic signature block |

## 3. Common Requirements Across Government and Philanthropic Funders  

### 3.1 Government Funders  

| Agency | Key Acquittal Requirements | Typical Timeline |
|--------|----------------------------|------------------|
| **National Institutes of Health (NIH)** | *Final Progress Report* (NPR), *Financial Status Report* (FSR), *Audit* (if applicable), *Data Management Plan* compliance, *Publication* acknowledgment. | 90 days after project end date |
| **National Science Foundation (NSF)** | *Final Technical Report*, *Financial Report* (FR), *Sub‑award Closeout* (if any), *Data Management* compliance, *Brochure* (optional). | 60 days after award end |
| **Department of Energy (DOE)** | *Final Technical Report*, *Financial Status Report*, *Audit* (if >$10 M), *Intellectual Property* assignment, *Safety & Environmental* compliance. | 30 days after project end |
| **U.S. Department of Housing & Urban Development (HUD)** | *Final Performance Report*, *Financial Status Report*, *Audit* (if >$750 K), *Grantee‑provided* certifications (e.g., debarment). | 45 days after end of period |
| **U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID)** | *Final Report* (including *Financial Closeout*), *Audit* (if >$10 M), *Sub‑award* compliance, *Donor reporting* (e.g., USAID’s “Performance Monitoring and Evaluation System”). | 60 days after closeout date |
| **Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA)** | *Final Technical Report*, *Financial Closeout*, *Security* compliance (e.g., export controls), *Data Rights* assignment. | 30 days after program end |

**Common Themes**  

* **Financial Certification** – Must be signed by a senior official.  
* **Audit Trail** – All expenditures must be supported by source documents (invoices, contracts, timecards).  
* **Data Management** – Requirements to preserve raw data, share via repositories, and comply with FAIR principles.  
* **Sub‑award Reporting** – If the grantee issues sub‑grants, each sub‑award must also undergo closeout.  

### 3.2 Philanthropic Funders  

| Foundation | Key Acquittal Requirements | Typical Timeline |
|------------|----------------------------|------------------|
| **Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation** | *Final Report* (technical + financial), *Audit* (if >$1 M), *Intellectual Property* policy, *Evaluation* findings, *Dissemination* plan. | 90 days after project end |
| **Ford Foundation** | *Final Narrative* and *Financial* reports, *Compliance* certifications (e.g., conflict of interest), *Data* sharing plan. | 60 days after end date |
| **John D. & Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation** | *Final Report* (including *Outcomes*), *Audit* (if >$500 K), *Sub‑grant* closeout, *Sustainability* plan. | 45 days after closeout |
| **Robert Wood Johnson Foundation** | *Final Narrative* and *Financial* reports, *Evaluation* documentation, *Dissemination* of findings, *Compliance* with *ethical* standards. | 60 days after end date |

**Common Themes**  

* **Impact Measurement** – Philanthropies often require rigorous evaluation and evidence of outcomes.  
* **Open Access** – Many foundations mandate that research outputs be freely accessible (e.g., open‑access journals, pre‑print servers).  
* **Conflict‑of‑Interest (COI) Disclosures** – Must be updated and attached to the final report.  

### 3.3 Overlap, Harmonization, and “Universal” Clauses  

| Universal Clause | Description | Where It Appears |
|------------------|-------------|------------------|
| **Allowable Costs** | Costs must be reasonable, necessary, and allocable per 2 CFR 200 (federal) or the funder’s cost principles. | All funders |
| **Reporting Frequency** | Quarterly or semi‑annual financial reports leading up to the final acquittal. | Federal & many philanthropies |
| **Audit Requirements** | If expenditure threshold is met, an independent audit is required (Single Audit for federal, GAGAS for state). | Federal, large philanthropies |
| **Sub‑award Compliance** | Sub‑grantees must also follow the same compliance standards (e.g., 2 CFR 200 Subpart E). | Federal & many philanthropies |
| **Data Rights / Ownership** | Clarifies who owns data, software, or intellectual property generated with funding. | Federal (FAR) & many foundations |
| **Termination for Convenience** | Funder may terminate the award without cause, requiring return of unspent funds. | All funders |
| **Compliance Certifications** | Grantee certifies that it is not suspended/debarred and that it will comply with all applicable laws. | Federal (SAM.gov) & foundations |

**Tip:** When drafting your closeout package, map each requirement to a checklist item. This makes it easy to see gaps and ensures you meet the most stringent standard across all funders.

## 4. Record‑Keeping Essentials  

### 4.1 Financial Documentation  

| Document | Purpose | Retention Period |
|----------|---------|------------------|
| **Source Bills & Invoices** | Evidence of incurred costs, vendor relationships. | 7 years (federal) |
| **Payment Receipts & Bank Reconciliations** | Verify cash disbursements, detect misappropriation. | 7 years |
| **Budget vs. Actual Reports** | Demonstrate adherence to approved budget. | 7 years |
| **Grant Award Documentation** | Terms, conditions, amendments, and any modifications. | 7 years |
| **Audit Reports (Internal & External)** | Provide audit trail for auditors and funders. | 7 years (or as required by law) |

**Best Practice:** Store all financial documents in a **digital repository** with role‑based access (e.g., Box, OneDrive) and enable version control. Use **GRAP (Government Relationship Automation Platform)** or **ERP** systems to link expenditures to grant codes automatically.  

### 4.2 Project‑Specific Records  

| Record Type | Content | Retention |
|-------------|---------|-----------|
| **Project Plans & Work Breakdown Structure** | Scope, milestones, deliverables. | 7 years |
| **Progress Reports & Technical Narratives** | Updates on activities, outcomes, challenges. | 7 years |
| **Human Subjects Documentation** | IRB approvals, informed consent, adverse event logs. | 7 years (or longer per IRB) |
| **Participant Data & Research Data** | Raw data, analysis scripts, metadata. | As required by funder (often 10 years) |
| **Intellectual Property Logs** | Invention disclosures, patent filings, licensing agreements. | 7 years |
| **Sub‑award Contracts & Reports** | Agreements with sub‑grantees, their closeout documents. | 7 years |

### 4.3 Human Subject Protections  

* **IRB Approvals** – Keep both the initial approval and any amendments.  
* **Informed Consent Forms** – Store signed copies; ensure they are archived with the data.  
* **Adverse Event Reports** – Document any unintended injuries or ethical breaches and the corrective actions taken.  

**Note:** The **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46) and **FDA** regulations require specific retention periods; failing to preserve these documents can trigger compliance violations.  

### 4.4 Intellectual Property & Data Management  

* **IP Assignment Agreements** – Ensure they are executed promptly and filed with the institution’s technology transfer office.  
* **Data Management Plans (DMP)** – Follow funder‑specific DMP templates (e.g., NIH’s DMSM, NSF’s Data Management Plan). Include details on storage, access, sharing, and preservation.  
* **Version Control** – Use tools like Git for code, and institutional repositories for datasets.

## 5. Audit Preparation and Execution  

### 5.1 Internal Controls & Audit Trails  

| Control Area | Typical Controls | Example Implementation |
|--------------|------------------|------------------------|
| **Segregation of Duties** | Separate individuals approve expenses, record them, and reconcile bank statements. | Finance manager approves, accounting clerk records, senior director reconciles. |
| **Policy & Procedure Documentation** | Written SOPs for expense reporting, sub‑award management, and data security. | SOP repository on SharePoint with approval workflow. |
| **Periodic Reconciliations** | Monthly bank, grant, and sub‑grant reconciliations. | Automated reconciliation using ERP tool. |
| **Access Controls** | Role‑based permissions limiting who can create, edit, or delete records. | LDAP integration with least‑privilege principle. |
| **Change Management** | Track all modifications to grant budgets, timelines, or reports. | Version‑controlled documents in Confluence. |

### 5.2 Engaging External Auditors  

1. **Early Communication** – Send the auditor a “pre‑audit briefing” that includes:  
   * Scope of the audit (federal vs. non‑federal components)  
   * Key personnel to interview (grants manager, finance lead)  
   * Access instructions for digital repositories  

2. **Provide Organized Documentation** – Use a **grant‑specific audit folder** that groups documents by award number, with clear labeling (e.g., “FSRs”, “Source Docs”, “IRB”).  

3. **Maintain a “Audit Log”** – Record dates, personnel, and outcomes of any audit findings or inquiries. This helps track remediation progress.  

### 5.3 Post‑Audit Remediation and Continuous Improvement  

| Step | Action | Owner |
|------|--------|-------|
| **Review Findings** | Read audit report, categorize findings (financial, compliance, internal control). | Audit Committee / Compliance Officer |
| **Corrective Action Plan** | Develop a timeline, responsible party, and evidence of remediation. | Grants Administration |
| **Implementation** | Execute actions (e.g., adjust policies, train staff, adjust procedures). | Relevant Department Leads |
| **Verification** | Obtain sign‑off from auditor or internal compliance team. | Compliance Officer |
| **Monitoring** | Conduct follow‑up reviews to ensure findings do not recur. | Internal Audit Function |

**Key Metric:** Track **“Days to Closeout”** for each grant after audit. Aim for < 30 days for minor findings, < 90 days for major findings.

## 6. Most Frequent Compliance Failures and How to Avoid Them  

| Failure | Why It Happens | How to Prevent |
|---------|----------------|----------------|
| **Misreporting Expenditures** (e.g., charging personal travel as direct costs) | Lack of clear cost‑allocation policies; insufficient training. | Implement **cost‑allocation matrices**, conduct **quarterly cost‑review meetings**, and require **receipts with expense codes**. |
| **Inadequate Documentation** (missing signatures, incomplete source documents) | Overreliance on memory; poor version control. | Use **standardized templates** in an **electronic document management system (EDMS)**; enforce **mandatory fields** and **workflow approvals**. |
| **Late or Missing Acquittal Reports** | Unrealistic project timelines; lack of internal tracking. | Set **automated reminders** in your grant management software; assign a **single point of contact** for closeout; create a **grant lifecycle calendar**. |
| **Non‑Compliance with Sub‑award Requirements** (e.g., failing to report sub‑award financials) | Sub‑grantees operate independently; lack of oversight. | Adopt a **centralized sub‑award management system**; require sub‑grantees to submit **annual certifications**; perform **annual sub‑award audits**. |
| **Ignoring Reporting Deadlines** (e.g., failing to submit quarterly financial reports) | Competing priorities; siloed communication. | Integrate **reporting workflows** with **financial close processes**; use **KPIs** (e.g., “% of reports on time”) to monitor performance. |
| **Weak Risk Assessment & Monitoring** (e.g., not updating risk registers after project changes) | Static risk assessments; no continuous monitoring. | Conduct **quarterly risk reviews**; use **risk dashboards** in your ERP; incorporate **key risk indicators (KRIs)** such as “unreconciled grant balances > 30 days”. |

### 6.1 Real‑World Failure Example: NIH Grant Acquittal  

*Case*: A university’s Department of Biology received a **$12 M NIH R01** to study a novel antiviral compound. Two years into the project, the department failed to update its **budget–actual reconciliation** after a sub‑award was restructured. When the final acquittal report was submitted, the auditors discovered **$450 K** in **unallowable indirect costs** that had been incorrectly allocated.  

*Consequences*: NIH imposed a **cost recovery** of $450 K, placed the department on **administrative probation**, and required a **corrective action plan** before any future funding.  

*Prevention*: Implement a **real‑time grant budgeting tool** (e.g., **Grant Management System – GMS**) that flags over‑expenditures and automatically alerts the grants manager. Conduct **monthly budget‑vs‑actual reviews** with the finance team.

## 7. Real‑World Examples  

### 7.1 Example 1: NIH Grant Acquittal Failure – Lessons from a $12 M Research Award  

| Phase | What Happened | Root Cause | Corrective Actions Implemented |
|-------|---------------|------------|--------------------------------|
| **Project Initiation** | Sub‑award contract was amended mid‑project to increase scope without formal budget revision. | Lack of **budget amendment tracking** in the grant management system. | Implemented a **change‑control workflow** requiring finance, PI, and compliance sign‑off. |
| **Financial Monitoring** | Quarterly financial reports showed consistent under‑budget for indirect costs, masking the true allocation. | **Inadequate variance analysis** and no **automated alerts** for cost drift. | Deployed **budget variance alerts** in the ERP; scheduled **bi‑weekly cost reviews**. |
| **Closeout Preparation** | Auditors found $450 K in unallowable indirect costs; the acquittal report was rejected. | **Missing documentation** for sub‑award amendments; **no final reconciliation**. | Created a **grant closeout checklist** (see Section 2) and assigned a **dedicated closeout coordinator**. |
| **Post‑Closeout** | NIH required a **cost recovery** and placed the department on probation for 2 years. | **Delayed detection** of misallocation. | Established a **continuous compliance monitoring program** with quarterly internal audits. |

**Takeaway:** Even large, well‑respected institutions can slip on seemingly “minor” administrative oversights. A systematic, automated approach to budget tracking and amendment management is essential.  

### 7.2 Example 2: Gates Foundation Compliance Issue – A $45 M Global Health Grant  

| Issue | Description | Impact | Remediation |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------------|
| **Data Sharing Non‑Compliance** | The grantee failed to deposit raw dataset in the required public repository (Gates Foundation’s open‑data policy). | Foundation threatened to **withhold the final tranche** of funding ($9 M) and required a **corrective action plan**. | Implemented a **Data Management Plan (DMP) enforcement** process; integrated repository upload checks into the project workflow. |
| **Conflict‑of‑Interest (COI) Oversight** | A senior researcher did not disclose a consultancy with a pharmaceutical partner while authoring project publications. | Foundation’s audit flagged a **material COI violation**, leading to a **re‑review of all publications** and a **$200 K penalty**. | instituted a **mandatory COI disclosure form** for all personnel; automated reminders tied to grant milestones. |
| **Sub‑grant Misalignment** | A sub‑grant to a local NGO was not reported in the grantee’s quarterly financial report. | Foundation’s auditors identified a **$150 K** gap in sub‑grant reporting, resulting in a **delayed acquittal**. | Adopted a **centralized sub‑grant tracking module**; required sub‑grantees to submit **quarterly financial snapshots** directly to the foundation’s portal. |

**Takeaway:** Philanthropic funders often emphasize **impact, openness, and ethics** as much as fiscal compliance. Building these values into your grant management culture reduces risk and aligns with donor expectations.  

### 7.3 Example 3: State Department Sub‑award Audit – Findings and Corrective Actions  

*Background*: A university consortium managed a **$30 M** State Department democracy‑promotion program, involving **12 sub‑awards** to NGOs in Eastern Europe.  

**Audit Findings**:  

1. **Missing Sub‑award Agreements** – 3 sub‑awards lacked signed agreements, leading to **uncontrolled expenditures** of $2.1 M.  
2. **Improper Cost Allocation** – Two sub‑grantees charged **personal travel** as direct costs, violating the **Cost Principles** (FAR Subpart 31.2).  
3. **Insufficient Monitoring** – No quarterly performance reviews were conducted; sub‑grantees reported only financial data.  

**Corrective Actions**:  

| Action | Description | Owner | Timeline |
|--------|-------------|-------|----------|
| **Agreement Repository** | Created a **centralized sub‑award contract repository** with mandatory e‑signatures and audit trails. | Grants Administration | 30 days |
| **Cost Allocation Training** | Conducted a **mandatory workshop** for all sub‑grant managers on allowable costs and documentation. | Compliance Office | 45 days |
| **Performance Monitoring System** | Implemented a **digital dashboard** (e.g., Power BI) that pulls sub‑grant performance metrics and triggers alerts for missed reviews. | Program Management | 60 days |
| **Independent Sub‑award Audits** | Engaged a third‑party auditor to perform **annual sub‑award reviews** for the next 3 years. | External Auditor | Ongoing |

**Outcome**: The State Department closed the audit with **no material weaknesses**, and the consortium received a **“Highly Satisfactory”** rating, paving the way for a **$50 M** follow‑on award.

## 8. Best Practices for Ongoing Compliance  

### 8.1 Governance Structure & Clear Accountability  

* **Grant Management Office (GMO)** – Centralized team responsible for all awards, from negotiation to closeout.  
* **Compliance Officer** – Reports to senior leadership; oversees policy development, training, and audit remediation.  
* **Financial Controller** – Signs off on financial certifications and ensures proper allocation of indirect costs.  
* **Project Leads** – Own day‑to‑day compliance (e.g., IRBs, data management, sub‑award reporting).  

**RACI Matrix Example** (for a typical grant):  

| Activity | Responsible | Accountable | Consulted | Informed |
|----------|-------------|-------------|----------|----------|
| Budget amendment | Grants Manager | Finance Director | PI | Department Chair |
| Sub‑award execution | Sub‑grant Manager | Grants Director | Legal Counsel | Sub‑grantee |
| Final acquittal report | Closeout Coordinator | CEO/Finance VP | Compliance Officer | Funders |

### 8.2 Training, Communication, and Documentation Policies  

* **Onboarding** – New staff receive a **grant compliance orientation** covering cost principles, reporting deadlines, and data rights.  
* **Quarterly Refreshers** – Webinars on emerging regulatory changes (e.g., new NIH reporting requirements).  
* **Documented SOPs** – Stored in a **knowledge base** (Confluence, SharePoint) with version control and approval workflows.  

### 8.3 Leveraging Technology (GRAP, ERP, CLM)  

| Tool | Core Function | Integration Benefits |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Grant Relationship Automation Platform (GRAP)** | Tracks award lifecycle, automates reminders, stores closeout checklists. | Direct link to ERP for automatic expense posting. |
| **Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP)** (e.g., SAP, Oracle) | Core financial accounting; cost allocation, sub‑ledger. | Real‑time grant balance visibility; audit trail. |
| **Contract Lifecycle Management (CLM)** | Manages award agreements, amendments, signatures. | Ensures all legal terms are captured and searchable. |
| **Document Management System (DMS)** | Stores source documents, IRBs, DMPs. | Role‑based access; version control; easy audit retrieval. |
| **Business Intelligence (BI) Dashboard** | Visualizes compliance KPIs (e.g., % on‑time reports, audit findings). | Enables proactive risk management. |

**Implementation Tip:** Start with a **pilot** for one funder type (e.g., federal) to fine‑tune processes before scaling to philanthropic awards.  

### 8.4 Continuous Monitoring & Risk‑Based Auditing  

* **Key Risk Indicators (KRIs)** – Example: “Unreconciled grant balance > $10 K” or “IRB expiration within 90 days.”  
* **Monthly Compliance Reviews** – Finance, grants, and compliance leaders meet to discuss any deviations.  
* **Annual External Audit** – Conduct a **risk‑based audit** focusing on high‑value awards and those with prior findings.  

**Sample Monitoring Schedule**:  

| Frequency | Activity | Owner |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **Daily** | System alerts for unapproved expenditures > $5 K | Finance Analyst |
| **Weekly** | Review of grant budget vs. actual (variance > 10 %) | Grants Manager |
| **Monthly** | Reconciliation of all open grants; update risk register | Compliance Officer |
| **Quarterly** | Sub‑award performance review; audit of sub‑grant documentation | Sub‑grant Manager |
| **Annually** | Full external audit; preparation of final acquittal for multi‑year awards | External Auditor |

## 9. Conclusion  

Grant compliance and acquittal reporting are not merely bureaucratic hoops; they are **guardrails that protect both funders and grantees** from financial loss, legal liability, and reputational harm. By understanding the **common requirements** across government and philanthropic funders, maintaining **rigorous record‑keeping**, preparing **thoughtfully for audits**, and **proactively avoiding frequent compliance failures**, organizations can navigate the complex funding landscape with confidence.  

Key takeaways:  

1. **Standardize** your closeout process using a checklist that maps each funder’s requirements.  
2. **Automate** reminders, budget variance alerts, and document workflows to reduce human error.  
3. **Train** staff continuously and embed a culture of compliance from the top down.  
4. **Monitor** risk indicators and conduct regular internal reviews to catch issues early.  
5. **Learn** from real‑world failures—document the root causes and embed corrective actions into your governance.  

When these practices become ingrained in daily operations, acquittal reporting transforms from a dreaded deadline into a **strategic milestone** that signals successful project delivery and responsible stewardship of resources.

## 10. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)  

**Q: What is the difference between a “grant closeout” and an “acquittal”?**  
A: *Closeout* is the administrative process of wrapping up a grant (returning funds, archiving documents). *Acquittal* is the formal certification that the grantee has satisfied all obligations, often submitted as part of the closeout package.  

**Q: Can a grant be closed out without an acquittal?**  
A: Generally, no. Most funders require an acquittal (or final report) before releasing the final tranche of funds or before the grant can be considered fully closed.  

**Q: How long do I need to retain acquittal documentation?**  
A: Federal regulations typically require **7 years** from the date of submission. Philanthropic foundations often align with the same period, though some may have shorter retention (e.g., 3 years). Always check the specific award terms.  

**Q: What happens if I miss the acquittal deadline?**  
A: Funders may impose **financial penalties**, **withhold future disbursements**, or **terminate the award**. Promptly contacting the funder to explain and propose a revised timeline can sometimes mitigate consequences.  

**Q: Do I need an external audit for every grant?**  
A: Only if the grant meets the **single audit threshold** (currently $750 K in federal expenditures) or if the philanthropic funder’s policy mandates an audit for awards above a certain amount. Smaller grants may rely on internal controls and periodic reviews.  

**Q: How can I ensure sub‑grantees comply with reporting?**  
A: Use a **centralized sub‑award management system** that requires sub‑grantees to submit reports directly, integrates with your grant management platform, and provides automated reminders. Conduct periodic audits of sub‑grantee documentation.
