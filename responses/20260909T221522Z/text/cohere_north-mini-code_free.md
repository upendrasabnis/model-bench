# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*A comprehensive guide from funder research to post‑award reporting*  

---  

## 1. Introduction  

Grant writing is both an art and a science. For nonprofits, a well‑crafted grant proposal can mean the difference between thriving programs and stalled services. This article walks you through every stage of the grant‑writing cycle, offering concrete examples, practical checklists, and tips that you can apply to any funding opportunity. Whether you are a seasoned grant writer or a volunteer administrator stepping into the role for the first first time, the principles below will help you produce proposals that are clear, compelling, and compliant.  

---  

## 2. Understanding the Grant Landscape  

### 2.1 Researching Funders  

**Why it matters** – Knowing who funds what, how they evaluate proposals, and what their strategic priorities are will save you time and increase your chances of success.  

**Steps to effective funder research**  

| Step | Action | Tools / Resources |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| 1 | Identify potential funders (foundation databases, government grants, corporate CSR) | *Foundation Center Candid* (FC Candid), *GrantStation*, *National Directory of Nonprofit Organizations* |
| 2 | Review past grants they have funded (search their website, annual reports, press releases) | *Foundation Center's Glassdoor*, *GrantStation’s Grant History* |
| 3 | Note eligibility criteria (mission alignment, geographic focus, population served) | Funders’ RFPs, eligibility FAQs |
| 4 | Understand required formats (PDF, Word, specific narrative sections) | RFP instructions, sample proposals |
| 5 | Capture deadlines, contact person, and any pre‑application requirements | Spreadsheet or grant management software (e.g., *Submittable*, *Fluxx*) |
| 6 | Record key evaluation criteria (e.g., measurable outcomes, budget clarity) | Funders’ evaluation rubrics, past reviewer comments |

**Example** – *The Robert Wood Johnson Foundation* focuses on health equity and requires a “Logic Model” in the proposal. By noting this early, you can allocate time to develop one rather than scramble later.  

### 2.2 Types of Grants  

| Category | Typical Features | Typical Funders |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Program‑Specific** | Funding for a defined initiative; often multi‑year | Foundations, government agencies |
| **Capacity‑Building** | Staff training, infrastructure, technology | Large foundations, corporate grants |
| **Research/Evaluation** | Studies, data collection, impact assessments | Think tanks, government research offices |
| **Emergency/Seed** | Small amounts for start‑up or crisis response | Community foundations, donor‑advised funds |

### 2.3 Practical Checklist – Funder Research  

- [ ] Create a master spreadsheet of all potential funders (name, website, focus, deadlines, contact).  
- [ ] Bookmark each funder’s “Grant Opportunities” page.  
- [ ] Download at least three past successful proposals (if available) for pattern analysis.  
- [ ] Summarize each funder’s top 3–5 priority themes.  
- [ ] Set up calendar alerts for deadlines and pre‑application due dates.

## 3. Developing the Foundation of Your Proposal  

### 3.1 Needs Assessment & Statement of Need  

A compelling needs statement answers three questions: **What** problem exists, **Why** it matters now, and **How** it affects the community.  

**Components of a strong needs statement**  

1. **Data‑driven context** – Use statistics from reputable sources (census, health departments, local surveys).  
2. **Human element** – Include a brief anecdote or quote from a client to personalize the data.  
3. **Gap analysis** – Show what currently exists versus what is needed.  
4. **Urgency** – Explain why the problem is worsening or why immediate action is required.  

**Concrete Example**  

> *“In County X, 1 in 5 children under 12 lives below the federal poverty line (U.S. Census Bureau, 2022). Our shelter currently serves 30 families monthly, but the waiting list has grown from 12 to 48 families over the past 18 months. Without additional funding, families will continue to experience housing instability, which correlates with increased rates of chronic illness and school absenteeism (Local Health Department, 2023).”*  

**Checklist – Crafting the Needs Statement**  

- [ ] Verify data sources and cite them.  
- [ ] Include at least one quantitative metric and one qualitative quote.  
- [ ] Clearly state the gap between current services and community need.  
- [ ] Explain the timeline (e.g., “waiting list increased 300% in 18 months”).  
- [ ] Align the need with the funder’s priority areas.  

### 3.2 Mission Alignment  

Funders want to see how your program reflects their strategic goals.  

- **Mission Match**: Does your program’s mission directly address the funder’s focus?  
- **Values Alignment**: Do your organization’s core values resonate with the funder’s culture (e.g., equity, innovation)?  

**Tip**: In your proposal, dedicate a short paragraph (often in the Executive Summary) that explicitly states the alignment.  

### 3.3 Organizational Capacity  

Demonstrate that you have the people, processes, and track record to deliver.  

| Capacity Area | Evidence to Provide |
|---------------|---------------------|
| **Leadership** | Résumés of key staff, board certifications |
| **Program Experience** | Years of service, number of beneficiaries served |
| **Financial Health** | Audited financial statements, stable revenue mix |
| **Systems** | Grant management software, monitoring tools |

**Example**: *“Our organization has implemented a comprehensive CRM system that tracks client outcomes for the past 5 years, enabling us to report a 95% satisfaction rate.”*

## 4. Crafting the Narrative  

### 4.1 Program Description  

Answer the “what” of your proposal. Use the **SMART** framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound) to describe each component.  

### 4.2 Goals, Objectives, and Outcomes  

- **Goals** – Broad, long‑term aspirations (e.g., “Improve high school graduation rates in our community”).  
- **Objectives** – Specific, measurable targets (e.g., “Increase graduation rate from 78% to 85% within 3 years”).  
- **Outcomes** – Short‑term and long‑term changes resulting from the program (e.g., “Students report increased confidence in math skills”).  

### 4.3 Implementation Strategy  

Outline *who* will do *what* and *when*. Use a timeline or Gantt chart for visual clarity.  

**Sample Timeline (12‑Month Program)**  

| Month | Activity |
|-------|----------|
| 1–2 | Needs assessment surveys & stakeholder interviews |
| 3–4 | Curriculum development with subject‑matter experts |
| 5 | Pilot testing with 10 students |
| 6–7 | Full‑scale rollout to 100 students |
| 8–10 | Mid‑point evaluation & adjustments |
| 11 | Data analysis & report preparation |
| 12 | Final impact report & dissemination |

### 4.4 Evaluation Plan  

A robust evaluation plan shows accountability and learning.  

| Evaluation Component | Description | Data Source |
|----------------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Process** | Did we deliver the program as planned? | Attendance logs, implementation checklists |
| **Outcome** | Did participants achieve the objectives? | Pre‑/post‑surveys, test scores |
| **Impact** | What broader changes occurred? | Community-level statistics, secondary data |
| **Cost‑Effectiveness** | What was the cost per outcome? | Budget data, outcome metrics |

**Example Evaluation Question**: *“What is the percentage increase in participants’ math proficiency scores after completing the program?”*

## 5. Building the Logical Framework  

A logic model is a visual roadmap linking inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact. It helps both you and the funder see the theory of change.  

### 5.1 Core Elements  

| Element | Definition | Example (after‑school tutoring program) |
|---------|------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Inputs** | Resources needed (staff, materials, funding) | 2 certified teachers, $15,000 budget, classroom space |
| **Activities** | Actions taken to achieve outputs | Weekly tutoring sessions, parent workshops |
| **Outputs** | Immediate products of activities | 200 tutoring hours delivered, 30 parent workshops conducted |
| **Outcomes** | Short‑term changes (knowledge, attitudes) | 80% of students report improved math confidence |
| **Impact** | Long‑term effects (graduation rates, earnings) | 10% increase in high school graduation rate within 5 years |

### 5.2 Example Logic Model (Visual)  

```
[Inputs] → [Activities] → [Outputs] → [Outcomes] → [Impact]
   |           |               |            |          |
   |           |               |            |          |
   V           V               V            V          V
Staff $15k   Tutoring      200 hrs       Math       Grad
Space      Sessions      delivered    confidence  +10%
```

### 5.3 Checklist – Logic Model Development  

- [ ] Identify all inputs (people, money, facilities).  
- [ ] List concrete activities that directly use those inputs.  
- [ ] Define measurable outputs (numbers, products).  
- [ ] Choose specific outcome indicators (pre/post surveys, test scores).  
- [ ] Articulate the long‑term impact you hope to achieve.  
- [ ] Review the model with stakeholders (program staff, beneficiaries).

## 6. Budget Development and Justification  

### 6.1 Direct vs. Indirect Costs  

| Cost Type | Description | Typical Examples |
|-----------|-------------|------------------|
| **Direct Costs** | Expenses directly tied to the grant activity | Salaries of program staff, supplies, travel |
| **Indirect Costs** | Organizational overhead allocated to the grant | Rent, utilities, administrative salaries, insurance |

### 6.2 Budget Narrative  

Each line item in the budget must be explained in the narrative. Use the format:  

> **Line Item**: Salary for Program Coordinator – 0.5 FTE  
> **Justification**: The coordinator will oversee daily program operations, mentor volunteers, and ensure compliance with grant requirements. A 0.5 FTE allocation provides 20 hours per week, covering the 12‑month program period.  

### 6.3 Budget Templates  

- **Excel Template**: Separate tabs for Personnel, Travel, Supplies, Equipment, Indirect Costs.  
- **Narrative Template**: One‑page description for each major category.  

### 6.4 Practical Budget Checklist  

- [ ] Use a consistent currency and rounding (e.g., $0.00).  
- [ ] Include a **budget justification** for each line item (max 2 sentences).  
- [ ] Align budget categories with the funder’s required format.  
- [ ] Double‑check that indirect cost rate is approved by the organization’s IRS status.  
- [ ] Ensure the total budget matches the narrative description.  
- [ ] Include a **contingency** line (typically 5–10% of total) if allowed.

## 7. Partnering and Collaborations  

### 7.1 Why Partnerships Matter  

- **Credibility**: Partner organizations can vouch for your capacity.  
- **Resources**: Shared services reduce costs and expand reach.  
- **Sustainability**: Long‑term partnerships can secure ongoing support.  

### 7.2 Types of Partnerships  

| Partnership Type | Example | Contribution |
|------------------|---------|--------------|
| **Service‑Delivery** | Local clinic + health education nonprofit | Clinical expertise + outreach |
| **Financial** | United Way + multiple nonprofits | Co‑funding for large projects |
| **Technical** | University research center + program evaluator | Data analysis, evaluation rigor |
| **Infrastructure** | Community center + after‑school program | Physical space, utilities |

### 7.3 Checklist – Partner Agreement  

- [ ] Draft a **Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)** outlining roles, responsibilities, and timeline.  
- [ ] Include **intellectual property** and data sharing clauses.  
- [ ] Specify **budget contributions** (in‑kind or cash).  
- [ ] Set **reporting obligations** (e.g., joint progress reports).  
- [ ] Obtain **sign‑off** from each partner’s leadership.

## 8. Writing the Proposal: Style and Structure  

### 8.1 Standard Proposal Sections  

| Section | Purpose | Key Points |
|---------|---------|------------|
| **Executive Summary** | Quick snapshot (200–300 words) | Highlight problem, solution, budget, expected outcomes |
| **Letter of Inquiry/Concept Note** | Often precedes full proposal | Briefly introduce organization and project idea |
| **Introduction** | Set context, mission | Reference funder’s priorities |
| **Program Description** | Detail the proposed activities | Use SMART objectives |
| **Needs Assessment** | Data‑driven justification | Include quotes, statistics |
| **Evaluation Plan** | Show how success will be measured | Include metrics, timeline |
| **Budget & Narrative** | Financial transparency | Provide line‑item justification |
| **Organizational Capacity** | Demonstrate ability to deliver | Include staff resumes, past achievements |
| **Sustainability** | Long‑term plan beyond grant | Diversified funding, community ownership |
| **Conclusion & Signature** | Closing statement, organizational commitment | Include contact info, board approval |

### 8.2 Writing Tips  

- **Clarity**: Write at a 7th‑grade reading level; avoid jargon.  
- **Consistency**: Use the same terminology for similar concepts throughout.  
- **Verb Tense**: Use present tense for facts, future tense for planned actions.  
- **Active Voice**: “We will train 30 volunteers” vs. “Training will be conducted by volunteers.”  
- **Quantify**: Replace vague statements with numbers (“served 500 families” vs. “served many families”).  

### 8.3 Example Opening Paragraph  

> *“The XYZ Community Center has served over 2,000 low‑income families in the past decade, yet a recent needs assessment reveals a 40% increase in demand for after‑school tutoring among elementary students. This proposal seeks $75,000 from the ABC Foundation to expand our existing tutoring program, targeting 150 students across three schools within the next 12 months.”*

## 9. Review and Edit Process  

### 9.1 Peer Review Checklist  

| Reviewer | Focus | Questions to Ask |
|----------|-------|------------------|
| **Program Staff** | Program logic, feasibility | “Does this plan make sense for the target population?” |
| **Finance** | Budget accuracy, compliance | “Are all costs reasonable and justified?” |
| **Legal/Compliance** | Grant requirements, reporting | “Do we meet all eligibility and reporting criteria?” |
| **External Expert** | Best practices, evaluation rigor | “Is the evaluation design robust?” |
| **Native Speaker/Editor** | Grammar, style, readability | “Is the proposal clear and free of errors?” |

### 9.2 Editing Checklist  

- [ ] Verify that all required sections are present.  
- [ ] Ensure narrative aligns with budget numbers.  
- [ ] Check for spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors.  
- [ ] Confirm that all citations and references are accurate.  
- [ ] Validate that the logic model is visually clear and referenced.  
- [ ] Review that the proposal meets the funder’s formatting (font, page limits, file type).  
- [ ] Obtain final sign‑off from executive leadership.

## 10. Submission and Follow‑up  

### 10.1 Submission Steps  

1. **Prepare electronic copies** (PDF, Word) exactly as required.  
2. **Upload to the funder’s portal** (track upload confirmations).  
3. **Send a confirmation email** to the funder’s grants office, referencing the application ID.  
4. **Log the submission** in your grant management system (include date, contact, status).  

### 10.2 Post‑Submission Communication  

- **Acknowledgment**: Expect an email within 1–3 business days.  
- **Inquiry**: If no acknowledgment after a week, follow up politely.  
- **Status updates**: Many funders provide a portal where you can track decision status.

## 11. Post‑Award Management  

### 11.1 Reporting Requirements  

| Report Type | Frequency | Key Elements |
|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Progress Report** | Quarterly or semi‑annual | Activities completed, challenges, budget burn‑rate |
| **Financial Report** | Monthly or as per grant terms | Expenditures vs. budget, receipts, bank reconciliations |
| **Evaluation Report** | Mid‑term and final | Outcome data, lessons learned, recommendations |
| **Compliance Report** | As required (e.g., EEOC, sub‑grant reporting) | Documentation of adherence to grant conditions |

### 11.2 Financial Management  

- **Separate Grant Account**: Keep grant funds distinct from operating accounts.  
- **Expense Documentation**: Retain receipts, invoices, and approval forms.  
- **Budget Monitoring**: Use a spreadsheet or software (e.g., *QuickBooks*, *Fluxx*) to track actual vs. planned spending.  
- **Allowable Costs**: Review funder guidelines for allowable indirect costs, travel, and equipment.  

### 11.3 Monitoring and Evaluation  

- **Monthly Check‑ins**: Hold brief meetings with program staff to review milestones.  
- **Data Quality**: Ensure data collection tools are reliable and stored securely.  
- **Adverse Events**: Have a protocol for reporting unexpected issues to the funder.  

### 11.4 Sustaining Success  

- **Diversify Funding**: Use the grant as a seed to secure additional sources.  
- **Community Ownership**: Train local volunteers to take over program components.  
- **Impact Documentation**: Create case studies, videos, and press releases to showcase results.

## 12. Conclusion  

Grant writing is a cyclical process: research → plan → write → submit → manage → report → learn. By following the best practices outlined above—rigorous funder research, data‑driven needs statements, clear logic models, realistic budgets, and robust post‑award management—you position your nonprofit to not only win grants but also deliver measurable impact that strengthens your mission and the communities you serve.
