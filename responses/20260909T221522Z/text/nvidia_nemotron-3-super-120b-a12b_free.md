## The Nonprofit Grant Writer’s Compass: A 1,000-Line Deep Dive into Best Practices from Funder Research to Post-Award Stewardship

### **I. FOUNDATION: MINDSET & PREREQUISITES**  
*Before typing a single word, align your approach with funder psychology and organizational readiness.*  

**1.1 The Grant Writer’s Ethical North Star**  
- **Truth over persuasion**: Funders detect exaggeration. Your credibility is your most valuable asset. *Example*: Claiming "90% of homeless youth in our city will gain housing" when your pilot served 12 people invites scrutiny. Instead: "Our pilot housed 12 youth (85% retention at 6 months); scaling aims to serve 120 annually by Year 3, addressing 15% of the city’s identified gap."  
- **Funder-centric framing**: Your proposal isn’t about *your* needs—it’s about how *their* priorities advance *shared* goals. *Check*: Does every section answer, "Why should *this* funder care *now*?"  
- **Internal alignment**: Secure buy-in from leadership, finance, and program staff *before* writing. A proposal contradicting your audit or strategic plan dies fast.  

**1.2 Non-Negotiable Prerequisites**  
| **Element**          | **Why It Matters**                                                                 | **Red Flag if Missing**                                  |  
|----------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|  
| Current 501(c)(3) status | Funders verify via IRS Pub 78; fiscal sponsors add complexity.                   | Using outdated/EIN-mismatched docs.                      |  
| Audited financials (or reviewed) | Proves fiscal responsibility; required for most foundations >$25k.              | Submitting only bank statements for large requests.      |  
| Board-approved strategic plan | Shows sustainability beyond the grant; funders avoid "one-off" projects.        | Proposal lacks ties to org-wide goals.                   |  
| Clear theory of change | Underpins logic models/evaluation; reveals if activities *actually* drive impact. | Vague promises like "we’ll raise awareness."             |  

**Practical Checklist: Pre-Write Readiness**  
[ ] IRS determination letter (current)  
[ ] Latest audited financial statements (or reviewed if <$500k revenue)  
[ ] Board minutes approving this project *and* budget  
[ ] Updated strategic plan (last 3 years)  
[ ] Logic model draft (see Section III)  
[ ] Funder-specific guidelines printed/annotated  
[ ] Conflict-of-interest disclosures for key staff

### **II. RESEARCHING FUNDERS: TARGETING WITH LASER PRECISION**  
*70% of grant success happens before writing. Spray-and-pray wastes time and burns bridges.*  

**2.1 Beyond Foundation Directory Online: Tiered Research Strategy**  
- **Tier 1: Perfect Fit (Spend 60% of research time here)**  
  - *Criteria*: Past grants to orgs like yours (same issue, geography, budget size), explicit alignment with your needs statement, current RFP open.  
  - *Tools*: Funder’s 990-PF (IRS.gov), Candid’s Foundation Maps, recent press releases, trustee LinkedIn profiles.  
  - *Example*: If seeking youth mental health funds, scan a foundation’s 990 for grants to "school-based counseling" in your state—not just "mental health."  

- **Tier 2: Adjacent Fit (30% time)**  
  - *Criteria*: Funds related issues (e.g., you do food security; they fund poverty alleviation), but no direct history. Requires framing your work as a *solution* to their priority.  
  - *Tactic*: Find their latest annual report—what *keeps them up at night*? Mirror that language.  

- **Tier 3: Long Shot (10% time; only if genuinely inspired)**  
  - *Criteria*: High-interest area but mismatched geography/population. Only pursue if you can genuinely adapt (not stretch) your model.  
  - *Warning*: >20% of proposals to Tier 3 funders fail on eligibility—check first!  

**2.2 Decoding the Funder’s Hidden Agenda**  
- **Read between the lines of guidelines**:  
  - *"Preference for collaborative approaches"* → They’ve seen siloed efforts fail. Highlight MOUs with partners.  
  - *"Emphasis on data-driven decisions"* → They want your logic model *and* third-party validation (e.g., university partnership).  
  - *"Interest in policy change"* → Your direct service must link to advocacy (e.g., "Client testimonials will inform our city council briefings").  
- **Analyze past grantees**:  
  - *Do*: Note patterns (e.g., 80% of their education grants go to orgs with parent engagement components).  
  - *Don’t*: Assume similarity = eligibility. A funder might reject your literacy ask if they *only* fund STEM.  

**2.3 Avoiding Landmines**  
- **Never ignore**: Geographic restrictions, ineligible costs (e.g., "no salaries"), matching requirements, or deadlines (submission ≠ review date!).  
- **The 990 Deep Dive**:  
  - *Schedule B*: Lists major donors—could reveal personal connections.  
  - *Part XV*: Grantmaking procedures—*this* is your bible for process quirks (e.g., "requires site visit before full proposal").  
  - *Grants Paid*: Sort by amount—your ask should fit their historical range (±30%).  
- **Ethical Boundary**: If a funder’s priorities conflict with your mission (e.g., they oppose reproductive health but you provide it), *do not apply*. It wastes everyone’s time and risks reputational harm.  

**Practical Checklist: Funder Vetting**  
[ ] Eligibility confirmed (501(c)(3), geography, issue area)  
[ ] Past 3 years’ grants reviewed for patterns/size  
[ ] Guidelines printed with key requirements highlighted (page limits, fonts, attachments)  
[ ] Contacted program officer (if allowed) with 2-3 specific, informed questions  
[ ] Internal scorecard: Fit (1-5), Competition (1-5), Effort Required (1-5) → Only pursue if avg. ≥4  
[ ] Timeline built: Research → Write → Internal Review → Funder Q&A → Submission → Buffer

### **III. CRAFTING THE COMPELLING NEEDS STATEMENT: THE HEART OF YOUR PROPOSAL**  
*A weak needs statement sinks even perfect budgets. This is where you make funders feel the urgency.*  

**3.1 The Anatomy of a Persuasive Needs Statement**  
It’s not a description of your program—it’s a **gap analysis** between *what is* and *what should be*, grounded in local data and human experience.  

| **Section**          | **Purpose**                                                                 | **Strong Example**                                                                 | **Weak Example**                                  |  
|----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|  
| **The Ideal**        | Paint the vision of what the community *deserves*.                          | "Every child in Oakwood reads at grade level by 3rd grade, unlocking lifelong opportunity." | "We want kids to read better."                   |  
| **The Reality**      | Shock with specific, local data showing the gap. Cite sources!              | "In Oakwood, only 42% of 3rd graders read proficiently (State DOE 2023)—28 points below the state avg. In Oakwood’s East Side, it’s 29%." | "Many kids struggle with reading."               |  
| **The Consequences** | Show *why this gap matters now*—human and systemic costs.                   | "Students not reading by 3rd grade are 4x likelier to drop out (Annie E. Casey). Oakwood’s dropout rate (18%) costs the district $2.1M/year in lost productivity and remediation." | "Poor reading leads to bad outcomes."            |  
| **The Root Causes**  | Demonstrate you understand *why* the gap exists (avoids "band-aid" solutions). | "Root causes include: 1) Lack of preschool access (only 35% enrollment in East Side), 2) Teacher turnover (40% annually in high-need schools), 3) Home libraries avg. 20 books vs. 100+ in affluent areas." | "Kids don’t have books."                         |  
| **Your Role**        | Position your org as the *necessary, unique* bridge—*not* the only solution. | "While Oakwood USD runs summer school, it reaches <15% of struggling readers. Our after-school literacy corps, trained in Orton-Gillingham, fills this gap by providing 2x/week small-group tutoring *during the school year*—when skills decay fastest." | "We run a tutoring program."                     |  

**3.2 Data Sourcing: Beyond Google**  
- **Primary is powerful**: Partner with schools/clinics for anonymized service data (e.g., "Our clinic screened 200 teens; 35% showed moderate-severe anxiety—double the county avg.").  
- **Secondary sources that impress**:  
  - *Local*: City/county health departments, United Way ALICE reports, school district dashboards.  
  - *National with local overlay*: CDC WONDER (health), EDFacts (education), HUD user (housing)—then filter to your ZIP/county.  
  - *Academic*: Google Scholar "Oakwood youth literacy barriers" → find theses/dissertations with hyperlocal insights.  
- **The Human Touch**: Always pair stats with a *brief*, anonymized vignette. *Example*: "Maria, a 3rd grader in our pilot, hid her reading struggles by acting out—until tutoring helped her catch up. She now volunteers to read aloud." *Never* use real names without consent.  

**3.3 Pitfalls That Destroy Credibility**  
- **The "We Are Special" Trap**: Claiming uniqueness without proof ("We’re the *only* org doing this!"). *Fix*: "While three orgs offer tutoring, we’re the only one providing *transportation* from East Side schools—a barrier cited by 68% of parents in our survey."  
- **Ignoring Intersectionality**: Treating needs as monolithic. *Fix*: Disaggregate data (e.g., "Reading proficiency: 52% of white 3rd graders vs. 28% of Black students vs. 19% of English learners").  
- **Future-Pacing Without Basis**: "Without our program, 1,000 kids will fail." *Fix*: Use trend data: "If current proficiency rates hold, Oakwood will see 1,200+ non-proficient 3rd graders by 2026—a 15% increase from 2023."  
- **Overloading with Data**: More isn’t better. Pick 3-5 *killer* stats that tell a cohesive story.  

**Practical Checklist: Needs Statement**  
[ ] Opens with a vivid, human-centered vision (not org-focused)  
[ ] Reality section uses ≤5 hyperlocal, recent (<3 yrs) data points with clear sources  
[ ] Consequences explain *tangible* human/systemic costs (not just "sad")  
[ ] Root causes analysis shows depth (avoids symptom-treating)  
[ ] Clearly states why *your* org is positioned to address this specific gap  
[ ] Includes 1 anonymized vignette that illustrates the data  
[ ] Every sentence answers: "Why should the funder care *about this specific gap*?"  
[ ] Jargon-free; readable by an intelligent outsider in <90 seconds  
[ ] Length: 1-1.5 pages max (funder guidelines permitting)

### **IV. PROJECT DESIGN: LOGIC MODELS & THEORY OF CHANGE**  
*Funders don’t fund activities—they fund *change*. Your logic model proves you understand the pathway.*  

**4.1 Theory of Change (ToC) vs. Logic Model: Know the Difference**  
- **Theory of Change**: The *why*—your belief system about how change happens. Often a narrative or visual map showing assumptions (e.g., "If parents gain navigational skills, they will advocate for better school resources, leading to systemic improvement").  
- **Logic Model**: The *how*—a linear, accountable roadmap of Resources → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact. *Required by most funders*.  
- **Critical Link**: Your ToC informs your Logic Model’s assumptions. If your ToC assumes "parental engagement drives school improvement," your Logic Model must include parent workshops as an activity.  

**4.2 Building a Fundable Logic Model: Step-by-Step**  
*Use this template—funders scan for these elements:*  

| **Component**    | **Definition**                                      | **Strong Example (Youth Mental Health)**                                  | **Weak Example**                     |  
|------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|  
| **Inputs**       | What you invest (must match budget)                 | "$75k grant; 2 FTE licensed therapists; partnership with 3 schools; trauma-informed curriculum" | "Staff and money"                    |  
| **Activities**   | What you *do* (verb-driven)                         | "Conduct weekly 50-min CBT group sessions; provide monthly family workshops; train teachers in trauma recognition" | "Offer counseling"                   |  
| **Outputs**      | Direct, quantifiable products of activities         | "120 youth served in groups; 80 family workshop attendees; 45 teachers trained" | "Helped many kids"                   |  
| **Outcomes**     | Changes in knowledge, skills, attitudes, behavior   | "60% of youth show reduced anxiety (SCARED scale); 50% of families report improved communication; 70% of trained teachers identify 1+ trauma signs/month" | "Kids feel better"                   |  
| **Impact**       | Long-term, systemic change (5-10+ years)            | "Reduced juvenile justice referrals in target schools by 25%; increased graduation rates among participants by 15pp" | "Better community"                   |  

**4.3 Making Outcomes SMART & Fundable**  
- **Specific**: "Reduce anxiety" → "Reduce scores on the SCARED-5 anxiety subscale by ≥5 points."  
- **Measurable**: Name the tool (SCARED, PHQ-9, custom survey) and frequency (pre/post, 3mo follow-up).  
- **Achievable**: Base targets on pilot data or literature (e.g., "Meta-analysis shows CBT groups achieve 40-60% symptom reduction").  
- **Relevant**: Tie directly to the need (e.g., if need was "youth anxiety linked to school avoidance," measure school attendance).  
- **Time-bound**: "By Month 12" or "at 6-month follow-up."  
- **Avoid**: Vague verbs like "improve," "enhance," "increase awareness." *Always* ask: "How will we *know* this changed?"  

**4.4 The Assumptions Trap: Where Models Fail**  
- **Unstated assumptions kill credibility**:  
  - *Assumption*: "Youth will attend weekly sessions."  
  - *Risk*: If transportation is a barrier (per your needs statement), attendance will fail.  
  - *Fix*: Add an activity: "Provide transit passes" or adjust outputs: "80% attendance rate among enrolled youth."  
- **How to stress-test**:  
  1. For each activity, ask: "What must be true for this to work?"  
  2. For each outcome, ask: "What other factors could influence this?"  
  3. Document top 3 assumptions and how you’ll monitor them (e.g., "Assumption: Schools will refer youth. Monitoring: Track referral sources monthly; if <50%, engage counselors").  

**Practical Checklist: Logic Model**  
[ ] Inputs match budget line items (e.g., if budget lists "2 therapists," inputs show "2 FTE therapists")  
[ ] Activities use strong verbs (facilitate, train, provide, conduct—not "offer" or "deliver")  
[ ] Outputs are direct products of activities (not outcomes)  
[ ] Outcomes are SMART and tied to needs statement consequences  
[ ] Impact is aspirational but plausible (not world peace in 2 years)  
[ ] Top 3 assumptions explicitly stated with monitoring plan  
[ ] Model fits on 1 page (use icons/color sparingly for clarity)  
[ ] Program staff can explain it in <2 minutes without notes

### **V. BUDGETING: WHERE TRUST IS WON OR LOST**  
*A sloppy budget screams incompetence—even if your narrative is brilliant.*  

**5.1 The Funder’s Budget Mindset**  
- They’re asking: "Is this *realistic*? Does it show fiscal maturity? Are we paying for *results* or just keeping lights on?"  
- **Golden Rule**: Every expense must tie to an activity in your logic model. If it doesn’t, cut it or justify why it’s essential overhead.  

**5.2 Line-Item Breakdown: What Funders Scrutinize**  
| **Category**       | **Best Practices**                                                                 | **Deadly Sins**                                  |  
|--------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|  
| **Personnel**      | - % time allocated to project (e.g., "Program Director: 0.2 FTE @ $60k = $12k")<br>- Fringe benefits shown separately (rate + calc: 25% of $12k = $3k)<br>- *Never* lump as "salaries" | - 100% time for execs on small projects<br>- Missing fringe<br>- Vague "staff costs" |  
| **Direct Costs**   | - Itemize *everything* >$500 (e.g., "Laptops (2) @ $800 = $1,600")<br>- In-kind donations valued at fair market (cite source: e.g., "Donated office space: 200 sq ft @ $25/sq ft/yr = $5k")<br>- Travel: IRS rate or actuals with justification | - "Supplies: $2k" (no detail)<br>- Overvaluing in-kind (e.g., claiming $50/hr for volunteer pro bono lawyer work when market is $150)<br>- First-class travel |  
| **Indirect/Overhead** | - Use your federally negotiated rate (if you have one)<br>- If not, use *de minimis* 10% of modified total direct costs (MTDC)<br>- *Never* invent a rate | - 30%+ overhead without justification<br>- Applying rate to unallowable costs (e.g., equipment) |  
| **Matching Funds** | - Specify source (e.g., "United Way grant: $10k"; "Board commitment: $5k")<br>- Confirm if cash or in-kind | - Vague "community support"<br>- Including unsecured funds as match |  

**5.3 Budget Narrative: Your Chance to Explain**  
- **Do**:  
  - Explain *why* costs are necessary (e.g., "Therapist mileage: Required for school-based sessions in 3 rural districts lacking public transit").  
  - Clarify odd line items (e.g., "Includes $500 for childcare during parent workshops—identified as barrier in 70% of focus groups").  
  - Show math for percentages (e.g., "Fringe: 28.3% based on FY23 audited rate").  
- **Don’t**:  
  - Repeat the line-item table.  
  - Apologize for costs ("We know this seems high, but...").  
  - Ignore unallowable costs (check guidelines—e.g., no food, no lobbying, no alcohol).  

**5.4 The Sustainability Question Funders Actually Care About**  
- They don’t expect you to replace their grant immediately—but they want to know you’re not creating a dependency.  
- **Strong answer**: "Year 1: 70% grant-funded. Year 2: 50% grant + 30% earned income (sliding-scale fees). Year 3: 30% grant + 50% earned + 20% corporate sponsors. By Year 4, <20% grant-reliant as school contracts scale."  
- **Weak answer**: "We will seek other grants." (Shows no plan.)  

**Practical Checklist: Budget**  
[ ] Total matches request amount exactly (no rounding)  
[ ] Personnel: % time + salary + fringe calc shown for each role  
[ ] All direct costs >$500 itemized with qty/rate  
[ ] In-kind valued at FMV with source cited (e.g., "per local commercial rates")  
[ ] Indirect rate: Either federally negotiated, *de minimis* 10%, or explicitly justified  
[ ] Matching funds: Source, amount, type (cash/in-kind), and confirmation status noted  
[ ] No unallowable costs (alcohol, lobbying, fines, entertainment per guidelines)  
[ ] Budget narrative explains *all* non-obvious line items  
[ ] Math checks out: Sum of line items = total; percentages correct  
[ ] Matches logic model inputs (e.g., if model lists "2 therapists," budget shows their cost)

### **VI. EVALUATION PLAN: PROVING YOU LEARN, NOT JUST DO**  
*Funders want to know you’ll use data to improve—not just check a box.*  

**6.1 Beyond Outputs: Measuring What Matters**  
- **Outputs vs. Outcomes**:  
  - *Output*: "Conducted 20 workshops" (activity count).  
  - *Outcome*: "80% of workshop participants reported increased knowledge of navigational skills (pre/post survey)."  
- **Funders care about outcomes**—but only if tied to your logic model and needs statement.  

**6.2 Designing a Credible Evaluation**  
- **Match rigor to risk/stakes**:  
  - *Low-risk/pilot*: Pre/post surveys, focus groups, attendance logs.  
  - *Medium-risk*: Comparison group (e.g., waitlist), validated instruments, basic stats (t-tests).  
  - *High-risk/scale*: RCT or quasi-experimental design (often requires external evaluator).  
- **Essential elements**:  
  - **Who collects data?** (Staff? External evaluator? Be specific—"Program Coordinator administers surveys"; not "we will collect data").  
  - **When?** (Baseline, immediate post, 3mo/6mo follow-up—justify timing).  
  - **How will you ensure quality?** (Training for data collectors, double-entry for surveys, inter-rater reliability for qualitative).  
  - **How will you use findings?** (Monthly team reviews? Quarterly board reports? Adjusting activities mid-grant?).  

**6.3 Common Evaluation Pitfalls**  
- **The Vanity Metric Trap**: Tracking easy-but-meaningless data (e.g., "number of Facebook likes").  
  *Fix*: Ask: "Does this metric tell us if we’re closer to our outcome?"  
- **Ignoring Attrition**: Reporting only on completers.  
  *Fix*: Use intent-to-treat analysis or report dropout reasons (e.g., "30% left due to job changes—we’re exploring evening sessions").  
- **Overpromising on Causality**: Claiming your program *caused* change without a comparison group.  
  *Fix*: Use language like "Participants showed X improvement compared to baseline" (not "vs. non-participants" without proof).  
- **Data Graveyard**: Collecting data never looked at again.  
  *Fix*: Build in a "data review" meeting in your project timeline.  

**Practical Checklist: Evaluation Plan**  
[ ] Clear link to logic model outcomes (e.g., "Measures Outcome 2.1: Increased parental knowledge")  
[ ] Specifies validated tools (name + version) or describes custom tool development/piloting  
[ ] Details data collection: Who, when, how, sample size, response rate target  
[ ] Addresses attrition: How will you track/minimize it? How will you analyze completers vs. dropouts?  
[ ] Explains data quality controls (training, verification, inter-rater reliability if qualitative)  
[ ] States *how* findings will be used for improvement (not just reporting)  
[ ] Includes timeline for analysis/reporting (e.g., "Quarterly data reviews; annual outcome report to board")  
[ ] Budget line for evaluation (even if internal—e.g., "0.1 FTE for data management")  
[ ] If using external evaluator: Scope of work, qualifications, cost justified

### **VII. THE PROPOSAL PACKAGE: POLISHING FOR PROFESSIONALISM**  
*Sloppy formatting signals sloppy thinking.*  

**7.1 Non-Negotiable Formatting Rules**  
- **Follow guidelines to the letter**: Font size, margins, page limits, file type (PDF vs. Word). *One deviation = instant triage*.  
- **Page limits are sacred**: If it says "max 5 pages," submit 4.9—not 5.1. Funders use page limits to test your ability to follow instructions.  
- **Clarity over creativity**: Use headings, bold sparingly for key points, and ample white space. Avoid decorative fonts/graphics unless explicitly allowed (and even then, keep it accessible).  
- **Accessibility matters**: Use alt text for images, sufficient color contrast, and logical heading structure (H1, H2, H3)—many funders use screen readers.  

**7.2 The Executive Summary: Written Last, Read First**  
- **Purpose**: Convince a busy program officer to keep reading in <30 seconds.  
- **Structure**:  
  1. **Hook** (1 sentence): The urgent problem you solve (from needs statement).  
  2. **Your Solution** (1 sentence): What you’ll do with *this* grant.  
  3. **Impact** (1 sentence): The measurable change you’ll create.  
  4. **Ask** (1 sentence): Amount + term + what it covers.  
  5. **Credibility** (1 sentence): Why you’re uniquely positioned (e.g., "10 years serving Oakwood youth with 90% client satisfaction").  
- *Example*: "In Oakwood, only 42% of 3rd graders read proficiently—leaving 580 children at risk of dropping out. Our Oakwood Literacy Corps will provide evidence-based tutoring to 120 struggling readers this year, targeting a 20-point proficiency gain. We seek $75,000 for one year to cover therapist salaries, materials, and school partnerships. With a decade of localized success and school district MOUs, we’re uniquely positioned to close this gap."  

**7.3 Attachments: The Make-or-Break Details**  
- **Always include if requested**:  
  - IRS determination letter (current)  
  - Board list with affiliations  
  - Key staff resumes (highlight *relevant* experience)  
  - Letters of commitment (not just support—specific: "XYZ School agrees to refer 40 students and provide space")  
  - Most recent audit/financials  
- **Never include unless asked**:  
  - Full strategic plan (share excerpt if relevant)  
  - Newspaper clippings (unless it’s a major award)  
  - Generic brochures  

**Practical Checklist: Proposal Package**  
[ ] Guidelines printed, annotated, and used as final checklist  
[ ] Executive summary written last, fits instructions (often 1 paragraph or 150 words)  
[ ] Page count verified *before* converting to PDF (check footers/headers)  
[ ] Font size ≥11pt; margins ≥0.75"; line spacing ≥1.1  
[ ] All tables/figures labeled and referenced in text  
[ ] IRB approval included if human subjects research >minimal risk  
[ ] Attachments: Only what’s requested, clearly labeled (e.g., "Attachment B: MOU with Oakwood USD")  
[ ] PDF properties: Title = "OrgName_Funder_ProjectTitle_Date"  
[ ] Final proofread: Read backwards sentence-by-line to catch typos  
[ ] Submitted ≥24h before deadline (never last-minute—tech fails happen)

### **VIII. POST-AWARD: TURNING GRANTS INTO RELATIONSHIPS**  
*The real work begins after the check clears. This is how you build trust for renewal.*  

**8.1 The Award Letter: Your New Contract**  
- **Read it 3x**:  
  1. First pass: Highlight reporting dates, amounts, and conditions.  
  2. Second pass: Note unallowable costs, matching requirements, and prior approval needs (e.g., "must get Funder approval to >10% budget shift").  
  3. Third pass: Calendar *all* deadlines with reminders 30/14/7 days out.  
- **Critical action**: Schedule a kickoff call with your program officer *within 2 weeks*—not to ask questions, but to say: "Thank you. Here’s how we’ll keep you updated."  

**8.2 Reporting: From Chore to Opportunity**  
- **Financial Reports**:  
  - Never estimate—use actuals from your accounting system.  
  - Show variance >±10% with explanation (e.g., "Travel under by 15% due to virtual trainings; redirected $ to materials").  
  - Match cost categories to your approved budget (don’t create new line items without approval).  
- **Narrative/Progress Reports**:  
  - **Structure**:  
    - *What happened* (activities/outputs vs. plan—be honest about delays).  
    - *What we learned* (surprises, barriers, adaptations—this builds trust).  
    - *Next steps* (how learning informs upcoming work).  
    - *Human impact* (1-2 strong vignettes + outcome data).  
  - **Deadly Sin**: Treating it like a renewal ask. Focus on *accountability*, not begging for more money.  
- **The Renewal Conversation Starts Now**:  
  - Share preliminary wins *informally* (e.g., "Midpoint update: 60% of youth showing anxiety reduction—on track for 60% target!").  
  - Invite the PO to a site visit or participant event (with consent).  
  - Ask: "What’s one thing we could do better to align with your goals?"  

**8.3 Stewardship That Gets You Funded Again**  
- **Beyond the report**:  
  - Send a *personalized*
