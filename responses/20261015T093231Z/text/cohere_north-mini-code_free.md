# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*An In‑Depth Guide from Funder Research to Post‑Award Reporting*  

---  

## 1. Introduction  

Nonprofit grant writing is a strategic craft that blends storytelling, data, and meticulous planning. Successful proposals not only convey a clear need and vision but also demonstrate fiscal responsibility, measurable outcomes, and alignment with a funder’s mission. This article walks you through every stage of the grant lifecycle, offering concrete examples, practical checklists, and proven best practices to help your organization secure funding and deliver impact.  

---  

## 2. Researching Funders  

### 2.1 Types of Funders  

| Funder Type | Typical Grants | Key Characteristics |
|-------------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Foundation** | Large endowments, thematic grants | Long‑term relationships, detailed reporting |
| **Government Agency** | Community block grants, health initiatives | Competitive, strict compliance |
| **Corporate** | Social responsibility programs | Often project‑specific, brand alignment |
| **International** | Global development funds | International standards, multi‑country focus |
| **Individual/Endowment** | Small seed grants | Personal connection, flexible terms |

### 2.2 Where to Find Funding Opportunities  

- **Grant Databases** – Foundation Center (Guidestar), National Philanthropic Trust, Grants.gov, Instrumentl.  
- **Funder Websites** – Direct “Grants” or “Apply” pages.  
- **Sector‑Specific Portals** – Education, health, arts, environment.  
- **Network Channels** – Professional associations, LinkedIn groups, conferences.  
- **Local Resources** – United Way, community foundations, city economic development offices.  

### 2.3 Research Checklist  

- [ ] **Confirm Eligibility** – Review mission, geography, target population, and funding limits.  
- [ ] **Read the Guidelines** – Note application deadline, required documents, and any pre‑application steps.  
- [ ] **Identify Key Contacts** – Note program officer name, email, and preferred communication method.  
- [ ] **Document Funding History** – Track past awards, current relationships, and any pending applications.  
- [ ] **Assess Alignment** – Map funder’s priorities to your program’s goals and outcomes.  
- [ ] **Capture Application Complexity** – Note required attachments, budget formats, and evaluation requirements.  
- [ ] **Create a Tracking Spreadsheet** – Columns: Funder, Deadline, Status, Contact, Notes, Alignment Score.  

### 2.4 Example: Funder Research Log  

```
| Funder | Grant Title | Deadline | Eligible? | Contact | Alignment | Status |
|--------|-------------|----------|-----------|---------|-----------|--------|
| XYZ Foundation | Community Health Initiative | 10/15/2024 | Yes | Jane Doe (jane@xyz.org) | High (health) | In Progress |
| City Health Dept | Preventive Care Grant | 09/30/2024 | Yes | Mike Lee (mike@city.gov) | Medium (access) | Not Started |

## 4. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

### 4.1 Core Elements  

1. **Context** – Brief description of the community or issue.  
2. **Data** – Statistics, research, or reports that quantify the need.  
3. **Impact** – Human stories, case studies, or testimonials that personalize the data.  
4. **Gap** – What is currently missing or insufficient in services.  
5. **Urgency** – Why immediate action is required (e.g., rising rates, funding cuts).  

### 4.2 Example Needs Statement  

> **Context:** In City X, 1 in 5 children under 12 lives in households with food insecurity, according to the 2023 Community Health Survey.  
>   
> **Data:** The local food bank reports a 22% increase in demand over the past two years, while federal nutrition assistance coverage has decreased by 8% due to recent policy changes.  
>   
> **Impact:** Maria, a single mother of three, relies on the food bank to feed her children; without this support, her children’s school performance has declined.  
>   
> **Gap:** Existing emergency food programs operate only three days per month and lack nutrition education.  
>   
> **Urgency:** With rising inflation and limited donor funding, the food bank’s current capacity will be exhausted by December 2024, leaving vulnerable families without essential support.  

### 4.3 Needs Statement Checklist  

- [ ] **Include Local Data** – Use the most recent, reputable sources.  
- [ ] **Add Human Element** – At least one vivid story or testimonial.  
- [ ] **Quantify the Gap** – Numbers make the need concrete.  
- [ ] **Highlight Urgency** – Explain timeline or consequences of inaction.  
- [ ] **Align with Funder Priorities** – Ensure the statement mirrors funder’s stated goals.

## 5. Developing the Proposal Narrative  

### 5.1 Structure Overview  

| Section | Purpose | Typical Length |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| **Executive Summary** | Snapshot of the proposal | 1‑2 pages |
| **Introduction/Background** | Context & problem | 1 page |
| **Needs Statement** | Data‑driven justification | 1‑2 paragraphs |
| **Program Description** | What you will do | 2‑3 pages |
| **Goals & Objectives** | SMART outcomes | 1 page |
| **Strategy/Implementation Plan** | How you’ll achieve outcomes | 2‑3 pages |
| **Evaluation Plan** | How you’ll measure success | 1 page |
| **Budget & Financial Management** | Cost breakdown & justification | 1‑2 pages |
| **Organizational Capacity** | Staff, experience, partnerships | 1 page |
| **Conclusion/Call to Action** | Recap benefits & request | 1 paragraph |

### 5.2 Example Goal Statement (SMART)  

> **Goal:** Reduce childhood obesity rates in City X by 15% within three years.  
>   
> **Objectives:**  
> - Increase daily physical activity sessions in after‑school programs from 2 to 4 per week (Objective 1).  
> - Provide nutrition workshops to 500 families, achieving a 30% knowledge gain (Objective 2).  
> - Partner with two local schools to implement daily fruit‑and‑vegetable snacks (Objective 3).  

### 5.3 Narrative Writing Checklist  

- [ ] **Use Clear, Concise Language** – Avoid jargon; aim for a 7th‑grade reading level.  
- [ ] **Quantify Every Claim** – “Help 200 families” vs. “help families.”  
- [ ] **Show Alignment** – Explicitly link each program element to the needs statement.  
- [ ] **Demonstrate Feasibility** – Include staff expertise, community partnerships, and resources.  
- [ ] **Highlight Sustainability** – Explain how the program will continue after the grant ends.

## 6. Designing a Logic Model  

### 6.1 Logic Model Components  

1. **Inputs** – Resources (staff, money, materials).  
2. **Activities** – Actions you will undertake.  
3. **Outputs** – Direct products of activities (e.g., number of workshops).  
4. **Outcomes** – Short‑term, intermediate, and long‑term changes.  
5. **Impact** – Broader, systemic changes.  

### 6.2 Example Logic Model (Food Bank Expansion)  

```
Inputs
- $150,000 grant
- 2 full‑time staff (Program Manager, Nutrition Educator)
- 5,000 lbs of donated food

Activities
- Conduct weekly food‑distribution drives
- Run nutrition education workshops
- Partner with local schools for fruit‑snack program

Outputs
- 300 families served per month
- 12 workshops delivered
- 2 schools partnered

Outcomes (Short‑term)
- 80% of participants report increased knowledge of healthy eating

Outcomes (Intermediate)
- 15% reduction in reported food insecurity among children in the target area

Outcomes (Long‑term)
- 10% decline in childhood obesity rates in City X over 3 years

Impact
- Strengthened community food security network; policy advocacy for increased nutrition assistance
```  

### 6.3 Logic Model Checklist  

- [ ] **Define All Inputs** – Include monetary, human, and in‑kind resources.  
- [ ] **Detail Activities** – Be specific enough to be measurable.  
- [ ] **Quantify Outputs** – Use numbers, not vague descriptors.  
- [ ] **Identify Outcome Levels** – Distinguish short, intermediate, and long‑term.  
- [ ] **Link to Evaluation** – Ensure each outcome has an indicator.

## 7. Budget Development and Justification  

### 7.1 Budget Categories  

| Category | Example Line Items | Tips |
|----------|-------------------|------|
| **Personnel** | Salaries (0.5 FTE), Benefits (20%) | Use salary scales; justify FTE. |
| **Travel** | Conference attendance, site visits | Provide purpose & budget rationale. |
| **Supplies** | Printed materials, workshop kits | Itemize per activity. |
| **Equipment** | Laptops, refrigerators | Note lifespan & depreciation. |
| **Overhead** | Rent, utilities (up to 10% of total) | Keep overhead transparent. |
| **Evaluation** | Survey software, data analysis | Link to outcomes. |

### 7.2 Example Budget Table  

```
Budget Summary – 12 Months
------------------------------------
Personnel
  Program Manager (0.5 FTE) – $45,000
  Nutrition Educator (0.3 FTE) – $27,000
  Benefits (20% of salaries) – $16,200
Supplies
  Printed workshop materials – $2,500
  Food bank distribution supplies – $3,000
Equipment
  Two commercial refrigerators – $8,000
Travel
  Site visits & partnership meetings – $1,200
Overhead (10% of total direct costs) – $12,420
Evaluation
  Survey platform license – $1,200
  Data analyst (200 hrs @ $25/hr) – $5,000
------------------------------------
Total Direct Costs: $141,420
Total Overhead: $14,142
Grand Total: $155,562
```  

### 7.3 Budget Justification Checklist  

- [ ] **Itemize Each Cost** – Provide a brief rationale for every line.  
- [ ] **Use Market Rates** – Compare to local salary surveys or vendor quotes.  
- [ ] **Explain In‑Kind Contributions** – Document value and source.  
- [ ] **Show Direct Link to Activities** – Connect each expense to a specific program component.  
- [ ] **Include Overhead Allocation** – Explain why the percentage is appropriate.

## 8. Timeline and Milestones  

### 8.1 Sample Timeline (Gantt‑Style)  

```
Month 1 – Project Kick‑off
Month 2 – Needs Assessment & Community Outreach
Month 3 – Program Design & Staff Hiring
Month 4 – Procurement of Equipment
Month 5 – Pilot Workshop
Month 6 – Full‑Scale Implementation
Month 7‑9 – Ongoing Activities & Data Collection
Month 10 – Mid‑Project Evaluation
Month 11 – Final Workshops & Reporting
Month 12 – Close‑out & Sustainability Planning

### 8.2 Milestone Checklist  

- [ ] **Define Key Milestones** – E.g., “Hire Program Manager by Month 2.”  
- [ ] **Assign Responsible Parties** – Who will deliver each milestone?  
- [ ] **Set Realistic Deadlines** – Allow buffer for unforeseen delays.  
- [ ] **Document Dependencies** – E.g., Equipment purchase depends on grant approval.  
- [ ] **Create a Visual Timeline** – Use Gantt chart or simple calendar view.

## 9. Evaluation and Metrics  

### 9.1 Evaluation Framework  

1. **Formative Evaluation** – Ongoing feedback during implementation.  
2. **Process Evaluation** – Assess whether activities are delivered as planned.  
3. **Outcome Evaluation** – Measure changes in target population.  
4. **Impact Evaluation** – Determine long‑term, systemic effects.  

### 9.2 Example Evaluation Plan  

```
Objective 1: Increase nutrition knowledge among families.
Indicator: % of participants scoring ≥80% on post‑workshop quiz.
Data Source: Pre‑ and post‑workshop surveys.
Target: 80% of 500 participants (400 families).
Timeline: End of Month 6.

Objective 2: Reduce food insecurity in target households.
Indicator: Change in household food security status (USDA scale).
Data Source: Monthly food security checklists.
Target: 15% reduction in insecure households.
Timeline: End of Month 12.
```  

### 9.3 Evaluation Checklist  

- [ ] **Select Measurable Indicators** – Use quantifiable metrics.  
- [ ] **Choose Data Collection Methods** – Surveys, interviews, observations.  
- [ ] **Plan Sample Size & Frequency** – Ensure statistical reliability.  
- [ ] **Assign Data Analysts** – Clarify roles and responsibilities.  
- [ ] **Develop Reporting Templates** – Align with funder requirements.

## 10. Writing the Executive Summary  

### 10.1 Essentials  

- **Hook** – Grab attention within the first sentence.  
- **Problem** – Briefly state the need.  
- **Solution** – Summarize your program and approach.  
- **Impact** – Highlight expected outcomes.  
- **Funding Request** – State amount and rationale.  
- **Call to Action** – Invite reader to next step.  

### 10.2 Example Executive Summary  

> **Problem:** In City X, 1 in 5 children experiences food insecurity, a rate that has risen 22% over the past two years.  
>   
> **Solution:** Our nonprofit proposes a 12‑month “Nutrition for Families” program that expands food distribution, provides nutrition education workshops, and partners with two local schools to offer daily fruit snacks.  
>   
> **Impact:** We aim to reduce childhood food insecurity by 15% and increase nutrition knowledge among 500 families.  
>   
> **Funding Request:** $155,562 to cover staff, supplies, equipment, and evaluation.  
>   
> **Call to Action:** We respectfully request your support to transform the lives of vulnerable families and build a healthier community.  

### 10.3 Executive Summary Checklist  

- [ ] **Keep Under 2 Pages** – Concise and scannable.  
- [ ] **Use Bold Headings** – Emphasize key sections.  
- [ ] **Include Numbers Early** – Data builds credibility.  
- [ ] **Mirror Funder Language** – Use terms from the RFP.  
- [ ] **Proofread** – No grammatical errors.

## 11. Review and Editing  

### 11.1 Peer Review Process  

1. **Internal Review** – Program staff, finance, and senior leadership.  
2. **External Review** – Peer organizations or consultants.  
3. **Funder Review** – If a “pre‑application” exists, incorporate feedback.  

### 11.2 Editing Checklist  

- [ ] **Format Compliance** – Follow font, margin, and page limits.  
- [ ] **Budget Alignment** – Ensure numbers match narrative.  
- [ ] **Logic Model Consistency** – Inputs → Activities → Outcomes.  
- [ ] **Evaluation Metrics** – Clearly linked to objectives.  
- [ ] **Legal & Ethical** – Disclosures, conflict‑of‑interest statements.

## 12. Post‑Award Management  

### 12.1 Subaward Management (if applicable)  

- **Select Subrecipients** – Ensure capacity and compliance track record.  
- **Draft Subaward Agreements** – Include scope, timeline, reporting, and audit rights.  
- **Monitor Progress** – Regular check‑ins, milestone reviews.  

### 12.2 Reporting Requirements  

| Report Type | Frequency | Key Content |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Financial Report** | Quarterly | Expenditures, cash balance, budget status. |
| **Progress Report** | Monthly/Bimonthly | Activities completed, outputs, challenges. |
| **Narrative Impact Report** | Semi‑annual | Outcomes achieved, lessons learned. |
| **Compliance Report** | As required | Audits, subaward disclosures, conflict checks. |

### 12.3 Financial Management Checklist  

- [ ] **Separate Grant Accounts** – Dedicated bank account and chart of accounts.  
- [ ] **Track Expenditures** – Use accounting software with grant modules.  
- [ ] **Reconcile Monthly** – Compare ledger to bank statements.  
- [ ] **Maintain Documentation** – Receipts, invoices, approval forms.  
- [ ] **Submit Timely Reports** – Meet all funder deadlines.  

### 12.4 Ongoing Relationship Building  

- **Newsletter Updates** – Share success stories and data.  
- **Site Visits** – Invite funder to see program in action.  
- **Open Communication** – Respond promptly to funder inquiries.

## 13. Conclusion  

Grant writing is a cyclical process that blends research, storytelling, and rigorous planning. By mastering each stage—from identifying the right funders and articulating a compelling needs statement, through developing robust logic models, budgets, and evaluation plans, to managing awards responsibly—nonprofits can increase their chances of securing funding and delivering lasting impact. Continuous learning, meticulous documentation, and strong relationships with funders are the cornerstones of sustainable success.
