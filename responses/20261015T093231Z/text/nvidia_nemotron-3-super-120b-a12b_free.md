**Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing: A Comprehensive Guide**  

---

### Introduction  
Grant writing is both an art and a science. Successful proposals blend compelling storytelling with rigorous data, clear budgets, and realistic evaluation plans. This guide walks you through every stage — from researching funders to post‑award reporting — offering concrete examples, step‑by‑step instructions, and practical checklists you can adapt to any nonprofit context.  

---

### Understanding the Grant Landscape  
- Recognize the three main funder categories: foundations, government agencies, and corporate giving programs.  
- Foundations often prioritize mission alignment and innovative approaches.  
- Government grants usually require strict compliance with federal or state regulations.  
- Corporate funders may seek brand visibility, employee engagement opportunities, or community impact tied to their business goals.  
- Review recent funding trends (e.g., increased support for mental health, climate resilience, equity initiatives).  
- Use databases such as Foundation Directory Online, Grants.gov, and Candid to scan for open opportunities.  
- Note typical award sizes, application cycles, and reporting frequencies for each funder type.  
- Identify whether a funder prefers unsolicited proposals or only accepts invited applications.  
- Keep a tracker of deadlines, contact persons, and any pre‑application webinars or informational sessions.  
- Understand the funder’s strategic plan to anticipate future priorities.

### Researching Funders  
**Step 1: Define Your Funding Needs**  
- List the specific project or program you need money for.  
- Quantify the amount required, broken down by category (personnel, supplies, overhead).  
- Determine the time frame (e.g., 12‑month pilot, 3‑year expansion).  

**Step 2: Build a Funder Profile**  
- Mission statement: Does it echo your nonprofit’s core purpose?  
- Geographic focus: Are they limited to a state, region, or nationwide?  
- Population served: Do they fund youth, seniors, veterans, etc.?  
- Funding history: Review their 990-PF forms or annual reports for past grantees.  
- Application guidelines: Note page limits, required attachments, and formatting rules.  

**Step 3: Use Research Tools**  
- Search Foundation Directory Online by keyword, location, and grant size.  
- Set up alerts in Grants.gov for new federal notices matching your CFDA codes.  
- Follow corporate CSR pages on LinkedIn for announcement of grant cycles.  
- Subscribe to newsletters from networks like GrantWatch or Philanthropy News Digest.  

**Step 4: Analyze Fit Scoring**  
- Create a simple rubric (0‑3) for mission alignment, geographic match, funding amount, and relationship potential.  
- Score each prospect; prioritize those with a total score of 9‑12.  

**Step 5: Validate Information**  
- Call the funder’s program officer (if allowed) to confirm current priorities.  
- Attend any virtual open houses or Q&A sessions.  
- Document notes in a shared spreadsheet for team reference.  

**Concrete Example**  
A community health nonprofit seeking $150,000 for a mobile clinic scored funders as follows:  
- Local Foundation A: Mission match 3, Geography 3, Amount 2, Relationship 1 = 9 (high priority).  
- State Health Department Grant: Mission match 2, Geography 3, Amount 3, Relationship 0 = 8 (medium priority).  
- National Corporate Wellness Program: Mission match 2, Geography 1, Amount 3, Relationship 0 = 6 (lower priority).  

**Checklist: Funder Research**  
- [ ] Project need clearly defined with budget outline.  
- [ ] Funder mission, geography, and population served documented.  
- [ ] Past grantees reviewed for similarity.  
- [ ] Application guidelines saved (PDF or web link).  
- [ ] Fit score calculated and prospects ranked.  
- [ ] Contact notes recorded after any interaction.

### Building Relationships Before You Apply  
- Initiate contact via a brief, personalized email introducing your organization and asking if they accept unsolicited proposals.  
- Request a 15‑minute informational call to learn about their current funding interests.  
- Share a one‑page impact snapshot that highlights outcomes relevant to their priorities.  
- Invite them to a site visit or virtual tour of your program (if appropriate).  
- Follow up with a thank‑you note summarizing key points and any requested materials.  
- Keep a relationship log: date, contact person, topics discussed, next steps.  
- Leverage board members or volunteers who may have existing connections to the funder.  
- Attend funder‑hosted workshops or conferences to network face‑to‑face.  
- Remember: relationship building is a long‑term investment; even if a proposal is declined, maintain the connection for future cycles.  

**Checklist: Pre‑Proposal Engagement**  
- [ ] Introductory email sent (personalized, <150 words).  
- [ ] Call scheduled and held; notes taken.  
- [ ] One‑page impact sheet prepared and shared.  
- [ ] Site visit or virtual tour arranged (if applicable).  
- [ ] Thank‑you note sent within 48 hours.  
- [ ] Relationship log updated.

### Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  
The needs statement convinces reviewers that the problem is urgent, significant, and solvable by your organization.  

**Elements of a Strong Needs Statement**  
1. **Data‑Driven Description** – Use recent statistics, surveys, or case studies to quantify the issue.  
2. **Human Impact** – Include a brief vignette or quote that illustrates the real‑life effect.  
3. **Gap Analysis** – Show what services currently exist and where the unmet need lies.  
4. **Root Causes** – Briefly mention underlying factors (e.g., policy barriers, lack of transportation).  
5. **Urgency** – Explain why action is needed now (e.g., rising trends, impending policy change).  

**Writing Tips**  
- Start with a striking fact or statistic to grab attention.  
- Keep language clear, avoid jargon, and maintain a tone of empathy paired with authority.  
- Limit the needs statement to one to two pages (or the funder’s specified length).  
- Cite sources inline (APA or funder‑preferred style) and provide a reference list if required.  
- Use comparative data (local vs. state/national) to highlight disparity.  

**Concrete Example**  
> “In 2023, 22% of children in Maple County lived below the federal poverty line, compared with 14% statewide (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024). At Lincoln Elementary, teachers reported that 38% of students arrived hungry, impairing concentration and academic performance (School Nutrition Survey, 2023). Existing food pantries operate only twice a month, leaving a gap of approximately 1,200 meals per week unmet. This need is urgent: research shows that children who miss breakfast score 10‑15% lower on standardized tests (Anderson et al., 2022).”  

**Checklist: Needs Statement**  
- [ ] Latest, credible data cited (source and date).  
- [ ] Clear quantification of the problem (numbers, percentages).  
- [ ] Human story or quote included.  
- [ ] Gap between need and current services explained.  
- [ ] Root causes briefly mentioned.  
- [ ] Urgency justified with trend or consequence.  
- [ ] Language free of jargon, empathetic yet authoritative.  
- [ ] Length conforms to funder limits.  
- [ ] References formatted correctly.

### Developing Goals and Objectives  
- **Goal**: Broad, long‑term change you hope to achieve (e.g., “Improve food security for children in Maple County”).  
- **Objectives**: SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound) steps that lead to the goal.  

**Writing SMART Objectives**  
- **Specific**: Who, what, where, why.  
- **Measurable**: Include a number or percentage.  
- **Achievable**: Realistic given resources and timeline.  
- **Relevant**: Directly tied to the need and goal.  
- **Time‑bound**: State the deadline or frequency.  

**Example**  
Goal: Increase access to nutritious meals for elementary students.  
Objective 1: By the end of Year 1, provide 5,000 healthy breakfasts to students at Lincoln Elementary, measured by meal distribution logs.  
Objective 2: Conduct monthly nutrition workshops for 150 parents, achieving at least 80% satisfaction as shown by post‑workshop surveys.  

**Checklist: Goals & Objectives**  
- [ ] One overarching goal stated clearly.  
- [ ] Each objective follows SMART criteria.  
- [ ] Objectives collectively lead to the goal.  
- [ ] Metrics and data sources identified for each objective.  
- [ ] Timeline attached to each objective.

### Creating Logic Models  
A logic model visualizes the relationship between resources, activities, outputs, outcomes, and impact.  

**Components**  
1. **Inputs** – Resources (funding, staff, partners, facilities).  
2. **Activities** – What you will do (e.g., hire nutritionist, procure food).  
3. **Outputs** – Direct products of activities (e.g., number of meals served, workshops held).  
4. **Outcomes** – Short‑term (knowledge change), medium‑term (behavior change), long‑term (health improvement).  
5. **Impact** – Ultimate societal change (e.g., reduced childhood obesity rates).  

**Steps to Build**  
- List all inputs needed for the project.  
- Break down the work plan into major activities.  
- For each activity, define measurable outputs.  
- Link outputs to outcomes using evidence‑based assumptions.  
- Review with stakeholders to ensure logic flows.  

**Simple Table Format**  

| Inputs                | Activities                     | Outputs                         | Short‑Term Outcomes          | Medium‑Term Outcomes          | Long‑Term Impact                |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------|---------------------------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Grant funds, staff    | Hire part‑time nutritionist    | 1 FTE nutritionist hired        | Increased nutrition knowledge| Improved breakfast attendance | Reduced food insecurity rates   |
| Partner farms         | Source local produce           | 2,000 lbs of produce sourced    | Greater access to fresh food | Higher fruit/veg consumption  | Better overall child health     |

**Checklist: Logic Model**  
- [ ] All major inputs listed.  
- [ ] Activities directly tied to inputs.  
- [ ] Outputs quantified (numbers, frequencies).  
- [ ] Outcomes categorized by time frame.  
- [ ] Impact statement reflects ultimate mission goal.  
- [ ] Model fits on one page (or funder’s required format).  
- [ ] Reviewed by program staff and an external advisor for clarity.

### Designing Program Activities  
- Align each activity with at least one objective.  
- Use evidence‑based practices (e.g., USDA’s Smart Snacks standards for school meals).  
- Build in flexibility for unforeseen challenges (e.g., supply chain delays).  
- Assign clear responsibility (who will lead each activity).  
- Develop a timeline or Gantt chart showing start/end dates and milestones.  
- Include contingency plans (e.g., alternate vendor if primary fails).  
- Document standard operating procedures (SOPs) for replication and quality control.  

**Concrete Example**  
Activity: Weekly breakfast service.  
- Responsible: Kitchen Manager (Jane Doe).  
- Steps: Menu planning → Food procurement → Preparation → Serving → Cleanup.  
- Timeline: Launch month 1, continue weekly for 12 months.  
- Metric: Number of breakfasts served per week (target 100).  

**Checklist: Program Activities**  
- [ ] Each activity linked to a specific objective.  
- [ ] Evidence‑based or best‑practice source cited.  
- [ ] Lead person and backup assigned.  
- [ ] Timeline with milestones created.  
- [ ] Required resources (staff, materials, vendors) identified.  
- [ ] Risk mitigation noted.  
- [ ] SOPs drafted or referenced.

### Developing a Realistic Budget  
A credible budget demonstrates fiscal responsibility and helps reviewers see value for money.  

**Budget Categories**  
1. **Personnel** – Salaries, wages, benefits, stipends.  
2. **Fringe Benefits** – Usually a percentage of personnel costs.  
3. **Contracts & Consultants** – External experts, evaluators.  
4. **Equipment** – Items >$5,000 with a useful life >1 year.  
5. **Supplies** – Consumables (food, office materials, program materials).  
6. **Travel** – Mileage, lodging, per diem for staff or participants.  
7. **Other Direct Costs** – Rent, utilities, participant incentives.  
8. **Indirect Costs / Overhead** – Facilities and administrative (F&A) rate, if allowed.  

**Best Practices**  
- Use the funder’s budget template if provided; otherwise, follow a standard format.  
- Show calculations (e.g., $20/hour × 1,040 hours = $20,800).  
- Justify each line item with a brief narrative (budget justification).  
- Include a contingency line (typically 5‑10% of total direct costs) only if permitted.  
- Match funds: Clearly indicate any cash or in‑kind contributions from your organization or partners.  
- Verify that totals match the narrative and logic model.  

**Concrete Example (Simplified)**  

| Category          | Amount  | Justification |
|-------------------|---------|---------------|
| Personnel (Nutritionist 0.5 FTE) | $30,000 | 0.5 FTE × $60,000 salary + 20% fringe |
| Supplies (Food)   | $45,000 | 5,000 meals × $9/meal (based on USDA cost) |
| Supplies (Nutrition Ed Materials) | $3,000 | Handouts, posters for workshops |
| Travel (Mileage)  | $1,200  | 2,000 miles × $0.60/mile for site visits |
| Indirect (10% of direct) | $7,920 | Standard F&A rate allowed by funder |
| **Total**         | **$87,120** | |

**Checklist: Budget Development**  
- [ ] Budget format matches funder requirements.  
- [ ] All costs itemized and calculated transparently.  
- [ ] Each line includes a brief justification.  
- [ ] Personnel costs reflect actual salaries and fringe rates.  
- [ ] Non‑personnel costs based on quotes or market research.  
- [ ] Matching funds (cash/in‑kind) clearly shown.  
- [ ] Indirect costs applied only if permitted, with rate cited.  
- [ ] Total matches the amount requested in the proposal narrative.  
- [ ] Math double‑checked; spreadsheet error‑free.

### Writing the Evaluation Plan  
Funders want to know how you will measure success and learn from the project.  

**Types of Evaluation**  
- **Formative** – Ongoing checks to improve implementation (e.g., monthly staff debriefs).  
- **Summative** – Final assessment of outcomes against objectives (e.g., pre/post surveys).  

**Components**  
1. **Evaluation Questions** – Derived from objectives (e.g., Did breakfast participation increase?).  
2. **Data Sources** – Surveys, attendance logs, interviews, secondary data.  
3. **Methods** – Quantitative (descriptive stats, t‑tests), qualitative (thematic analysis), or mixed methods.  
4. **Data Collection Timeline** – When each instrument will be administered.  
5. **Analysis Plan** – Who will analyze, software used, benchmarks for success.  
6. **Reporting** – How results will be shared with funder, staff, community.  
7. **Use of Findings** – How you will adjust programming or scale successful components.  

**Concrete Example**  
Evaluation Question: Did the breakfast program improve student attendance?  
- Data Source: School attendance records (baseline vs. program period).  
- Method: Compare average daily attendance rates using paired t‑test.  
- Success Criterion: ≥5% increase in attendance compared to prior year.  
- Analysis: Conducted by external evaluator using SPSS; results summarized in final report.  

**Checklist: Evaluation Plan**  
- [ ] Evaluation questions directly tied to each objective.  
- [ ] Data sources identified and accessible.  
- [ ] Collection methods described (instrument, frequency).  
- [ ] Timeline for data collection matches project schedule.  
- [ ] Analysis plan specifies statistical tests or qualitative approach.  
- [ ] Person or team responsible for evaluation named.  
- [ ] Budget line for evaluation (if external) included.  
- [ ] Plan for disseminating results to stakeholders.  
- [ ] Description of how findings will inform future programming.

### Sustainability and Impact  
Funders look for lasting change beyond the grant period.  

**Strategies**  
- **Diversify Funding** – Pursue additional grants, individual donors, earned income.  
- **Integrate into Core Operations** – Embed successful activities into the organization’s regular budget.  
- **Build Partnerships** – Formal MOUs with schools, health clinics, or local businesses.  
- **Capacity Building** – Train staff or volunteers to continue activities after grant ends.  
- **Policy Advocacy** – Work toward systemic changes that support the intervention (e.g., school wellness policies).  
- **Impact Metrics** – Track long‑term indicators (e.g., graduation rates, health outcomes) even after funding stops.  

**Concrete Example**  
After the grant, the nonprofit plans to transition breakfast service to the school district’s food service program, using the pilot data to secure a line item in the district’s annual budget. Simultaneously, they will launch a “Friends of Breakfast” monthly giving campaign to cover any shortfall.  

**Checklist: Sustainability**  
- [ ] Plan for diversifying revenue streams post‑grant.  
- [ ] Steps to integrate successful activities into core operations described.  
- [ ] Partnership agreements or MOUs outlined.  
- [ ] Capacity‑building activities (training, manuals) specified.  
- [ ] Policy or systems change efforts identified, if applicable.  
- [ ] Long‑term impact metrics defined and data source noted.  
- [ ] Timeline for sustainability activities aligned with grant end date.

### Writing the Executive Summary  
Though it appears first, write it last. It should be a standalone, compelling snapshot of the entire proposal.  

**Length** – Usually ½ to 1 page (or funder‑specified).  
**Content**  
- Brief statement of the problem and its significance.  
- Summary of the proposed solution (goal, objectives, key activities).  
- Amount of funding requested and total project cost.  
- Expected outcomes and impact.  
- Organizational capacity to implement (brief track record).  
- Sustainability plan in one sentence.  

**Concrete Example**  
> “Maple County faces a growing child food‑insecurity crisis, with 22% of children living below the poverty line and many lacking reliable breakfast. This project will launch a daily, nutritious breakfast program at Lincoln Elementary, serving 5,000 meals over 12 months and providing monthly nutrition workshops for families. We request $150,000 to cover personnel, food supplies, materials, and evaluation. Anticipated outcomes include a 10% increase in student attendance and a 15% improvement in self‑reported food security. The organization’s five‑year track record of successful school‑based health initiatives, combined with a sustainability plan to transition the program to the district’s food service, ensures lasting impact.”  

**Checklist: Executive Summary**  
- [ ] Written after full proposal is complete.  
- [ ] Problem statement concise, data‑backed.  
- [ ] Solution (goal, objectives, key activities) clearly summarized.  
- [ ] Funding request and total cost stated.  
- [ ] Expected outcomes and impact highlighted.  
- [ ] Organizational credibility demonstrated in ≤2 sentences.  
- [ ] Sustainability note included.  
- [ ] Length conforms to funder limits.  
- [ ] Free of jargon; readable by a lay reviewer.

### Assembling the Full Proposal  
Follow the funder’s outline precisely. Common sections: Cover Letter, Executive Summary, Needs Statement, Goals & Objectives, Program Description (Activities, Logic Model), Evaluation Plan, Budget & Justification, Organizational Capacity, Sustainability, Appendices.  

**Tips for Assembly**  
- Use consistent heading styles (e.g., Heading 1, Heading 2) for automatic table of contents.  
- Number pages consecutively; include a header with project name and page number.  
- Ensure fonts, margins, and spacing comply with guidelines (often 11‑pt Times New Roman, 1‑inch margins).  
- Label all tables and figures; reference them in the text.  
- Include a checklist of required attachments (e.g., IRS determination letter, audit, letters of support).  
- Perform a final read‑through for flow and redundancy.  
- Convert to PDF unless otherwise specified; verify that hyperlinks work.  

**Checklist: Proposal Assembly**  
- [ ] All required sections present and in correct order.  
- [ ] Page numbering and headers/footers applied.  
- [ ] Font, size, margins per guidelines.  
- [ ] Tables/figure numbered and referenced.  
- [ ] Appendices complete and labeled (Appendix A, B, …).  
- [ ] File naming convention followed (e.g., OrgName_ProjectName.pdf).  
- [ ] Final PDF opens correctly; no missing pages.  
- [ ] Submission confirmation email saved.

### Review and Editing Checklist  
Before submission, run a rigorous quality‑control process.  

**Content Review**  
- Does the needs statement compellingly justify the project?  
- Are goals, objectives, activities, and evaluation logically aligned?  
- Does the budget match the narrative and logic model?  
- Is the sustainability plan realistic and specific?  

**Mechanical Review**  
- Spelling, grammar, punctuation (use tools like Grammarly or Hemingway).  
- Consistent terminology (e.g., always “breakfast program” not “morning meal service”).  
- Correct citation format throughout.  
- Accurate totals in budget spreadsheet; cross‑check with narrative.  
- All URLs functional and not expired.  

**Peer Review**  
- Have at least two colleagues unfamiliar with the draft read for clarity.  
- Incorporate feedback, then do a final proofread.  

**Checklist: Pre‑Submission Review**  
- [ ] Needs statement supported by latest data.  
- [ ] Goal‑objective‑activity‑evaluation matrix complete.  
- [ ] Budget justification matches line items.  
- [ ] Sustainability actions concrete and time‑bound.  
- [ ] Spelling/grammar checked; read aloud for flow.  
- [ ] Terminology consistent throughout.  
- [ ] Citations and references formatted correctly.  
- [ ] All required attachments included.  
- [ ] Peer reviewer comments addressed.  
- [ ] Final PDF verified.

### Post‑Award Management  
Receiving the grant is only the beginning. Effective management ensures compliance, builds trust, and positions you for future funding.  

**Kick‑Off Meeting**  
- Review the award letter, reporting schedule, and any special conditions.  
- Assign a grant manager (often the program director or finance officer).  
- Set up a shared folder for all grant‑related documents.  

**Financial Management**  
- Establish a separate cost center or tracking code in your accounting system.  
- Record expenditures against budget lines monthly.  
- Save all receipts, invoices, and timesheets; maintain an audit trail.  
- Conduct internal budget vs. actual reviews quarterly.  

**Program Management**  
- Hold monthly team meetings to review progress toward objectives.  
- Update the logic model if activities shift (document changes and rationale).  
- Keep participants’ data confidential and secure (follow HIPAA/FERPA as applicable).  

**Communication with Funder**  
- Submit required reports on time (financial, progress, narrative).  
- Use the funder’s preferred format; include charts or photos when encouraged.  
- Notify the funder promptly of any significant changes (scope, budget, timeline).  
- Share successes and lessons learned via newsletters, social media, or briefings.  

**Checklist: Post‑Award Setup**  
- [ ] Award letter reviewed; special conditions noted.  
- [ ] Grant manager assigned and contact info shared with funder.  
- [ ] Accounting cost center/tracking code created.  
- [ ] Shared document repository set up (with version control).  
- [ ] Kick‑off meeting held; minutes circulated.  
- [ ] Reporting calendar entered into team planner.  
- [ ] Internal financial review schedule established.  
- [ ] Data protection protocols confirmed (e.g., encrypted files).

### Reporting Requirements  
Most funders require a combination of financial and narrative reports, often quarterly, semi‑annual, or annual.  

**Financial Reports**  
- **SF‑425 (Federal Financial Report)** or funder‑specific template.  
- Show cumulative expenditures, unobligated balance, and any program income.  
- Explain variances >10% between budgeted and actual costs.  

**Narrative/Progress Reports**  
- Summarize activities completed during the period.  
- Report on output metrics (e.g., number of meals served, workshops held).  
- Discuss preliminary outcome data and any challenges faced.  
- Include lessons learned and adjustments to the work plan.  
- Attach supporting documents (e.g., sample workshop agenda, photos with consent).  

**Final Report**  
- Comprehensive overview of the entire grant period.  
- Full accounting of expenditures, with reconciliation to the award amount.  
- Detailed outcome analysis comparing baseline to end‑line data.  
- Narrative of impact, including quotes, case studies, and photographs.  
- Sustainability update: what will continue, what ended, and why.  
- Dissemination plan for results (presentations, publications, policy briefs).  

**Concrete Example (Quarterly Report Excerpt)**  
> “During Q2, we served 1,250 breakfasts (target 1,200) and conducted three nutrition workshops reaching 45 parents (target 40). Attendance logs show a 4% increase in average daily attendance compared to baseline, on track to meet the 5% annual goal. A supply‑chain delay caused a temporary shortage of fresh fruit; we mitigated this by partnering with a local food bank for supplemental produce, ensuring no meals were skipped.”  

**Checklist: Reporting**  
- [ ] Reporting schedule entered in calendar with reminders.  
- [ ] Financial report template downloaded and pre‑filled with chart of accounts.  
- [ ] Expenditures tracked by budget line; receipts filed digitally.  
- [ ] Narrative draft outline prepared before data collection ends.  
- [ ] Output metrics pulled from program databases (attendance logs, survey tools).  
- [ ] Variance explanations drafted for any >10% budget deviations.  
- [ ] Photos and quotes collected with signed release forms.  
- [ ] Final report sections assigned to responsible staff.  
- [ ] Submission confirmation saved; copy archived.

### Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them  
| Pitfall | Consequence | Prevention Strategy |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| Vague needs statement | Reviewers doubt significance | Use current, localized data; include a human story |
| Misaligned objectives | Activities don’t measure intended change | Map each objective to at least one activity and one output |
| Overly optimistic budget | Funds run out prematurely | Get three quotes for major expenses; include a modest contingency only if allowed |
| Missing attachments | Application deemed incomplete | Use a checklist; attach PDFs of all required docs before final review |
| Ignoring funder’s formatting rules | Automatic disqualification | Read the guideline twice; run a compliance check before submitting |
| Weak evaluation plan | Cannot demonstrate impact | Tie evaluation questions directly to objectives; specify data sources and analysis |
| Poor post‑award tracking | Compliance issues, strained relations | Set up a dedicated grant manager and monthly financial review from day one |
| Overpromising outcomes | Credibility damage if not met | Base targets on pilot data or literature; be realistic and explain assumptions |
| Failure to acknowledge partners | Missed relationship building | List all collaborators in the proposal and in reports; share credit |
| Leaving sustainability as an afterthought | Funder questions long‑term value | Develop a sustainability plan concurrent with program design |

**Checklist: Pitfall Avoidance**  
- [ ] Needs statement backed by specific data and anecdote.  
- [ ] Objective‑activity matrix completed and reviewed.  
- [ ] Budget quotes obtained; contingency only if permitted.  
- [ ] Attachment checklist used; all files appended before final PDF.  
- [ ] Formatting compliance verified (fonts, margins, page limits).  
- [ ] Evaluation questions derived from objectives; methods described.  
- [ ] Grant manager appointed; financial tracking set up pre‑award.  
- [ ] Outcome targets grounded in evidence; assumptions noted.  
- [ ] Partner roles and letters of support included.  
- [ ] Sustainability section drafted alongside program narrative.
