**6.  Researching Funders – Step‑by‑Step**  
6.1  Identify Potential Funders (Foundations, Government, Corporate, Individual)  
6.2  Build a Master Funder Database (Spreadsheet Columns: Name, Focus, Deadline, Contact, Link, Funding Range)  
6.3  Conduct Deep‑Dive Research (Annual Reports, 990s, Press Releases, Past Grants)  
6.4  Evaluate Fit – Mission Alignment, Geographic Scope, Funding Priorities, Competitive Landscape  
6.5  Create a “Fit Scorecard” (1‑5 rating for each criterion)  
6.6  Set Up Alerts (Foundation Center, GrantStation, IRS Tax‑Exempt Search)  

**7.  Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement**  
7.1  Define the Problem with Data (Statistics, Case Studies, Trends)  
7.2  Use Real‑World Examples (e.g., “In 2023, 1 in 4 children in County X experienced food insecurity”)  
7.3  Highlight the Gap Between Current Resources & Desired Outcomes  
7.4  Connect the Need to the Funder’s Priorities (Quote from funder’s strategic plan)  
7.5  Write in Clear, Concise Language – Avoid Jargon, Use Active Voice  
7.6  Checklist:  
   - [ ] Starts with a compelling hook (question, statistic, anecdote)  
   - [ ] Provides up‑to‑date, credible data (cite sources)  
   - [ ] Describes the affected population (who, where, when)  
   - [ ] Explains why the problem matters now  
   - [ ] Links directly to the funder’s mission  

**8.  Articulating Goals, Objectives, and Outcomes**  
8.1  Distinguish Goals (Broad) vs. Objectives (Specific, Measurable)  
8.2  Follow the SMART Framework (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound)  
8.3  Example Objective: “By December 2025, increase the literacy rate of 3rd‑grade students in District Y by 15%.”  
8.4  Define Outcomes vs. Outputs (Outcomes = change in behavior/condition; Outputs = activities delivered)  
8.5  Use Outcome‑Based Language (“Participants will demonstrate…”)  
8.6  Checklist:  
   - [ ] Each objective has a clear metric and target date  
   - [ ] Outcomes are linked to the needs statement  
   - [ ] Objectives are realistic given budget and capacity  

**9.  Designing a Logic Model**  
9.1  Identify Inputs (Funding, Staff, Partnerships, Materials)  
9.2  Map Activities (Workshops, Service Delivery, Outreach)  
9.3  Specify Outputs (Number of workshops held, materials distributed)  
9.4  Define Short‑Term Outcomes (Knowledge gained, skills practiced)  
9.5  Link to Intermediate Outcomes (Behavior change, service utilization)  
9.6  Show Long‑Term Impact (Community health improvement, economic mobility)  
9.7  Visual Layout Tips: Use a left‑to‑right flowchart, color‑code phases, keep legends simple  
9.8  Checklist:  
   - [ ] All components are labeled clearly  
   - [ ] Connections are logical (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes)  
   - [ ] Includes a “Assumptions” box for external factors  
   - [ ] Can be explained in ≤ 30 seconds to a non‑expert  

**10.  Writing the Project Description**  
10.1  Structure: Executive Summary → Problem → Solution → Methods → Timeline → Staff → Evaluation  
10.2  Executive Summary – 150‑word snapshot (mission, need, ask, expected impact)  
10.3  Methods – Detail each activity, responsible party, frequency, and required resources  
10.4  Timeline – Gantt‑style milestones, align with budget periods  
10.5  Staff & Capacity – Highlight key personnel, qualifications, and any partners  
10.6  Sustainability Plan – How the program will continue after grant period  
10.7  Checklist:  
   - [ ] All sections flow logically, no repetitive sentences  
   - [ ] Uses active verbs (“deliver,” “train,” “evaluate”)  
   - [ ] Includes a clear “ask” (dollar amount, in‑kind support)  
   - [ ] Avoids over‑promising; statements are evidence‑based  

**11.  Budget Development – The Financial Blueprint**  
11.1  Budget Types: Direct Costs (Personnel, Supplies), Indirect Costs (Overhead), Capital Expenses  
11.2  Build a Detailed Line‑Item Budget Spreadsheet (Columns: Category, Description, Quantity, Unit Cost, Total)  
11.3  Personnel – Include salaries, fringe rates, and time‑percentage allocations  
11.4  Supplies & Materials – List items, quantity, unit cost, vendor quotes  
11.5  Travel – Specify purpose, destinations, frequency, cost per trip  
11.6  Contractual Costs – Sub‑grants, consultant fees, matching contributions  
11.7  Indirect Costs – Apply the funder’s negotiated rate (e.g., 10% of modified total direct costs)  
11.8  Matching Funds – Document pledged cash or in‑kind contributions (list donor, amount, condition)  
11.9  Budget Narrative – Explain each line item, justify costs, reference quotes or market rates  
11.10  Checklist:  
   - [ ] Totals match the requested grant amount exactly  
   - [ ] All numbers are rounded to the nearest dollar (or consistent decimal)  
   - [ ] Narrative aligns with activities described in the project description  
   - [ ] No “blank” rows or vague entries (e.g., “miscellaneous”)  
   - [ ] Budget is reviewed by finance staff for compliance  

**12.  Logic Model ↔ Budget Alignment**  
12.1  Cross‑Reference Each Activity with Its Cost Line Items  
12.2  Ensure Output Quantities in Budget Match Logic Model Outputs  
12.3  Flag Any Budget Items Not Reflected in the Logic Model (justify or remove)  
12.4  Verify that all matching contributions are accounted for in both models  

**13.  Drafting the Evaluation Plan**  
13.1  Set Evaluation Questions (What will we learn? How will we know we succeeded?)  
13.2  Choose Evaluation Type (Process, Outcome, Impact) and Methodology (Quantitative, Qualitative, Mixed)  
13.3  Identify Data Sources (Surveys, Interviews, Administrative Data)  
13.4  Define Indicators (Input: # of staff trained; Output: # of workshops; Outcome: % increase in skill)  
13.5  Establish Data Collection Timeline (Baseline, Mid‑term, Endline)  
13.6  Assign Responsibility (Internal staff, external evaluator)  
13.7  Plan for Data Analysis (Statistical tests, thematic coding)  
13.8  Report Back to Funders (Final Report, Executive Summary, Visual Dashboards)  
13.9  Checklist:  
   - [ ] Evaluation plan is integrated into the project timeline  
   - [ ] Indicators are SMART and linked to objectives  
   - [ ] Data collection tools are pilot‑tested  
   - [ ] Ethical considerations (IRB, consent) are addressed  

**14.  Writing the Cover Letter & Attachments**  
14.1  Cover Letter Structure: Greeting → Organization Overview → Funding Ask → Alignment → Closing  
14.2  Tailor Language to Each Funder (use their terminology, reference their recent work)  
14.3  Attach Required Documents in Specified Order (Cover Sheet, Narrative, Budget, Attachments)  
14.4  Verify Formatting (Font, Margins, Page Limits)  
14.5  Proofread for Typos, Consistent Terminology, and Correct Names of Program Officers  
14.6  Checklist:  
   - [ ] Letter is ≤ 1 page, professional tone  
   - [ ] Includes correct funder name and contact person  
   - [ ] Clearly states total amount requested and budget period  
   - [ ] All attachments are labeled and numbered as requested  

**15.  Final Review & Submission Checklist**  
15.1  Peer Review – Have at least two staff members read the full proposal  
15.2  Compliance Check – Meet all formatting, page‑limit, and submission‑portal requirements  
15.3  File Naming Convention (e.g., “OrgName_Funder_2025_Proposal.pdf”)  
15.4  Backup Files – Store on secure drive and cloud (multiple copies)  
15.5  Submission Confirmation – Verify receipt email or portal status  
15.6  Post‑Submission – Set reminders for follow‑up, debrief meeting, and next‑cycle planning  

**16.  Post‑Award Management – From Funding to Impact**  
16.1  Kick‑off Meeting – Review timeline, reporting schedule, and key contacts  
16.2  Quarterly Progress Reports – Include narrative updates, financial spend, and indicator snapshots  
16.3  Annual Audited Financial Statements – Submit as required  
16.4  Mid‑term Site Visits – Prepare documentation, answer reviewer questions  
16.5  Final Close‑out Report – Summarize outcomes, lessons learned, and sustainability plan  
16.6  Checklist:  
   - [ ] All required reports submitted on time  
   - [ ] Financial records reconciled and archived  
   - [ ] Data for all outcomes collected and analyzed  
   - [ ] Stakeholder feedback gathered (beneficiaries, partners)  
   - [ ] Lessons documented for future grant cycles  

**17.  Common Pitfalls & How to Avoid Them**  
17.1  Over‑Ambitious Goals – Keep objectives realistic and evidence‑based  
17.2  Vague Language – Replace “improve community health” with “reduce asthma hospitalizations by 10% in ZIP code 12345”  
17.3  Ignoring Funder Priorities – Re‑read the RFP after each draft round  
17.4  Budget Mis‑alignment – Re‑calculate totals after any narrative change  
17.5  Missing Attachments – Use a pre‑submission checklist (see Section 15)  
17.6  Late Submissions – Build a submission calendar with buffer days

**19.  Real‑World Example Walkthrough**  
19.1  *Scenario*: “Bright Futures” seeks a $250,000 grant to expand after‑school STEM tutoring for 300 middle‑school students in Metro City.  
19.2  *Research*: Identified “TechFuture Foundation” as a fit; their 2023 annual report emphasizes “STEM education for underserved youth.”  
19.3  *Needs Statement*: Cites 2022 Metro School District report – 38% of 8th‑graders lack access to extracurricular STEM programs.  
19.4  *Logic Model*: Inputs → Activities (tutoring sessions, mentor matching) → Output (200 tutoring hours) → Outcome (15% increase in math test scores).  
19.5  *Budget*: Personnel (2 part‑time tutors @ $45k), Supplies (laptops, kits @ $5k), Indirect (10% of direct). Total = $250k.  
19.6  *Evaluation*: Pre‑ and post‑test scores, attendance tracking, parent satisfaction surveys.  
19.7  *Submission*: Cover letter tailored to TechFuture’s “Innovation in Education” priority; attached logic model, budget, and evaluation plan.  
19.8  *Post‑Award*: Quarterly reports show 120 tutoring sessions delivered, 12% improvement in math scores, and 95% attendance rate.

**21.  Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)**  
21.1  *Q*: How long should a needs statement be?  
21.2  *A*: Typically 150‑300 words; concise but data‑rich.  

21.3  *Q*: Can I include in‑kind contributions in the budget?  
21.4  *A*: Yes, but they must be documented, valued, and clearly linked to the project.  

21.5  *Q*: What if my organization lacks a 501(c)(3) status?  
21.6  *A*: Seek a fiscal sponsor or apply for a provisional status; some funders accept non‑profit collaborations.  

21.7  *Q*: How do I handle a budget shortfall after award?  
21.8  *A*: Submit a budget amendment with justification; request additional funding if the funder permits.

22.2  Celebrate successes and share lessons across the organization  
22.3  Keep the funder relationship alive even after the grant ends (thank‑you notes, impact updates)  
22.4  Continuously refine the master database and checklists based on experience  
22.5  Remember: a well‑crafted proposal is both a fundraising tool and a roadmap for mission‑driven change
