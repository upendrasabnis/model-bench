# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*An in‑depth guide from funder research to post‑award reporting*  

---  

## 1. Introduction  

Writing successful grants is both an art and a science.  It requires a deep understanding of the funder’s mission, a crystal‑clear articulation of community need, a realistic budget, a logical theory of change, and a robust evaluation plan.  This article walks you through each stage of the grant‑writing process, offering concrete examples, practical checklists, and tips that can be applied to any nonprofit—whether you are a small grassroots organization or a mid‑size service provider.  

> **Quick takeaway:**  Grant writing is a systematic process.  The more you document and repeat the steps, the higher your success rate.  

---  

## 2. Understanding the Grant Landscape  

### 2.1 Why Funder Research Matters  

Before you even draft a needs statement, you must know *who* you are asking, *what* they fund, and *how* they evaluate proposals.  Research helps you:  

* Align your project with the funder’s strategic priorities.  
* Avoid “spray‑and‑pray” approaches that waste time and resources.  
* Build relationships with funders (many foundations value repeat partnerships).  

### 2.2 Strategies and Tools for Funder Research  

| Tool | How to Use It | Example |
|------|---------------|---------|
| **Foundation Center (Candid)** | Search by geography, issue area, or funding size. | Look up “Robert Wood Johnson Foundation – Health Services.” |
| **GrantStation & Grantspace** | Subscription‑based databases with alerts for new deadlines. | Set up alerts for “Education – K‑12.” |
| **Funder Websites & Annual Reports** | Direct source for current priorities and application cycles. | Review the “2024 Funding Priorities” PDF. |
| **Social Media & Newsletters** | Many funders share upcoming opportunities via Twitter, LinkedIn, or email lists. | Follow @FordFoundation on Twitter. |
| **Local Grant‑Making Networks** | Attend regional nonprofit conferences; meet funders in person. | Join your state’s Nonprofit Network. |
| **IRS Form 990 (for private foundations)** | Reveals grant‑making patterns and grant amounts. | Analyze a foundation’s 990 to see typical grant size. |

### 2.3 Checklist for Funders Research  

- [ ] **Identify 5–10 potential funders** that match your mission.  
- [ ] **Document each funder’s:**  
  - Primary funding focus (e.g., youth development, environmental justice).  
  - Geographic restrictions (local, regional, national).  
  - Funding size range (e.g., $10k–$250k).  
  - Application deadlines (note in a calendar).  
  - Required documentation (e.g., budget template, logic model).  
- [ ] **Review past grants** (annual reports, press releases) to see funded projects.  
- [ ] **Create a “Funder Profile Sheet”** (see Example below) for quick reference.  
- [ ] **Set up alerts** for new opportunities via GrantStation or email newsletters.

## 3. Crafting the Compelling Needs Statement  

### 3.1 What Makes a Needs Statement Powerful?  

A needs statement does more than list statistics; it tells a story that:  

1. **Shows urgency** – “X children are currently without safe housing.”  
2. **Demonstrates scope** – “In the past year, 30% of families in our service area have experienced chronic homelessness.”  
3. **Links to outcomes** – “Without intervention, 70% of these children drop out of school.”  
4. **Establishes credibility** – Cite data from reputable sources (census, health departments, academic studies).  

### 3.2 Components of a Strong Needs Statement  

| Component | What to Include | Example |
|-----------|----------------|---------|
| **Population Served** | Demographics, size, geographic location. | “Children ages 5‑12 in the Westside neighborhood (population 12,500).” |
| **Problem Statement** | Clear, concise description of the issue. | “30% of these children lack access to after‑school tutoring, leading to lower academic performance.” |
| **Data & Evidence** | Recent statistics, trend analysis, source citations. | “According to the 2023 County Education Report, 42% of Westside students scored below grade level in math.” |
| **Impact** | Short‑ and long‑term consequences if problem persists. | “Without support, these children are 2.5× more likely to repeat a grade.” |
| **Gap Analysis** | What currently exists vs. what is needed. | “Only one free tutoring program operates in the area, serving 15 students.” |
| **Call to Action** | How the proposed project will address the gap. | “Our proposed program will expand services to 100 children, filling the critical gap.” |

### 3.3 Example Needs Statement  

> **Population:**  Children ages 5‑12 in the Riverbend School District (≈ 2,300 students).  
> **Problem:**  According to the 2022 District Education Survey, 48 % of Riverbend students score below grade level in mathematics, a 12‑point increase since 2018.  
> **Impact:**  Students who fall behind in math are 3.2× more likely to drop out by grade 12, perpetuating a cycle of low‑income employment.  
> **Gap:**  Only one after‑school math‑tutoring program exists, serving 30 students annually; demand exceeds capacity by 250 % (based on 120 families on the waiting list).  
> **Call to Action:**  Our “Math Boost” program will expand tutoring capacity to serve 120 students, reduce the achievement gap by 20 % within two years, and provide teachers with data‑driven instructional resources.  

### 3.4 Checklist for Needs Statement  

- [ ] **Define the target population** (age, location, size).  
- [ ] **Quantify the problem** (percentage, number of individuals, trend).  
- [ ] **Cite reputable data sources** (census, school districts, health agencies).  
- [ ] **Explain the consequences** (short‑term and long‑term).  
- [ ] **Identify existing services** and the gap they leave.  
- [ ] **Articulate how your project directly addresses the gap** (specific outcomes).  
- [ ] **Keep language clear and jargon‑free**; aim for a 7th‑grade reading level.

## 4. Developing the Project Narrative  

### 4.1 Core Elements of the Narrative  

The narrative tells the reader *what* you will do, *why* you will do it, and *how* you will do it.  Include:  

1. **Project Title** – Concise, descriptive, and memorable.  
2. **Statement of Need** – (Already covered in the needs statement; repeat concisely.)  
3. **Goals & Objectives** – SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound).  
4. **Approach & Methods** – Step‑by‑step description of activities.  
5. **Staff & Partners** – Roles, qualifications, and collaboration agreements.  
6. **Sustainability** – How the project will continue after grant ends.  

### 4.2 Example Goals & Objectives  

| Goal | Objective (SMART) | Measurement |
|------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Goal:** Improve math proficiency for 120 Riverbend students. | **Objective 1:** Provide 120 students with 30 hours of after‑school tutoring. | Track via attendance logs. |
| | **Objective 2:** Increase math test scores by at least 15 % within 12 months. | Compare pre‑ and post‑program standardized test scores. |
| | **Objective 3:** Train 5 volunteer tutors to use the program’s curriculum. | Issue certificates and log training hours. |

### 4.3 Example Project Narrative (excerpt)  

> **Project Title:** *Math Boost After‑School Tutoring Program*  
>   
> **Statement of Need:** 48 % of Riverbend students score below grade level in mathematics, a trend that correlates with higher dropout rates.  
>   
> **Goals & Objectives:** (as above)  
>   
> **Approach:**  
> - **Curriculum Development:** Adapt the “Math Mastery” curriculum (licensed at $2,500) to align with district standards.  
> - **Staffing:** Hire two certified math teachers (40 hrs/week each) and recruit five trained volunteers.  
> - **Delivery:** Run sessions Monday‑Thursday, 3:30‑5:30 pm, at Riverbend Elementary School.  
> - **Monitoring:** Weekly attendance logs; bi‑monthly assessments.  
>   
> **Partnerships:** Collaborate with Riverbend School District (provides space, student referrals) and the local library (supplies study materials).  
>   
> **Sustainability:** After the 2‑year grant, the program will transition to a fee‑for‑service model ($150 per student per semester) and seek ongoing support from the district’s budget.  

### 4.4 Checklist for Narrative  

- [ ] **Project title** is clear and catchy.  
- [ ] **Need statement** is summarized (≤ 2 sentences).  
- [ ] **Goals** are limited to 3‑5 maximum.  
> [ ] **Objectives** are SMART and quantifiable.  
- [ ] **Approach** is described chronologically with concrete actions.  
- [ ] **Staff/partner roles** are defined (titles, responsibilities).  
- [ ] **Sustainability plan** outlines post‑grant funding sources.  
- [ ] **Readability:** Aim for 7th‑grade level; use active voice.

## 5. Designing the Budget  

### 5.1 Types of Budgets  

| Budget Category | Description | Typical Examples |
|-----------------|-------------|------------------|
| **Personnel** | Salaries, benefits, wages. | Two teachers ($55,000 each), part‑time admin ($20,000). |
| **Materials & Supplies** | Curriculum, books, software, tutoring kits. | Math Mastery license ($2,500), workbooks ($1,200). |
| **Facilities** | Rent, utilities, equipment. | After‑school room at elementary school ($12,000). |
| **Travel & Mileage** | Transportation for outreach, conferences. | Staff travel to partner sites ($800). |
| **Evaluation & Reporting** | Surveys, data analysis, report writing. | External evaluator ($7,500). |
| **Indirect Costs** | Organization overhead (if allowed). | 10 % of total direct costs. |

### 5.2 Example Budget (rounded to nearest $100)  

| Category | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|----------|--------|--------|
| Personnel (2 teachers) | $110,000 | $110,000 |
| Volunteer Stipends (5 × $2,000) | $10,000 | $10,000 |
| Materials & Supplies (curriculum, books) | $3,700 | $3,700 |
| Facilities (rent, utilities) | $12,000 | $12,000 |
| Travel & Mileage | $800 | $400 |
| Evaluation (external evaluator) | $7,500 | $5,000 |
| Indirect Costs (10 % of direct) | $14,700 | $14,200 |
| **Total Direct Costs** | **$159,000** | **$156,000** |
| **Total with Indirect** | **$173,700** | **$172,200** |

### 5.3 Budget Best Practices  

1. **Use a line‑item format** – each expense appears on its own line.  
2. **Include a narrative explanation** for large or unusual line items (e.g., “Why $7,500 for evaluation?”).  
3. **Align with the logic model** – each budget line should support a specific activity or outcome.  
4. **Be realistic** – consult staff, vendors, and previous projects for cost estimates.  
5. **Separate direct vs. indirect** – many funders have caps on indirect costs.  
6. **Use consistent formatting** – same font, size, and column headings throughout the document.  

### 5.4 Checklist for Budget  

- [ ] **All expenses are justified** with brief notes.  
- [ ] **Personnel costs include benefits** (if applicable).  
- [ ] **Materials are priced from at least two vendors** (where possible).  
- [ ] **Facilities costs reflect actual usage** (not inflated).  
- [ ] **Travel includes mileage reimbursement rate** (e.g., $0.65/mile).  
- [ ] **Evaluation costs are tied to specific data collection methods**.  
- [ ] **Indirect cost rate is approved by the organization’s IRS status** (if claimed).  
- [ ] **Budget totals match the narrative scope** (e.g., number of students × cost per student).  
- [ ] **Formatting is consistent** (e.g., bold headings, aligned columns).

## 6. Building the Logic Model  

### 6.1 Why a Logic Model?  

A logic model is the visual backbone that connects inputs, activities, outputs, and outcomes.  It helps funders see *how* you will achieve impact and serves as a planning tool for your team.  

### 6.2 Core Components  

| Component | Definition | Example (Math Boost) |
|-----------|------------|----------------------|
| **Inputs** | Resources you bring to the project (staff, money, materials). | 2 teachers, $110k salary, curriculum license. |
| **Activities** | What you will do with those resources. | 30 hours of after‑school tutoring, weekly assessments. |
| **Outputs** | Immediate, tangible results of activities. | 120 students tutored, 5 volunteers trained. |
| **Outcomes** | Short‑term, intermediate, and long‑term changes you aim for. | ↑ Math scores (15 % gain), ↑ graduation intent, ↓ dropout rate. |
| **Impact** | Broader, longer‑term societal changes. | Increased economic mobility for Riverbend families. |

### 6.3 Example Logic Model Diagram (textual)  

```
[Inputs]
   • 2 Certified Teachers
   • $110,000 Salary
   • Math Mastery Curriculum

          ↓ (Activities)
[Provide 30 hrs/week tutoring]
   • 120 students
   • 5 volunteer trainers
   • Weekly assessments

          ↓ (Outputs)
[Deliver 3,600 tutoring hrs]
   • Attendance logs
   • Pre/post test data
   • Volunteer certifications

          ↓ (Outcomes)
[Short‑term: ↑ Math scores by 15 %]
[Intermediate: ↑ Academic confidence]
[Long‑term: ↓ Dropout rate]

          ↓ (Impact)
[Improved economic prospects for Riverbend families]
```

### 6.4 Checklist for Logic Model  

- [ ] **Inputs** are realistic and fully funded.  
- [ ] **Activities** directly lead to the intended outputs.  
- [ ] **Outputs** are measurable (numbers, percentages).  
- [ ] **Outcomes** are SMART and aligned with the funder’s mission.  
- [ ] **Impact** reflects broader community change.  
- [ ] **Connections are clear** (use arrows or numbered steps).  
- [ ] **Language is simple**; avoid jargon.  
- [ ] **Review with stakeholders** (staff, partners) for validation.

## 7. Writing the Evaluation Plan  

### 7.1 Importance of Evaluation  

Funders need evidence that your program works.  A solid evaluation plan shows you:  

* Are measuring progress.  
* Can demonstrate accountability.  
* Will contribute to the field’s knowledge base.  

### 7.2 Key Elements  

| Element | What to Include | Example |
|---------|----------------|---------|
| **Evaluation Questions** | What you want to know (e.g., Did scores improve?). | “Did Math Boost increase students’ math scores by ≥ 15 %?” |
| **Data Sources** | Where you’ll get data (surveys, tests, observations). | Pre‑/post‑standardized math tests, student surveys. |
| **Methods** | How you’ll collect data (instrument, timing). | Standardized tests administered at baseline and 12 months. |
| **Timeline** | When each data point will be gathered. | Baseline (Month 0), Mid‑point (Month 6), Endline (Month 12). |
| **Sample Size & Selection** | Who will be measured (e.g., all participants). | All 120 enrolled students. |
| **Data Analysis Plan** | How you’ll analyze (statistical tests, qualitative coding). | Paired t‑test for pre/post scores; thematic analysis of student focus groups. |
| **Reporting Schedule** | How often you’ll share results with funder. | Quarterly progress reports; final evaluation report at project end. |
| **Evaluation Team** | Who will conduct the evaluation (internal, external). | External evaluator hired for independence. |
| **Budget for Evaluation** | Costs for data collection, analysis, reporting. | $7,500 (see budget section). |

### 7.3 Example Evaluation Plan (excerpt)  

> **Evaluation Questions**  
> 1. Did Math Boost increase participants’ math proficiency by at least 15 % over 12 months?  
> 2. What were participants’ perceptions of tutoring effectiveness?  
> 3. How did the program affect academic confidence and attitudes toward school?  
>   
> **Data Sources & Methods**  
> - **Quantitative:** Standardized math assessments (baseline & 12‑month).  
> - **Qualitative:** Semi‑structured student interviews (n = 30) at project end.  
>   
> **Timeline**  
> - **Month 0:** Baseline testing & survey distribution.  
> - **Month 6:** Mid‑point check‑in (optional).  
> - **Month 12:** Endline testing & final interviews.  
>   
> **Analysis**  
> - Paired t‑tests for math score changes.  
> - Thematic coding of interview transcripts using NVivo.  
>   
> **Reporting**  
> - Quarterly progress reports to the funder.  
> - Final evaluation report (including findings, lessons learned, recommendations) due 30 days after project close.  

### 7.4 Checklist for Evaluation Plan  

- [ ] **Evaluation questions** are aligned with project goals.  
- [ ] **Data sources** are appropriate and feasible.  
- [ ] **Methods** are clearly described (instrument, administration).  
- [ ] **Timeline** is realistic and includes milestones.  
- [ ] **Sample size** is justified (power analysis if possible).  
- [ ] **Analysis plan** is detailed enough for an external reviewer.  
- [ ] **Reporting schedule** matches funder requirements.  
- [ ] **Evaluation team** is identified (internal lead, external consultant).  
- [ ] **Budget** for evaluation is included and justified.

## 8. Preparing the Application Package  

### 8.1 What Goes Where?  

| Document | Purpose | Tips |
|----------|---------|------|
| **Cover Letter** | Personalized introduction; highlight alignment. | Address the program officer by name; reference a recent grant or news article. |
| **Executive Summary** | 1‑page snapshot of the entire proposal. | Write after the full draft; keep it ≤ 500 words. |
| **Project Narrative** | Detailed story (see Section 4). | Use headings for each major section. |
| **Budget & Budget Narrative** | Financial plan (see Section 5). | Include a one‑sentence explanation for each line item. |
| **Logic Model** | Visual roadmap (see Section 6). | Provide as a separate PDF or embedded within narrative. |
| **Evaluation Plan** | How you’ll measure success (see Section 7). | Attach as its own document. |
| **Organizational Profile** | IRS status, 3‑year history, staff credentials. | Include annual report or brochure if available. |
| **Letters of Support** | Confirm partnerships and in‑kind contributions. | Get signatures well in advance; attach PDFs. |
| **Resume/CV** | Key staff qualifications. | Highlight relevant experience. |

### 8.2 Checklist for Application Package  

- [ ] **All required documents** are present and labeled correctly.  
- [ ] **Cover letter** is personalized and concise.  
- [ ] **Executive summary** is written after the full draft.  
- [ ] **Narrative** follows a logical flow; headings are consistent.  
- [ ] **Budget** includes line‑item explanations.  
- [ ] **Logic model** is clear and includes all components.  
- [ ] **Evaluation plan** meets funder’s specific requirements.  
- [ ] **Organizational profile** includes latest audited financial statements (if requested).  
- [ ] **Letters of support** are signed, dated, and scanned.  
- [ ] **Proofread** for grammar, spelling, and formatting errors.

## 9. Post‑Award Management  

### 9.1 Why Post‑Award Matters  

Even after the check clears, the work is far from over.  Effective post‑award management ensures:  

* Compliance with funder requirements.  
* Accurate reporting and continued funding.  
* Learning and improvement for future proposals.  

### 9.2 Core Responsibilities  

| Responsibility | Description | Tools/Examples |
|----------------|-------------|----------------|
| **Project Management** | Track milestones, budgets, and staff time. | Gantt charts, Excel trackers, Asana. |
| **Financial Management** | Reconcile expenditures, maintain receipts, prepare reports. | QuickBooks, funder‑specific financial templates. |
| **Reporting** | Submit progress, interim, and final reports on schedule. | Online portals (e.g., Grants.gov), PDF templates. |
| **Compliance** | Follow funder‑specific rules (e.g., sub‑grant policies, audit requirements). | Compliance calendars, checklists. |
| **Communication** | Keep funder informed (monthly updates, newsletters). | Email newsletters, phone calls. |
| **Evaluation & Learning** | Use data to improve program; document lessons. | Evaluation dashboards, meeting minutes. |

### 9.3 Example Reporting Template (Progress Report)  

```
Project: Math Boost After‑School Tutoring Program
Funding Period: Jan 1 2025 – Dec 31 2026
Report Period: July 1 2025 – Sept 30 2025

1. Project Activities & Accomplishments
   - Conducted 120 tutoring sessions (target: 120).
   - Trained 5 volunteer tutors (target: 5).
   - Administered pre‑test to all 120 students.

2. Budget Status
   - Personnel expenditures: $22,500 (90% of annual allocation).
   - Materials: $600 (curriculum updates).
   - Total spent: $23,100 (78% of budgeted).

3. Challenges & Mitigations
   - School facility scheduling conflicts → negotiated alternate dates.

4. Next Steps (Oct 2025–Dec 2025)
   - Begin mid‑year assessment.
   - Recruit additional volunteer tutors.

5. Evaluation Highlights
   - Preliminary analysis shows 8% gain in math scores among 30 students tested.
```

### 9.4 Checklist for Post‑Award Reporting  

- [ ] **Review funder’s reporting calendar** (due dates, formats).  
- [ ] **Gather all required data** (attendance, financials, evaluation results).  
- [ ] **Complete the report** using the funder’s template.  
- [ ] **Proofread** for accuracy and compliance.  
- [ ] **Attach supporting documents** (receipts, letters, evaluation summaries).  
- [ ] **Submit on time** (use email + confirmation receipt).  
- [ ] **Keep a record** of all communications and submitted files.  
- [ ] **Monitor budget variances** and note any over‑runs/under‑runs.

## 10. Conclusion  

Grant writing is a marathon, not a sprint.  Success hinges on a disciplined, repeatable process that blends thorough research, clear storytelling, meticulous budgeting, logical planning, and rigorous evaluation.  By following the checklists and examples above, nonprofit leaders can craft proposals that not only meet funder criteria but also tell a compelling story of impact.  

> **Key Takeaway:**  Treat each grant cycle as a project with its own timeline, deliverables, and stakeholders.  Document everything, stay organized, and continuously refine your approach based on feedback and results.
