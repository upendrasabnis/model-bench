## Introduction  

Grant compliance is the backbone of trustworthy relationships between funders and grantees. Whether you receive a multi‑million‑dollar award from a federal agency, a philanthropic foundation, or a state grant program, you are bound by a set of contractual obligations that ensure public or private resources are used appropriately, transparently, and in line with the grant’s objectives.  

When a grant’s obligations are fully satisfied, the funder may issue an **acquittal**—a formal release that confirms the grantee has met all requirements and that no further liabilities or obligations remain. An acquittal report is more than a “thank you”; it is a legal and financial document that can protect the grantee from future claims, facilitate future funding, and close the financial books.  

This article walks you through:  

1. **What acquittal reporting is** and why it matters.  
2. **Common requirements** across government and philanthropic funders.  
3. **Record‑keeping best practices** that support compliance.  
4. **Audit preparation and response** strategies.  
5. **The most frequent compliance failures** and how to avoid them.

## 1. Understanding Acquittal Reporting  

### 1.1 Definition and Purpose  

**Acquittal reporting** is the formal process by which a grantee demonstrates that it has completed all obligations attached to a grant award. The acquittal report typically includes:  

- A **final financial status** (expenditures, unobligated balances, and any required matching contributions).  
- **Compliance certifications** that the grant was used in accordance with the award terms, applicable laws, and regulations.  
- **Project completion documentation** (final deliverables, evaluation results, and any required close‑out reports).  

The purpose of acquittal is threefold:  

1. **Legal closure** – It releases the grantee from further liability under the grant agreement.  
2. **Financial closure** – It allows the funder to reconcile its records and close the award in its system.  
3. **Transparency** – It provides a public record (especially for government grants) that funds were used appropriately.  

### 1.2 How Acquittal Differs from Other Reports  

| Report Type | Focus | Timing | Typical Content |
|-------------|-------|--------|-----------------|
| **Progress Report** | Ongoing project status | Quarterly / semi‑annual | Milestones, interim financials, challenges |
| **Financial Report** | Expenditure verification | Monthly / as defined | Budget vs. actual, supporting docs |
| **Final Report** | Overall project outcomes | At project end | Lessons learned, impact assessment |
| **Acquittal Report** | **Full compliance closure** | **After all obligations met** | **Final financial status, certifications, close‑out docs** |

While a final report may summarize outcomes, an acquittal report **must** contain the funder‑specific certifications and often a **formal sign‑off** from senior management.  

### 1.3 Real‑World Example: NIH Acquittal  

In 2018, the **National Institutes of Health (NIH)** issued an acquittal for a $12 million cancer‑research grant awarded to **Stanford University**. The acquittal package included:  

- A **certification of compliance** signed by Stanford’s Chief Financial Officer.  
- A **final financial status** showing $11.8 million expended, $200 k unobligated, and full matching contributions.  
- **Project completion documents** such as the peer‑reviewed manuscript collection and a data‑management plan.  

The acquittal allowed Stanford to **close the grant in NIH’s REPORTER system** and **avoid any post‑award audits** that could have been triggered by unresolved obligations.

## 2. Common Requirements Across Government and Philanthropic Funders  

While each funder tailors its requirements, there are **core compliance elements** that appear in most grant agreements.  

### 2.1 Financial Compliance  

| Requirement | Government (e.g., NSF, NIH) | Philanthropy (e.g., Gates Foundation) |
|-------------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Allowable Costs** | Direct costs must be reasonable, allocable, and necessary. Indirect costs must be negotiated and approved. | Similar rules; “reasonable and necessary” language is common. |
| **Budget Adherence** | Grantees must stay within the approved budget, with prior written approval for any modifications. | Budget modifications often require a **budget revision** and funder sign‑off. |
| **Matching/Cost‑Sharing** | Federal grants frequently require a matching contribution (often 1:1). | Many foundations require a **matching commitment** or **in‑kind contribution** but may be more flexible. |

### 2.2 Reporting Requirements  

- **Progress Reports** – Frequency and format vary (e.g., NIH’s **Progress Report (PHS396)**).  
- **Financial Reports** – Typically quarterly or annual; government grants often use **FS-1** or **SF‑425** forms.  
- **Final/Acquittal Reports** – Must include the **certification of compliance** and **final financial status**.  

### 2.3 Subaward Management  

Both government and philanthropic funders require **subaward (or pass‑through) compliance**:  

- **Written subaward agreements** that mirror the prime award’s terms.  
- **Subawardee reporting** – subawardees must submit **financial and progress reports** to the grantee, which then aggregates them.  
- **Monitoring** – Grantees must verify subawardees’ compliance and may conduct **site visits** or **audits**.  

### 2.4 Intellectual Property (IP) and Data Management  

- **Government grants** (e.g., NIH, NSF) include **IP provisions** that give the government a **non‑exclusive, royalty‑free license** for the funded invention.  
- **Philanthropic grants** often require **open‑access** or **data sharing** clauses, especially for global health or education initiatives.  

### 2.5 Real‑World Example: Gates Foundation Requirements  

The **Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation** mandates a **“Compliance and Ethics Handbook”** for all grantees. Key requirements include:  

- **Annual certification** that the grantee has implemented anti‑corruption policies.  
- **Third‑party monitoring** for high‑risk countries.  
- **Open‑data licensing** for any research outputs funded by the foundation.  

A 2021 Gates‑funded malaria‑prevention project in **Uganda** had to submit a **detailed acquittal report** that included a **certificate of compliance** from the Ugandan Ministry of Health, confirming that all funds were disbursed according to local procurement laws.

## 3. Record‑Keeping Essentials  

### 3.1 The Documentation Hierarchy  

| Level | Description | Example |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Master File** | Central repository for all grant‑related documents (award letters, agreements, certifications). | Cloud‑based folder in **Box** or **SharePoint** with role‑based access. |
| **Financial Records** | All transactions related to the grant: invoices, receipts, bank statements, check registers. | **QuickBooks** export with grant‑code tagging. |
| **Time & Attendance** | Employee effort logs that allocate salary and fringe benefits to the grant. | **BambooHR** or **Homebase** timesheets with grant codes. |
| **Supporting Documentation** | Contracts, subaward agreements, procurement documents, audit findings. | PDFs stored with **grant‑specific subfolders**. |
| **Compliance Documentation** | Certifications, training records, conflict‑of‑interest disclosures. | **DocuSign** signed agreements stored in compliance folder. |

### 3.2 Financial Record‑Keeping Best Practices  

1. **Separate Bank Accounts** – Many grantees maintain a **dedicated grant account** to simplify tracing expenditures.  
2. **Chart of Accounts (COA)** – Use a COA that **links each expense category** to the specific grant(s) it supports. This makes **reconciliation** straightforward.  
3. **Invoice Matching** – Implement a **three‑way match** (purchase order, receipt, invoice) before payment.  
4. **Retention Period** – Federal regulations typically require **retain records for at least three years** after the grant’s acquittal. Philanthropic funders may require **five years**.  

### 3.3 Time & Attendance Records  

- **Salary Allocation** – Use **percentage of effort** for each employee on the grant, verified by a supervisor.  
- ** Fringe Benefits** – Ensure fringe costs are calculated consistently with the grant’s negotiated indirect cost rate.  

### 3.4 Digital vs. Physical Storage  

- **Digital** is preferred for **searchability** and **remote access**. Use **version control** and **audit trails**.  
- **Physical** documents (e.g., original signed contracts) can be scanned and stored as **PDF surrogates**; keep the originals in a **climate‑controlled** environment.  

### 3.5 Real‑World Example: NSF Audit of a University Research Center  

In 2020, the **National Science Foundation (NSF)** conducted a **financial compliance audit** of a university’s **Center for Advanced Materials** that was administering a $15 million grant. The audit highlighted that the center’s **centralized grant management system** (a cloud‑based **Grant Management Platform**) enabled:  

- **Instant retrieval** of any grant‑related invoice for the auditors.  
- **Automated reconciliation** of subaward payments.  
- **Clear audit trail** of who approved each expense.  

Because of the robust record‑keeping infrastructure, the university resolved all audit findings within **30 days**, avoiding any financial penalties.

## 4. Audits: Preparation and Response  

### 4.1 Types of Audits  

| Audit Type | Scope | Typical Triggers |
|------------|-------|------------------|
| **Financial Audit** | Verifies that expenditures comply with GAAP and grant terms. | Random selection, high‑risk grants, funder suspicion. |
| **Compliance Audit** | Focuses on regulatory adherence (e.g., procurement, subaward reporting). | Non‑compliance findings from previous audits. |
| **Programmatic Audit** | Assesses whether the grant achieved its intended outcomes. | Funders wanting to evaluate impact. |
| **Internal Audit** | Self‑assessment performed by the grantee to preempt external findings. | Ongoing quality‑control process. |

### 4.2 Internal Controls to Support Audits  

- **Segregation of Duties** – No single person should both approve and record a transaction.  
- **Periodic Reconciliations** – Monthly bank reconciliations, quarterly grant‑budget reconciliations.  
- **Access Controls** – Limit system access to only those who need it; maintain logs of who accessed or edited records.  
- **Policy Manuals** – Document procurement, conflict‑of‑interest, and subaward policies; distribute to all staff.  

### 4.3 Audit Timeline  

1. **Notification** – Funders typically give **30–60 days’ notice** before the audit begins.  
2. **Preparation** – Assemble all requested documents (financial statements, supporting docs, compliance certifications).  
3. **On‑site Visit** – Auditors may interview staff, inspect physical documents, and test internal controls.  
4. **Draft Findings** – Grantee has an opportunity to **comment** on draft findings.  
5. **Final Report** – Includes **material weaknesses**, **questioned costs**, and **recommended corrective actions**.  

### 4.4 Responding to Audit Findings  

- **Acknowledge** each finding and assess its **financial impact**.  
- **Develop a Corrective Action Plan (CAP)** with **responsible parties**, **timelines**, and **evidence of implementation**.  
- **Monitor Progress** – Many funders require **monthly status updates** on CAP implementation.  
- **Closeout** – Once the CAP is fully implemented and verified, the funder may **close the audit** and issue an **acquittal**.  

### 4.5 Real‑World Example: Department of Education Audit  

In 2019, the **U.S. Department of Education** audited a nonprofit’s **$5 million** **STEM education grant**. The audit identified **three material weaknesses**:  

1. **Improper procurement** – Grants funds were used to purchase equipment without competitive bidding.  
2. **Missing subaward documentation** – Two subgrantees’ agreements lacked required clauses.  
3. **Inadequate time‑keeping** – Salary allocations were estimated rather than documented.  

The nonprofit responded with a **comprehensive CAP**:  

- Implemented a **new procurement policy** and trained staff.  
- Retroactively executed **subaward agreements** with all required clauses.  
- Deployed a **time‑keeping system** (web‑based) for all grant‑funded personnel.  

Six months later, the Department of Education **closed the audit** with **no further findings**, and the nonprofit received its **acquittal**.

## 5. Most Frequent Compliance Failures  

### 5.1 Misallocation of Funds  

**What happens:** Grant money is spent on unallowable costs (e.g., personal travel, entertainment) or on a different project than approved.  

**Why it occurs:** Inadequate budget tracking, lack of clear cost allocation policies, or pressure to spend funds quickly.  

**Real‑World Example:** In 2016, the **U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS)** found that a community health organization had used **$250,000** of an HIV‑prevention grant to fund a **staff retreat** at a luxury resort. The organization repaid the amount, paid a civil penalty, and was required to implement a **new cost‑allocation system**.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Use **grant‑specific chart of accounts** to tag every expense.  
- Conduct **monthly budget variance analysis**.  
- Require **receipts and supporting documentation** for every expenditure.  

### 5.2 Late or Incomplete Reporting  

**What happens:** Progress, financial, or acquittal reports are submitted after the deadline or missing required sections.  

**Why it occurs:** Poor project management, insufficient staff, or misunderstanding of reporting cycles.  

**Real‑World Example:** A university’s **research team** missed the **NIH’s quarterly progress report** for a $3 million grant. The NIH placed the award **on hold**, halting disbursement of the next installment. The team had to **retroactively submit** the report, provide an explanation, and faced a **administrative penalty**.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Set **internal reminders** (calendar alerts) for each reporting deadline.  
- Use **grant management software** that automates report generation and reminders.  
- Conduct **quarterly internal audits** to verify report completeness.  

### 5.3 Inadequate Documentation  

**What happens:** Financial transactions lack supporting documentation (e.g., missing invoices, unsigned contracts).  

**Why it occurs:** Overreliance on digital shortcuts, loss of physical receipts, or failure to scan documents.  

**Real‑World Example:** A nonprofit’s **grant accountant** claimed **$10,000** for office supplies but only provided a **single receipt** for **$2,000**. The funder’s auditors questioned the remaining **$8,000**, leading to a **questioned cost** and a requirement to **return the funds**.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Implement a **document capture workflow**: scan receipts immediately, upload to the grant folder, and assign a unique reference number.  
- Use **mobile receipt capture apps** (e.g., Expensify) to ensure every expense is captured at the point of purchase.  

### 5.4 Failure to Meet Matching Requirements  

**What happens:** The grantee fails to secure or document the required non‑federal contribution (cash or in‑kind).  

**Why it occurs:** Over‑optimistic matching commitments, difficulty quantifying in‑kind contributions, or loss of partner commitments.  

**Real‑World Example:** A state transportation department’s **$20 million** highway safety grant required a **50 % matching contribution**. The department pledged **$10 million** in-kind labor from a local construction firm, but the firm later withdrew. The grant was placed **in default**, and the department had to **repay the full grant amount** plus interest.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Secure **written matching commitments** before the grant begins.  
- Track **in‑kind contributions** with detailed documentation (timesheets, equipment logs).  
- Conduct **periodic matching verification** (e.g., quarterly) to ensure the contribution remains intact.  

### 5.5 Improper Subaward Management  

**What happens:** Subawardees are not properly vetted, contracts lack required clauses, or subaward reporting is missing.  

**Why it occurs:** Pressure to disburse funds quickly, lack of centralized subaward tracking, or insufficient due‑diligence.  

**Real‑World Example:** A large university awarded **$2 million** in subgrants to external researchers for a climate‑change study. One subgrantee was later found to have **fabricated data**. The university’s audit revealed that the subaward agreement **did not include a data‑integrity clause**. The university faced **reputational damage** and had to **implement a subaward compliance checklist**.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Use a **standardized subaward agreement template** that mirrors the prime award’s compliance terms.  
- Perform **due‑diligence** (financial health, past audit history) on subawardees.  
- Implement a **subaward tracking system** that logs approvals, deliverables, and reporting due dates.  

### 5.6 Conflict‑of‑Interest (COI) Violations  

**What happens:** Grantees fail to disclose personal or organizational relationships that could bias the grant’s outcomes.  

**Why it occurs:** Lack of awareness, informal relationships, or pressure to expedite decisions.  

**Real‑World Example:** A pharmaceutical company’s **clinical trial** grant to a university was later scrutinized when the **principal investigator** failed to disclose that he owned stock in the sponsor. The **U.S. Office of Inspector General** launched an investigation, resulting in **civil penalties** and a requirement to **establish a COI policy**.  

**Prevention Tips:**  

- Require **annual COI disclosures** from all personnel involved in grant activities.  
- Maintain a **central COI registry** that flags any potential conflicts.  
- Provide **training** on COI expectations for staff, faculty, and subawardees.

## 6. Strategies to Avoid Compliance Failures  

### 6.1 Develop a Comprehensive Compliance Framework  

1. **Policy Development** – Write clear, funder‑specific policies covering procurement, subaward management, COI, and record‑keeping.  
2. **Procedure Manuals** – Detail step‑by‑step procedures (e.g., “How to Process a Grant‑Related Invoice”).  
3. **Training Programs** – Conduct **mandatory training** for all staff, faculty, and administrators at onboarding and annually.  
4. **Governance Structure** – Designate a **Compliance Officer** (often the CFO or a senior administrator) to oversee all compliance activities.  

### 6.2 Implement Regular Internal Audits  

- **Monthly**: Reconcile grant bank accounts and budget vs. actual.  
- **Quarterly**: Review subaward agreements, time‑keeping, and progress reports.  
- **Annually**: Conduct a full **self‑assessment audit** covering all compliance domains.  

### 6.3 Leverage Grant Management Software  

Modern **grant management platforms** (e.g., **Grant Management by Tenet**, **FastBridge**, **Neon CRM**) provide:  

- **Automated reminders** for reporting deadlines.  
- **Expense tracking** with grant‑code tagging.  
- **Document repositories** with version control and audit trails.  
- **Compliance dashboards** that highlight missing items.  

### 6.4 Establish Clear Policies and Procedures  

- **Procurement Policy** – Define thresholds for competitive bidding, required documentation, and approval signatures.  
- **Subaward Policy** – Outline due‑diligence, contract templates, and reporting obligations.  
- **COI Policy** – Set disclosure deadlines, review processes, and remediation steps.  
- **Record‑Keeping Policy** – Specify retention periods, storage media, and access controls.  

### 6.5 Real‑World Example: A Successful Nonprofit’s Compliance Program  

**Organization:** **Save the Children International (SCI)** – a large NGO managing over $150 million in annual grants.  

**Key Elements of Their Program:**  

- **Dedicated Compliance Team** – 12 staff members responsible for all grant agreements, audits, and reporting.  
- **Grant Management System (GMS)** – Custom-built using **Microsoft Power Platform**, integrating budget tracking, expense capture, and reporting workflows.  
- **Quarterly Internal Audits** – Performed by an independent internal audit unit, reporting directly to the Board’s Audit Committee.  
- **Annual Compliance Training** – Mandatory e‑learning modules for all staff, with quizzes and certification.  

**Outcomes:**  

- **Zero material audit findings** in the past five years.  
- **Acquittal rate of 98 %** – most grants close without dispute.  
- **Awarded “Best Compliance Program”** by the **International Aid Transparency Initiative (IATI)** in 2022.  

SCI’s program demonstrates that **systematic, well‑resourced compliance** reduces risk, protects reputation, and facilitates smoother grant closeouts.
