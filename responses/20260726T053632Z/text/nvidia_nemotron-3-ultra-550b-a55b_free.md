BEST PRACTICES IN NONPROFIT GRANT WRITING

INTRODUCTION
Grant writing is a strategic process that aligns organizational mission with funder priorities.
A well‑crafted proposal demonstrates credibility, feasibility, and impact.
This guide walks through each stage from funder research to post‑award reporting.
Each section includes concrete examples and practical checklists.

RESEARCHING FUNDERS
Identify funders whose giving history matches your program area.
Use databases such as Foundation Directory Online, GrantStation, and Guidestar.
Review annual reports, 990 forms, and press releases for giving patterns.
Create a funder profile sheet for each prospect.
Funder Profile Sheet Example
- Name: The Greenfield Foundation
- Geographic focus: Midwest United States
- Program interests: Youth education, environmental stewardship
- Average grant size: $25,000‑$75,000
- Application deadline: March 1 and September 1
- Contact person: Ms. Laura Chen, Program Officer
- Preferred format: Online portal, 10‑page narrative max
- Reporting requirements: Quarterly narrative, final financial report
Checklist for Funder Research
- Compile a list of at least 20 potential funders.
- Verify eligibility criteria for each funder.
- Note deadlines and submission methods.
- Rank funders by alignment score (mission fit, grant size, likelihood).
- Schedule introductory meetings or calls where possible.

BUILDING RELATIONSHIPS BEFORE THE ASK
Attend funder‑hosted webinars, site visits, and networking events.
Send a brief “letter of inquiry” (LOI) to gauge interest.
Personalize communications referencing the funder’s recent grants.
Maintain a contact log with dates, topics discussed, and follow‑up actions.
Relationship‑Building Checklist
- Identify 3‑5 key staff at each target foundation.
- Connect on LinkedIn with a personalized note.
- Request a 15‑minute informational call.
- Share a one‑page impact snapshot after each conversation.
- Track all interactions in a CRM or spreadsheet.

CRAFTING A COMPELLING NEEDS STATEMENT
The needs statement frames the problem your project will solve.
Use data, stories, and local context to make the need vivid.
Structure: (1) Scope of the problem, (2) Evidence, (3) Gap in current services, (4) Urgency.
Needs Statement Example
In 2023, 42 % of high‑school seniors in Riverbend County lacked access to college‑readiness counseling (State Dept. of Education). Existing school counselors serve a 350:1 student ratio, leaving 1,200 students without individualized guidance. Without intervention, these students are 30 % less likely to enroll in post‑secondary education within two years of graduation.
Checklist for Needs Statement
- Cite at least three credible data sources.
- Include a brief beneficiary anecdote.
- Quantify the gap (numbers, percentages, dollars).
- Explain why existing resources are insufficient.
- Keep the statement under 500 words.

DEVELOPING CLEAR GOALS, OBJECTIVES, AND OUTCOMES
Goals are broad, long‑term aspirations.
Objectives are SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound.
Outcomes describe the change expected for beneficiaries.
Goal Example: Increase college enrollment among Riverbend County seniors.
Objective Example: By June 2026, provide individualized college‑readiness plans to 800 seniors, achieving a 20 % increase in FAFSA completion rates.
Outcome Example: 600 seniors enroll in a two‑ or four‑year institution within 12 months of graduation.
Checklist for Goals/Objectives/Outcomes
- Write one overarching goal per project.
- Draft 3‑5 SMART objectives.
- Define at least two measurable outcomes per objective.
- Align each objective with funder’s stated priorities.
- Verify that objectives are realistic given staff capacity and budget.

DESIGNING A LOGIC MODEL
A logic model visualizes the theory of change: Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact.
Use a table format for clarity.
Logic Model Example (simplified)
Inputs: $150,000 grant, 2 full‑time counselors, curriculum materials, data‑tracking software.
Activities: Conduct needs assessments, develop individualized plans, host monthly workshops, track FAFSA submissions.
Outputs: 800 individualized plans completed, 12 workshops delivered, 800 FAFSA forms submitted.
Short‑Term Outcomes: 20 % rise in FAFSA completion, 15 % increase in college‑application submissions.
Long‑Term Outcomes: 600 seniors enrolled in post‑secondary education, 10 % reduction in local youth unemployment.
Impact: Strengthened community workforce, higher median household income over 10 years.
Checklist for Logic Model
- List all resources (staff, funds, partners) as Inputs.
- Map each major activity to an Input.
- Quantify Outputs with numbers and dates.
- Connect Outputs to specific Short‑Term Outcomes.
- Show pathway from Short‑Term to Long‑Term Outcomes and Impact.
- Review model with program staff and external evaluator.

BUDGET DEVELOPMENT AND JUSTIFICATION
Budgets must be realistic, detailed, and directly tied to the logic model.
Separate line items into Personnel, Fringe Benefits, Travel, Equipment, Supplies, Contractual, Indirect Costs.
Provide a narrative justification for each major line item.
Budget Example (excerpt)
Personnel
- Program Director (0.5 FTE) – $45,000 – Oversees project, ensures fidelity to logic model.
- College‑Readiness Counselors (2 FTE) – $120,000 – Deliver individualized plans and workshops.
Fringe Benefits (25 % of salaries) – $41,250 – Health, retirement, payroll taxes.
Travel – $5,000 – Counselor travel to 5 high schools (mileage, lodging).
Supplies – $3,000 – Workshop materials, printing of guides.
Contractual – $12,000 – External evaluator for outcome measurement.
Indirect Costs (10 % MTDC) – $22,625 – Administrative overhead per funder policy.
Total Requested – $248,875
Checklist for Budget
- Align every expense with a specific activity in the logic model.
- Use current salary scales and fringe rates.
- Include a contingency line (max 5 % of total) if allowed.
- Verify that indirect cost rate matches funder’s policy.
- Provide a budget narrative for each line item over $5,000.
- Ensure total does not exceed funder’s maximum award.

WRITING THE PROJECT NARRATIVE
Follow the funder’s required sections and page limits.
Typical sections: Executive Summary, Statement of Need, Project Description, Evaluation Plan, Sustainability, Organizational Capacity.
Use headings that mirror the RFP.
Write in active voice, avoid jargon, and keep sentences concise.
Executive Summary Example (150 words)
The Riverbend College‑Readiness Initiative will serve 800 high‑school seniors in Riverbend County over two years. Leveraging two dedicated counselors and a proven curriculum, the program will deliver individualized college‑readiness plans, monthly workshops, and FAFSA assistance. Expected outcomes include a 20 % increase in FAFSA completion and 600 students enrolling in post‑secondary education. The $248,875 request covers personnel, evaluation, and direct service costs. Riverbend Nonprofit has a 15‑year track record of improving youth outcomes, with a 92 % program retention rate.
Checklist for Narrative
- Tailor each section to the funder’s scoring criteria.
- Keep Executive Summary under the word limit.
- Use data from the Needs Statement throughout.
- Reference the Logic Model in the Project Description.
- Include a clear Evaluation Plan with indicators, data sources, and timeline.
- Address sustainability with concrete strategies (e.g., diversified funding, earned income).
- Highlight organizational capacity: staff expertise, board governance, financial health.

EVALUATION PLAN DESIGN
Distinguish between process evaluation (are activities delivered?) and outcome evaluation (did change occur?).
Define indicators, data collection methods, responsible parties, and analysis plan.
Evaluation Plan Example
Indicator: FAFSA completion rate.
Target: 20 % increase over baseline.
Data Source: State FAFSA database, school records.
Collection Frequency: Quarterly.
Responsible: Evaluation Consultant.
Analysis: Compare pre‑ and post‑intervention rates using chi‑square test.
Checklist for Evaluation Plan
- List at least one indicator per objective.
- Specify quantitative and qualitative methods.
- Assign data collection to named staff or consultants.
- Include a timeline with milestones.
- Plan for IRB review if human subjects research involved.
- Budget for evaluation costs in the grant budget.

SUSTAINABILITY AND LEVERAGING RESOURCES
Funders want evidence the program continues after grant ends.
Show diversified revenue streams, in‑kind contributions, and partnership commitments.
Sustainability Strategies Example
- Secure a three‑year commitment from the County School District for counselor space (in‑kind $30,000/yr).
- Launch a “Friends of College Readiness” giving circle targeting $50,000 annually.
- Apply for state workforce development grants in Year 2.
- Develop a fee‑for‑service model for private college‑prep workshops.
Checklist for Sustainability
- Identify at least three post‑grant funding sources.
- Document in‑kind commitments with letters of support.
- Outline a timeline for securing each source.
- Show how program outcomes create a case for ongoing investment.
- Include a sustainability narrative in the proposal.

ORGANIZATIONAL CAPACITY AND GOVERNANCE
Demonstrate that your nonprofit can manage the grant responsibly.
Provide organizational chart, key staff bios, board roster, and recent audit summary.
Capacity Highlights Example
- 15‑year history, $2.3 M annual budget, clean audits for 10 consecutive years.
- Executive Director holds MPA, 12 years nonprofit leadership.
- Finance Director CPA, oversees $1.5 M in federal grants.
- Board includes two former school superintendents and a CFO of a regional bank.
Checklist for Capacity Section
- Attach current 990 and audited financial statements.
- Include bios for all staff named in the budget.
- Show board governance policies (conflict of interest, term limits).
- List any relevant certifications (e.g., Grant Management Certificate).
- Provide evidence of prior successful grant management (e.g., past funder references).

PRE‑SUBMISSION REVIEW AND COMPLIANCE
Create a master checklist that mirrors the RFP requirements.
Conduct internal peer review, finance review, and legal review.
Check formatting, page limits, font size, file type, and naming conventions.
Pre‑Submission Checklist
- All required forms completed and signed.
- Narrative matches page/word limits.
- Budget totals match across narrative, budget spreadsheet, and budget narrative.
- Letters of support attached and dated within 6 months.
- DUNS/UEI number and SAM registration current.
- Conflict‑of‑interest disclosures submitted.
- Electronic submission tested on funder portal (if applicable).
- Backup copy saved in secure cloud storage.

POST‑AWARD MANAGEMENT AND REPORTING
Set up a grant management calendar with all reporting deadlines.
Assign a grant manager responsible for compliance.
Track expenditures against budget monthly.
Collect outcome data per evaluation plan.
Prepare narrative and financial reports per funder schedule.
Post‑Award Checklist
- Kick‑off meeting with funder program officer within 30 days.
- Establish accounting codes for grant‑specific expenses.
- Submit quarterly financial reports on time.
- Submit semi‑annual narrative reports with outcome data.
- Conduct mid‑term evaluation and share findings with funder.
- Request budget modifications in writing before any changes.
- Archive all correspondence, receipts, and data for audit readiness.
- Close‑out: final financial report, final narrative, equipment disposition, and thank‑you letter.

COMMON PITFALLS AND HOW TO AVOID THEM
Pitfall 1: Misaligned funder fit – Avoid by scoring each prospect before investing time.
Pitfall 2: Vague needs statement – Avoid by using local data and beneficiary voices.
Pitfall 3: Over‑ambitious objectives – Avoid by pilot‑testing activities and consulting staff.
Pitfall 4: Budget inconsistencies – Avoid by cross‑checking every line item across documents.
Pitfall 5: Weak evaluation – Avoid by hiring an evaluator early and defining indicators upfront.
Pitfall 6: Missed deadlines – Avoid by building a master calendar with buffer weeks.
Pitfall 7: Poor sustainability plan – Avoid by securing at least one committed partner before submission.
Pitfall 8: Inadequate organizational documentation – Avoid by maintaining an up‑to‑date grant readiness packet.

FINAL THOUGHTS
Grant writing is a cycle of learning, relationship‑building, and disciplined execution.
Invest time in research; a well‑matched funder increases success odds dramatically.
A clear logic model serves as the backbone for narrative, budget, and evaluation.
Rigorous post‑award management protects reputation and opens doors for renewal.
Use the checklists in this guide as living documents—update them after each grant cycle.
Continuous improvement turns occasional wins into a sustainable funding pipeline.
