# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*An in‑depth guide from funder research to post‑award reporting*  

---

## 1. Introduction  

Grant writing is both an art and a science. Successful proposals combine rigorous research, clear storytelling, and meticulous attention to detail. For nonprofits, mastering the grant‑writing process can mean the difference between sustaining programs, expanding impact, and securing a stable financial future.  

This article walks you through every stage of a typical grant‑writing cycle, offering practical checklists, concrete examples, and actionable tips you can apply to any funding opportunity. Whether you are a seasoned grant professional or a volunteer stepping into the role for the first time, the following framework will help you produce compelling, fundable proposals.  

---

## 2. Understanding the Grant Landscape  

### 2.1 Types of Grants  

| Grant Type | Typical Funding Source | Key Characteristics |
|------------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **Program‑Specific** | Foundations, government agencies | Must align with funder’s priority areas |
| **Capacity‑Building** | Community foundations | Supports org‑level infrastructure (staff, systems) |
| **Research/Evaluation** | Universities, think‑tanks | Requires rigorous data collection & analysis |
| **Emergency/Disaster Relief** | Corporate CSR, disaster agencies | Fast‑tracked, short‑term, often unrestricted |
| **Endowment/Annual** | Individuals, trusts | Multi‑year, often with reporting on investment performance |

### 2.2 Funding Cycles  

- **Annual Cycles** – Most foundations release RFP cycles once per year.  
- **Rolling/Continuous Cycles** – Some agencies accept applications year‑round (e.g., United Way).  
- **Seasonal Peaks** – Education grants often open in spring; health grants may peak in fall.  

### 2.3 Legal & Compliance Basics  

- **IRS Status** – Must be a 501(c)(3) or have a fiscal sponsor.  
- **State Registration** – Some states require charitable registration before applying.  
- **Audit Requirements** – Understand the funder’s audit policies (e.g., Uniform Guidance for federal grants).

## 3. Researching Funders  

### 3.1 Where to Find Information  

| Source | What You’ll Learn | How to Access |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|
| **Foundation Websites** | Grant guidelines, priority areas, deadlines | Direct URL, newsletter sign‑up |
| **Foundation Annual Reports** | Strategic focus, recent grant trends | PDF downloads, “About Us” sections |
| **Grant Databases** | Comprehensive listings, past grantees | Candid, Foundation Center (now Candid), GrantStation |
| **IRS Form 990** | Financial health, program spending ratios | IRS.gov searchable database |
| **Local News & Reports** | Community impact, partner organizations | News archives, community newsletters |
| **Social Media** | Recent initiatives, leadership changes | Twitter, LinkedIn, Facebook |

### 3.2 How to Conduct a Deep Dive  

1. **Create a Funder Profile Sheet** – Capture mission, focus areas, geographic scope, grant size, typical award length, reporting frequency.  
2. **Read at Least 3–5 Recent Grants** – Identify language patterns, required attachments, and any “special” requirements (e.g., letters of commitment).  
3. **Note Any Restrictions** – Geographic, demographic, program type, or matching‑gift requirements.  
4. **Map Past Grantees** – Look for organizations with similar missions or overlapping service areas; they may be “preferred” partners.  

### 3.3 Checklist for Funder Research  

- [ ] Mission & priority areas documented  
- [ ] Recent grant guidelines downloaded  
- [ ] 3–5 past awards reviewed & summarized  
- [ ] Restrictions (geographic, demographic, matching) noted  
- [ ] Contact person identified (program officer, director)  
- [ ] IRS 990 and financial health checked  
- [ ] Newsletter or alerts subscribed to for updates

## 4. Defining Your Organization’s Mission & Vision  

A clear, concise mission statement is the foundation of every proposal. It tells funders *why* you exist and *what* you aim to achieve.  

### 4.1 Crafting the Core Statement  

- **Mission:** “To empower underserved youth through after‑school STEM education.”  
- **Vision:** “A community where every child has access to high‑quality STEM learning that prepares them for future careers.”  

### 4.2 Why It Matters  

- **Alignment:** Funders need to see a direct link between your mission and theirs.  
- **Credibility:** Demonstrates focus and strategic thinking.  
- **Impact Measurement:** Provides a north star for outcome indicators.  

### 4.3 Checklist for Mission/Vision  

- [ ] Mission is 1‑sentence, action‑oriented  
- [ ] Vision describes the long‑term desired state  
- [ ] Values are listed (e.g., equity, innovation, collaboration)  
- [ ] Mission aligns with at least two funder priority areas

## 5. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

The needs statement answers the question: *What problem are we solving, and why is it urgent?*  

### 5.1 Core Elements  

| Element | What to Include | Example |
|---------|----------------|---------|
| **Problem Description** | Data, statistics, anecdotes that illustrate the gap | “In City X, 42% of middle‑school students score below grade level in math, and 68% lack access to after‑school tutoring.” |
| **Geographic Scope** | Specific community, neighborhood, or demographic | “Serving the low‑income neighborhoods of Eastside and Riverside districts.” |
| **Population Served** | Age, gender, ethnicity, disability status, etc. | “Children ages 8‑12 from families earning ≤150% of the federal poverty line.” |
| **Impact of the Problem** | Short‑ and long‑term consequences | “Without intervention, these students face a 30% higher dropout rate and limited college‑and‑career pathways.” |
| **Urgency** | Timeline, trends, or external factors | “Statewide education reforms will be implemented next year; we must act now to ensure our students are prepared.” |
| **Evidence of Existing Efforts** | What else is being done, gaps | “Two existing after‑school programs serve 30 students total, leaving a gap of 120 students needing services.” |

### 5.2 Example Needs Statement  

> **Problem:** In our mid‑size city, 38% of elementary students are not proficient in reading by the end of third grade, placing them at a significant academic disadvantage.  
>   
> **Population:** Low‑income, multilingual families in the downtown corridor, where 62% of households earn ≤200% of the federal poverty level.  
>   
> **Impact:** Students who are not reading at grade level are three times more likely to drop out, limiting lifelong economic opportunities.  
>   
> **Urgency:** The district’s new literacy initiative will be fully implemented next school year, and we have identified a pipeline of 150 students who will need supplemental support.  
>   
> **Gap:** Only one existing after‑school program offers reading intervention, serving 40 students; the remaining 110 students lack access.  

### 5.3 Checklist for a Strong Needs Statement  

- [ ] Data sources cited (census, district reports, peer‑reviewed studies)  
- [ ] Specific numbers (percentages, counts) included  
- [ ] Geographic boundaries clearly defined  
- [ ] Demographic details accurate and inclusive  
- [ ] Problem linked to funder’s priority areas  
- [ ] Urgency justified with timeline or trend data  
- [ ] Gap analysis shows need for your organization’s solution

## 6. Developing the Project Description  

The project description outlines *what* you will do, *how* you will do it, and *who* will benefit.  

### 6.1 Key Sections  

1. **Program Overview** – Brief summary of the intervention.  
2. **Goals & Objectives** – SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound) statements.  
3. **Methods/Implementation Plan** – Step‑by‑step activities.  
4. **Target Population** – Detailed demographics and eligibility criteria.  
5. **Evaluation Plan** – How you’ll measure success (see Section 15).  

### 6.2 Example Goal & Objectives  

- **Goal:** Increase reading proficiency among 8‑12‑year‑old students in the target neighborhoods by 25% within 12 months.  
- **Objective 1:** Provide weekly after‑school tutoring sessions to 150 students (30 students per cohort, 5 cohorts).  
- **Objective 2:** Conduct pre‑ and post‑program assessments using a validated reading fluency test.  
- **Objective 3:** Train 5 volunteer tutors on differentiated instruction strategies.  

### 6.3 Checklist for Project Description  

- [ ] Program overview ≤ 2 paragraphs  
- [ ] Goals are SMART and aligned with needs statement  
- [ ] Objectives are measurable and time‑bound  
- [ ] Methods are realistic given staff, budget, and partnerships  
- [ ] Target population description matches needs statement  
- [ ] Evaluation plan outlines data collection, analysis, and reporting

## 7. Building a Strong Logic Model  

A logic model is a visual roadmap that links inputs, activities, outputs, and outcomes. It helps funders see the theory of change and ensures you have a clear measurement strategy.  

### 7.1 Core Components  

| Component | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Inputs** | Resources needed (staff, money, materials) | $75,000 grant, 2 FTE program managers, 10,000 reading kits |
| **Activities** | Specific actions you’ll take | 150 tutoring sessions, 5 tutor trainings, weekly progress meetings |
| **Outputs** | Immediate, tangible products | 150 students served, 5 trained tutors, 10 reading kits distributed |
| **Outcomes** | Short‑ and long‑term changes | 25% increase in reading proficiency, improved school attendance, higher self‑efficacy |
| **Impact** | Broader societal changes | Reduced dropout rates, increased college enrollment in the community |

### 7.2 Sample Logic Model (Textual)  

```
Inputs
 └─ $75,000 grant
 └─ 2 Program Managers (FTE)
 └─ 10,000 reading kits

Activities
 ├─ Conduct 150 after‑school tutoring sessions (15 sessions × 10 students)
 ├─ Deliver 5 tutor training workshops
 ├─ Administer pre/post reading assessments

Outputs
 ├─ 150 students served
 ├─ 5 tutors trained
 └─ 10,000 kits distributed

Outcomes
 ├─ 25% increase in reading proficiency (measured by standardized test)
 ├─ 10% improvement in school attendance
 └─ 80% student satisfaction (survey)

Impact
 ├─ Lower dropout rate in target neighborhoods
 └─ Increased college/career readiness
```

### 7.3 Checklist for Logic Model  

- [ ] Inputs are realistic and fully budgeted  
- [ ] Activities are directly linked to inputs  
- [ ] Outputs are countable and time‑bound  
- [ ] Outcomes are measurable and aligned with goals  
- [ ] Impact statements connect to broader community change  
- [ ] Model is visually clear (use charts, tables, or simple flowcharts)

## 8. Budget Development  

A budget is more than a spreadsheet; it’s a story of how you will allocate resources to achieve your outcomes.  

### 8.1 Distinguishing Direct vs. Indirect Costs  

| Cost Type | Definition | Example |
|-----------|------------|---------|
| **Direct Costs** | Expenses directly tied to project activities | Tutor wages, reading kits, assessment fees |
| **Indirect Costs** | Organizational overhead that supports the project | Rent for program space, utilities, administrative staff time (allocated via a negotiated indirect cost rate) |
| **Capital Expenses** | One‑time purchases (equipment, furniture) | Purchase of 10 new computers for tutoring lab |
| **Travel/Per Diem** | Costs for field visits, conferences | $200 for staff travel to a regional education summit |

### 8.2 Sample Budget Template (Textual)  

```
Project Title: After‑School Reading Intervention
Funding Period: 12 months
Total Requested: $75,000

I. Personnel
   - Program Manager (0.5 FTE) – $30,000
   - Tutor (0.25 FTE) – $15,000
   - Trainer (0.1 FTE) – $7,500
   Subtotal Personnel: $52,500

II. Materials & Supplies
   - Reading kits (150 students × $50) – $7,500
   - Assessment tools – $2,000
   - Office supplies – $500
   Subtotal Supplies: $10,000

III. Indirect Costs (15% of Personnel)
   - Indirect cost allocation – $7,875
   Subtotal Indirect: $7,875

IV. Travel & Training
   - Trainer certification – $2,500
   - Participant travel – $1,125
   Subtotal Travel: $3,625

TOTAL: $75,000
```

### 8.3 Budget Best Practices  

- **Use Standard Categories** – Follow funder’s budget template if provided.  
- **Itemize Clearly** – Each line item should be specific and justified.  
- **Include a Narrative Explanation** – Briefly explain any unusual line items (e.g., in‑kind contributions).  
- **Round Numbers Reasonably** – Avoid $47,332.89; round to nearest dollar or $100.  
- **Separate Direct vs. Indirect** – Many funders have caps on indirect costs.  

### 8.4 Checklist for Budget Development  

- [ ] Budget aligns with logic model inputs  
- [ ] All line items are justified with a brief narrative  
- [ ] Direct costs are itemized; indirect costs are clearly labeled  
- [ ] Budget uses consistent formatting (fonts, column alignment)  
- [ ] Totals match the requested amount  
- [ ] Contingency fund (typically 5‑10%) included if appropriate  
- [ ] In‑kind contributions documented and valued

## 11. Writing the Narrative (Tips & Structure)  

Even with excellent data, the narrative must be engaging.  

### 11.1 Recommended Structure  

1. **Executive Summary** – 2‑3 sentences that capture the problem, solution, and impact.  
2. **Introduction** – Brief context, mission, and why this project matters.  
3. **Needs Assessment** – Data, gap analysis, urgency.  
4. **Program Description** – Goals, methods, timeline (use bullet points for clarity).  
5. **Logic Model** – Visual or textual representation.  
6. **Evaluation Plan** – How you’ll measure outcomes.  
7. **Budget & Timeline** – Concise tables.  
8. **Partnerships** – Letters of support.  
9. **Conclusion** – Recap impact, call to action.  

### 11.2 Writing Style Tips  

- **Use Active Voice** – “We will conduct tutoring sessions” vs. “Tutoring sessions will be conducted.”  
- **Keep Sentences Under 20 Words** – Improves readability.  
- **Incorporate Numbers Early** – “42% of students…” grabs attention.  
- **Avoid Jargon** – If you must use it, define it in plain language.  
- **Storytelling Elements** – Include a brief anecdote of a student who could benefit.  

### 11.3 Example Narrative Opening  

> **Executive Summary**  
> Community Youth Services proposes a 12‑month after‑school reading intervention to increase reading proficiency among 150 low‑income elementary students in the Eastside and Riverside neighborhoods. By delivering weekly tutoring, training volunteer mentors, and providing age‑appropriate reading kits, we aim to raise average reading scores by 25% within one year. This project directly addresses the funding foundation’s priority of improving literacy outcomes for underserved children and aligns with our organization’s mission to empower youth through education.  

### 11.4 Checklist for Narrative Writing  

- [ ] Executive summary is compelling and concise  
- [ ] Each section has a clear heading  
- [ ] Numbers and data are integrated naturally  
- [ ] Active voice dominates the prose  
- [ ] Jargon is defined or avoided  
- [ ] An anecdote or testimonial is included (if appropriate)  
- [ ] Final draft is proofread for grammar, spelling, and consistency

## 12. Reviewing & Editing  

A polished proposal reflects professionalism.  

### 12.1 Peer Review Process  

1. **Internal Review** – Program staff, finance, and senior leadership.  
2. **External Review** – A trusted colleague from another nonprofit or a grant consultant.  
3. **Funder‑Specific Review** – Compare each requirement checklist against the proposal.  

### 12.2 Editing Checklist  

| Category | Questions |
|----------|-----------|
| **Content** | Are all required sections present? Is the needs statement clear and data‑driven? |
| **Clarity** | Are sentences easy to understand? Are acronyms defined on first use? |
| **Consistency** | Are headings, fonts, and numbering uniform? Are numbers aligned? |
| **Compliance** | Have all funder‑specific forms been completed? Are signatures attached? |
| **Proofreading** | No spelling, grammar, or typographical errors? |
| **Formatting** | PDFs are printable, fonts are standard (Arial, Times New Roman), page numbers included. |
| **Budget** | All line items justified? Totals match narrative? |
| **Attachments** | Letters of support, IRS 990, organizational charts are included. |

### 12.3 Final Review Tips  

- **Read Aloud** – Helps catch awkward phrasing.  
- **Use Track Changes** – If editing electronically, keep a record of revisions.  
- **Save Multiple Versions** – Keep a master copy, a “ready‑to‑submit” version, and a backup.

## 13. Submitting the Grant  

Even a great proposal can be disqualified by a missed deadline or formatting error.  

### 13.1 Submission Steps  

1. **Complete Funder’s Application Portal** – Upload PDFs, fill required fields.  
2. **Attach All Required Documents** – Budget narrative, logic model, letters, IRS 990, organizational chart.  
3. **Double‑Check File Naming** – Use consistent naming (e.g., “OrgName_Program_Budget_2025.pdf”).  
4. **Save Confirmation** – Take screenshots of submission confirmation page.  
5. **Follow Up** – Send a thank‑you email within 48 hours (if allowed).  

### 13.2 Common Pitfalls  

- **Late Submission** – Even a few minutes over can disqualify.  
- **Missing Attachments** – E‑signatures not accepted; physical signatures required.  
- **Incorrect File Format** – Some funders require .docx, not .pdf.  
- **Inconsistent Data** – Budget totals not matching narrative.  

### 13.3 Checklist for Submission  

- [ ] Application deadline noted on calendar and met  
- [ ] All required fields completed  
- [ ] All attachments uploaded and named correctly  
- [ ] Budget narrative matches budget table  
- [ ] Letters of support signed and dated  
- [ ] Organization’s IRS 990 and registration documents included  
- [ ] Submission confirmation saved (email + screenshot)  
- [ ] Thank‑you email drafted (optional but recommended)

## 14. Post‑Award Management  

Grant management begins the moment you sign the award letter.  

### 14.1 Core Responsibilities  

| Area | Key Tasks | Frequency |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Compliance** | Follow funder‑specific guidelines (e.g., sub‑grant rules, reporting formats) | Ongoing |
| **Financial Management** | Reconcile expenses, maintain detailed receipts, prepare quarterly reports | Quarterly |
| **Reporting** | Submit progress reports, final reports, any required evaluations | As per schedule |
| **Monitoring & Quality Assurance** | Conduct site visits, check deliverable quality, update risk register | Periodic |
| **Stakeholder Communication** | Keep partners, board, and staff informed of project status | Ongoing |
| **Documentation** | Store all grant-related documents in a secure, searchable system | Ongoing |

### 14.3 Checklist for Post‑Award Management  

- [ ] Grant agreement reviewed and key terms highlighted  
- [ ] Calendar set for all reporting deadlines (progress, financial, final)  
- [ ] Financial tracking system (e.g., QuickBooks, Excel) implemented and reconciled monthly  
- [ ] Sub‑grantees or partners notified of requirements and deliverables  
- [ ] Risk assessment completed (e.g., budget overruns, compliance breaches)  
- [ ] Internal audit trail maintained (receipts, invoices, emails)  
- [ ] Quarterly team meetings scheduled to review status  
- [ ] Final evaluation plan executed and outcomes documented

## 15. Evaluation & Learning  

Evaluation is not a box‑check; it’s a continuous learning loop that improves your program and demonstrates impact to funders.  

### 15.1 Evaluation Framework  

1. **Formative Evaluation** – Ongoing feedback during implementation (e.g., monthly focus groups).  
2. **Summative Evaluation** – End‑of‑project assessment of outcomes vs. objectives.  
3. **Process Evaluation** – How well the program was delivered (did activities run as planned?).  

### 15.2 Data Collection Methods  

| Method | What It Yields | Example |
|--------|----------------|---------|
| **Surveys** | Quantitative attitudes, satisfaction | Student and parent post‑program survey (Likert scale) |
| **Tests/Assessments** | Objective skill measurement | Standardized reading fluency test |
| **Focus Groups** | Qualitative insights, barriers, facilitators | “What worked? What didn’t?” with teachers |
| **Document Review** | Implementation fidelity | Attendance logs, tutor training certificates |
| **Observation** | Real‑time behavior, engagement | Classroom observation checklist |

### 15.3 Example Evaluation Plan (Excerpt)  

```
Goal: Increase reading proficiency by 25% in 12 months.

Indicators
 ├─ Pre‑program average reading score: 62%
 ├─ Post‑program average reading score: ≥77.5%
 ├─ Student attendance rate: ≥90%
 └─ Parent satisfaction: ≥85% positive responses

Data Sources
 ├─ Standardized reading test (pre & post)
 ├─ Monthly attendance logs
 ├─ Parent satisfaction survey (administered post‑program)
 └─ Tutor log of instructional strategies used

Analysis
 ├─ Compare pre/post scores using paired t‑test
 ├─ Calculate attendance percentage per student
 ├─ Summarize survey results with frequencies and percentages

Reporting
 ├─ Quarterly progress report (include updated indicators)
 ├─ Final evaluation report (6 months after project end)
```

### 15.4 Checklist for Evaluation  

- [ ] Evaluation questions align with program goals  
- [ ] Data collection methods are appropriate and feasible  
- [ ] Sample size and participants defined  
- [ ] Ethical considerations (consent, confidentiality) addressed  
- [ ] Data storage and analysis plan documented  
- [ ] Timeline for data collection, analysis, and reporting built into project schedule  
- [ ] Findings are used to improve ongoing activities (formative) and future proposals (summative)

## 16. Conclusion  

Grant writing is a cyclical process that blends research, strategic planning, clear communication, and rigorous management. By following the best‑practice framework outlined above, nonprofits can:  

- **Identify the right funders** early and avoid wasted effort.  
- **Tell a data‑driven, compelling story** that resonates with funders’ priorities.  
- **Present a realistic budget, timeline, and logic model** that inspire confidence.  
- **Secure strong letters of support** that reinforce partnership credibility.  
- **Navigate the post‑award phase** with compliance, reporting, and evaluation excellence.  

Remember that each grant cycle is an opportunity to refine your proposal, deepen your impact, and build lasting relationships with funders. Treat every application as a learning experience, and let the lessons learned feed back into the next cycle.
