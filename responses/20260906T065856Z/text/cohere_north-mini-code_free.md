<a name="why-grant-writing-matters"></a>  
## 1. Why Grant Writing Matters  

Grant writing is more than just filling out forms; it is the **primary conduit** through which nonprofits secure the resources needed to fulfill their missions. A well‑crafted grant proposal can:  

- **Unlock funding** that may represent 30‑80 % of an organization’s annual budget.  
- **Validate your program** in the eyes of donors, partners, and the community.  
- **Drive strategic planning** by forcing you to clarify goals, outcomes, and impact metrics.  
- **Build credibility** that leads to multi‑year funding relationships.  

Conversely, a poorly written grant can waste valuable staff time, damage relationships with funders, and create compliance risks.

<a name="researching-funders"></a>  
## 2. Researching Funders  

Before you even draft a needs statement, you must understand **who** you are approaching and **what** they fund.  

### 2.1 Types of Funders  

| Funder Type | Typical Focus | Funding Size | Application Cycle |
|-------------|---------------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Foundation** (e.g., Ford, Robert Wood Johnson) | Mission‑aligned social issues | $50k‑$5M+ | Annual or bi‑annual |
| **Government** (e.g., HHS, EPA) | Public policy, evidence‑based programs | $25k‑$10M+ | Fixed fiscal year windows |
| **Corporate** (e.g., Pfizer, Coca‑Cola) | Community development, employee volunteerism | $10k‑$500k | Varies, often project‑based |
| **Individual/Endowments** | Personal passions, local causes | $1k‑$100k | Rolling or seasonal |
| **Public Charities** (e.g., United Way) | Broad community impact | $5k‑$250k | Quarterly/annual |

### 2.2 Where to Find Funding Opportunities  

1. **Foundation Directory Online (FDO)** – searchable by mission, geography, grant size.  
2. **GrantStation, Instrumentl, Foundation Center Grantmaker Directory** – alerts and filters.  
3. **Funder websites** – many post calls on their “Grants” or “Funding” pages.  
4. **State and local government portals** – e.g., Grants.gov (federal), CivicEngage (state).  
5. **Professional networks** – Grant Professionals Association (GPA) webinars, LinkedIn groups.  

### 2.3 Research Checklist  

- [ ] **Mission Alignment** – Does the funder’s priority match our program’s goals?  
- [ ] **Geographic Restrictions** – Are we located in the service area?  
- [ ] **Funding Limits** – Minimum/maximum grant size, per‑grant caps.  
- [ ] **Eligibility** – Only 501(c)(3) organizations? Must partner with schools?  
- [ ] **Application Deadlines** – Note exact date/time zones; set calendar alerts.  
- [ ] **Required Documents** – Budget, logic model, evaluation plan, organizational profile.  
- [ ] **Reporting Frequency** – Quarterly, semi‑annual, annual?  
- [ ] **Contact Person** – Identify program officer; note any specific email address.  
- [ ] **Previous Grants** – Review funded projects to understand tone and format preferences.  
- [ ] **Special Requirements** – Diversity statements, sustainability plans, matching gifts.  

### 2.4 Real‑World Example  

**Organization:** *Youth Futures Initiative (YFI)*, a small nonprofit serving at‑risk teens in a mid‑size city.  

**Goal:** Secure $250k to expand after‑school mentorship program.  

**Research Steps:**  

1. Used **Foundation Directory** to filter for “Youth Development – Urban” foundations.  
2. Identified **The Collins Foundation** – mission aligned, $200k‑$500k grants, annual deadline.  
3. Downloaded Collins’ **previous grant reports** (available on their website).  
4. Noted they preferred **logic models** and **outcome metrics** tied to school attendance.  
5. Saved the **contact officer’s email** and observed they responded within 5 business days to inquiries.  

**Result:** YFI tailored its proposal to Collins’ preferred format, included a robust logic model, and received a $250k three‑year grant.

## 3. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

The needs statement is the **opening narrative** that convinces reviewers that your project addresses a critical gap. It should be data‑driven, emotionally resonant, and specific.  

### 3.1 Core Elements  

| Element | What to Include | Why It Matters |
|---------|----------------|----------------|
| **Context** | Brief description of the community or issue landscape. | Sets the stage for the problem. |
| **Data** | Statistics, census data, local health department reports, or reputable surveys. | Provides objective evidence of need. |
| **Impact** | How the problem manifests (e.g., dropout rates, health outcomes). | Shows the human cost. |
| **Gap Analysis** | What currently exists vs. what is missing. | Highlights the opportunity for your program. |
| **Urgency** | Timeline, worsening trends, or upcoming deadlines (e.g., school year start). | Creates a sense of immediacy. |
| **Vision of Change** | What success looks like after the grant is implemented. | Gives readers a clear endpoint to aim for. |

### 3.2 Balancing Data and Storytelling  

- **Data**: Use **hard numbers** (e.g., “30 % of students in our target ZIP code drop out before graduation”).  
- **Storytelling**: Pair data with a **real‑life example** (e.g., “Maria, a 16‑year‑old mother, left school last year because of lack of mentorship”).  

Combine them: *“In 2023, the local school district reported a 28 % absenteeism rate among 9th‑12th graders. Among them, 42 % cited lack of adult role models as the primary reason. Consider the case of Jamal, a 17‑year‑old who missed 45 days of school last year, ultimately dropping out. Without intervention, Jamal’s trajectory mirrors that of 1 in 4 students in our community.”*  

### 3.3 Sample Needs Statement  

> **Community Need:** In the Riverbend district, 22 % of children ages 6‑12 live below the federal poverty line, and local health surveys show that 35 % of these children exhibit delayed literacy development compared to the state average of 18 %. The nearest public library branch is 3 miles away, and the existing after‑school program serves only 40 % of at‑risk youth.  
>   
> **Impact:** Without accessible literacy support, these children are 2.5× more likely to repeat a grade, leading to higher dropout rates and reduced lifelong earning potential.  
>   
> **Gap:** While two private tutoring centers operate in the area, they charge fees that exceed family budgets, leaving a void for free, evidence‑based literacy programming.  
>   
> **Urgency:** The district’s funding cycle ends in June, and the school year begins in August; delays would miss the critical intervention window for 150 students.  
>   
> **Vision:** By implementing the “Read Together” program, we will increase literacy scores by 20 % within one year, reducing grade repetition by 15 % and preparing students for academic success.  

### 3.4 Needs Statement Checklist  

- [ ] **Clear problem identification** – “We serve X population with Y issue.”  
- [ ] **Quantitative data** – Use at least two reputable sources.  
- [ ] **Qualitative illustration** – Include a short anecdote or case study.  
- [ ] **Geographic/temporal scope** – Define who is affected and where/when.  
- [ ] **Evidence of existing services** – Show what already exists and its limitations.  
- [ ] **Urgency marker** – “Immediate,” “critical,” “time‑sensitive.”  
- [ ] **Desired outcome** – Specific, measurable goal that aligns with the grant’s purpose.

<a name="developing-the-proposal-narrative"></a>  
## 4. Developing the Proposal Narrative  

The narrative tells the **story of your solution**. It should be organized, concise, and aligned with funder priorities.  

### 4.1 Typical Narrative Sections  

1. **Program Description** – What you will do, for whom, and why.  
2. **Goals & Objectives** – Broad mission‑level goals and SMART objectives.  
3. **Approach/Methodology** – Step‑by‑step plan, including staffing and timeline.  
4. **Partnerships & Collaboration** – Existing relationships that strengthen implementation.  
5. **Sustainability Plan** – How the program will continue after grant ends.  

### 4.2 Narrative Writing Tips  

- **Lead with impact** – Start each section with the intended outcome.  
- **Use active voice** – “We will train 30 teachers” vs. “Training will be provided to teachers.”  
- **Be specific** – Avoid vague statements like “improve community health.” Instead, “reduce childhood obesity rates by 5 % in our target schools within 12 months.”  
- **Align with funder language** – Mirror the funder’s priority words (e.g., “equity,” “outcomes,” “scalable”).  

### 4.3 Narrative Example  

> **Program Description**  
> The “Read Together” program will provide free, evidence‑based literacy interventions to 150 children ages 6‑12 in the Riverbend district. Sessions will be held after school (Monday–Thursday) at the community center.  
>   
> **Goals & Objectives**  
> - **Goal:** Increase literacy proficiency by 20 % within one year.  
> - **Objective 1:** Conduct 120 small‑group reading workshops (SMART: 10 workshops/month).  
> - **Objective 2:** Train 5 volunteer reading mentors (SMART: complete certification by month 3).  
> - **Objective 3:** Achieve a 15 % reduction in grade‑level retention among participants (measured via school records).  
>   
> **Approach/Methodology**  
> 1. **Needs Assessment** (Week 1) – Survey teachers, administer baseline reading assessments.  
> 2. **Curriculum Selection** – Adopt the “Literacy Lab” framework (research‑backed, aligned with state standards).  
> 3. **Staffing** – Hire a certified reading specialist ($45k) and part‑time assistants ($15k).  
> 4. **Implementation** – 30‑minute literacy blocks + 15‑minute vocabulary games.  
> 5. **Monitoring** – Weekly attendance logs, monthly progress reports.  
>   
> **Partnerships**  
> We will collaborate with the Riverbend Public Library (provides meeting space) and the local school district’s reading coaches (curriculum review).  
>   
> **Sustainability**  
> After the grant, we plan to transition to a “membership model” ($50 per family) and seek ongoing municipal funding to maintain the program.  

### 4.4 Narrative Checklist  

- [ ] **Section headings** match funder’s required format.  
- [ ] **Program description** includes target population, geographic area, and service delivery method.  
- [ ] **Goals & objectives** are SMART and linked to the funder’s priority metrics.  
- [ ] **Approach** is detailed enough to demonstrate feasibility but not overly bureaucratic.  
- [ ] **Partnerships** are described with clear roles and MoUs if applicable.  
- [ ] **Sustainability** outlines revenue streams, community ownership, and scalability.

<a name="logic-model-development"></a>  
## 5. Logic Model Development  

A **logic model** visualizes the connection between resources, activities, outputs, and outcomes. It helps reviewers see *how* you will achieve impact.  

### 5.1 Core Components  

| Component | Description | Example (Read Together) |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Inputs** | Resources needed (funding, staff, materials). | $70k grant, 1 reading specialist, 5 mentors, curricula. |
| **Activities** | What you will do (programs, outreach). | 120 reading workshops, 5 mentor trainings, 4 family literacy nights. |
| **Outputs** | Direct, tangible products of activities. | 150 children served, 30 mentors trained, 120 workshop manuals. |
| **Outcomes** | Short‑term, intermediate, long‑term changes. | ↑ literacy scores (short‑term), ↓ grade retention (intermediate), improved college readiness (long‑term). |
| **Impact** | Broader, societal changes (optional). | Increased community literacy rate, reduced dropout rates. |

### 5.2 How to Build a Logic Model  

1. **Start with the End in Mind** – Define the long‑term impact you want.  
2. **Work Backwards** – Identify what outcomes must happen first (intermediate), then what activities produce those outcomes.  
3. **Use a Simple Table or Visual Diagram** – Most funders accept either format.  
4. **Label Clearly** – Use consistent terminology (e.g., “Outputs” column).  
5. **Add Evidence** – Cite research that supports the link between activities and outcomes.  

### 5.3 Logic Model Example (Table)  

| Inputs | Activities | Outputs | Outcomes | Impact |
|--------|------------|---------|----------|--------|
| $70k grant, 1 reading specialist, 5 mentors, Literacy Lab curriculum | • Conduct 120 small‑group reading workshops (10/month) <br>• Train mentors on differentiated instruction <br>• Host 4 family literacy nights | • 150 children participated <br>• 30 mentors certified <br>• 120 workshop kits distributed | **Short‑Term:** <br>• 20 % increase in literacy scores (pre/post assessments) <br>**Intermediate:** <br>• 15 % reduction in grade‑level retention <br>**Long‑Term:** <br>• 10 % rise in high‑school graduation rates among participants | Community literacy rate rises from 68 % to 78 % (district‑wide). |

### 5.4 Logic Model Checklist  

- [ ] **All inputs** are listed with amounts and sources.  
- [ ] **Activities** are realistic given staff and time.  
- [ ] **Outputs** are measurable (numbers, percentages).  
- [ ] **Outcomes** are tiered (short, intermediate, long).  
- [ ] **Assumptions** are noted (e.g., school cooperation).  
- [ ] **Evidence** links each outcome to existing research or best practices.

## 6. Budget Development & Justification  

A budget is a **story of resource allocation**; reviewers need to see that every dollar directly supports the program’s goals.  

### 6.1 Budget Types  

| Type | Description | When to Use |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| **Line‑Item Budget** | Detailed categories (salaries, travel, supplies) with unit costs. | Most foundations; easy to audit. |
| **Functional Budget** | Groups expenses by function (Program Services, Administration, Fundraising). | Large grants, government. |
| **Narrative Budget** | Brief explanation for each line item (often required in the budget narrative). | All funders. |

### 6.2 Core Budget Categories (Typical for a Community Program)  

1. **Personnel** – Salaries, benefits, stipends.  
2. **Travel** – Participant transportation, staff mileage.  
3. **Supplies & Materials** – Books, kits, printing.  
4. **Facility** – Rent, utilities, renovations.  
5. **Equipment** – Computers, audio‑visual gear.  
6. **Evaluation & Monitoring** – Surveys, data analysis software.  
7. **Marketing & Outreach** – Flyers, social media ads.  
8. **Administrative Overhead** – Allocated portion of organization’s indirect costs (if allowed).  

### 6.3 Budget Narrative Tips  

- **Explain the rationale** for each line item (e.g., “We need 5 reading mentors because research shows a 1:5 child‑to‑mentor ratio yields optimal literacy gains”).  
- **Use the same terminology** as the logic model (e.g., “Literacy Lab curriculum $2,500”).  
- **Show cost‑effectiveness** (e.g., “Each workshop costs $125, serving 10 children = $12.50 per child”).  
- **Avoid “nice‑to‑have” items** unless they are essential to program success.  

### 6.4 Sample Budget Table (Read Together)  

| Category | Item | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total | Justification |
|----------|------|----------|-----------|-------|----------------|
| Personnel | Reading Specialist (0.5 FTE) | 1 | $45,000 | $45,000 | Certified specialist required for curriculum delivery |
| Personnel | Mentor Stipend | 5 | $500 | $2,500 | $500 covers training, background check, stipend |
| Supplies | Literacy Lab Curriculum | 1 | $2,500 | $2,500 | Evidence‑based, includes teacher guide & student workbooks |
| Supplies | Books & Reading Kits | 150 | $20 | $3,000 | One kit per child for home practice |
| Facility | Community Center Rental | 12 months | $800/mo | $9,600 | Space for after‑school sessions |
| Equipment | Projector & Screen | 1 | $1,200 | $1,200 | Needed for multimedia literacy activities |
| Travel | Student Transportation | 150 students | $15 | $2,250 | Safe, reliable transport to/from program |
| Evaluation | Survey Software (SurveyMonkey) | 1 year | $300 | $300 | Tracks pre/post literacy assessments |
| Admin Overhead | 10 % of total direct costs | — | — | $6,150 | Indirect costs for organization support |
| **Total** | | | | **$72,950** | |

### 6.5 Budget Checklist  

- [ ] **All line items** have a clear, itemized description.  
- [ ] **Costs are realistic** and based on quotes or prior experience.  
- [ ] **Budget aligns** with the logic model’s inputs.  
- [ ] **Budget narrative** explains each category’s necessity.  
- [ ] **Indirect cost** is disclosed and approved by funder.  
- [ ] **Formatting** matches funder’s template (e.g., two‑decimal places, no abbreviations).

## 7. Timeline & Milestones  

A timeline demonstrates **project management capability** and helps reviewers visualize progress.  

### 7.1 Why a Timeline Matters  

- Shows you can plan and execute within the grant period.  
- Helps with **monitoring** and **reporting** milestones to funders.  
- Provides a **benchmark** for evaluating whether you are on track.  

### 7.2 Typical Timeline Elements  

| Phase | Key Activities | Start | End | Milestones |
|-------|----------------|-------|-----|------------|
| **Month 0‑1** | Needs assessment, stakeholder interviews | Month 0 | Month 1 | Draft needs assessment report |
| **Month 1‑2** | Curriculum selection, mentor recruitment | Month 1 | Month 2 | Signed mentor agreements |
| **Month 2‑3** | Staff training, logistics planning | Month 2 | Month 3 | Completed trainer certification |
| **Month 3‑12** | Program implementation (monthly cycles) | Month 3 | Month 12 | 120 workshops completed |
| **Month 10‑12** | Data collection, analysis | Month 10 | Month 12 | Baseline & endline assessments completed |
| **Month 12‑13** | Reporting, sustainability planning | Month 12 | Month 13 | Final grantee report submitted |

### 7.3 Sample Timeline Graphic (textual representation)  

```
[Month 0]   [Month 1]   [Month 2]   [Month 3]   …   [Month 12]
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|--------|
Needs Assess| Recruit   | Train     | Implement | Report |
Survey     | Mentors   | Staff     | Workshops | Data   |
```

### 7.4 Timeline Checklist  

- [ ] **All major phases** are included (planning, implementation, evaluation, close‑out).  
- [ ] **Milestones** are specific, measurable, and time‑bound.  
- [ ] **Dependencies** are noted (e.g., mentor training must finish before workshops).  
- [ ] **Budget alignment** – Ensure each phase’s costs correspond to timeline.  
- [ ] **Contingency plan** – Note potential delays (e.g., weather, staffing changes).

<a name="evaluation--impact-measurement"></a>  
## 8. Evaluation & Impact Measurement  

Evaluation is the **proof point** that demonstrates your program works.  

### 8.1 SMART Goals  

- **Specific** – What exactly will you change?  
- **Measurable** – How will you quantify it?  
- **Achievable** – Is the goal realistic given resources?  
- **Relevant** – Does it align with funder priorities?  
- **Time‑bound** – When will you achieve it?  

**Example:** *“Increase participants’ literacy scores by 20 % within 12 months.”*  

### 8.2 Indicators & Data Sources  

| Indicator | Data Source | Frequency | Owner |
|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Pre‑post literacy scores | Standardized reading assessments | Baseline, 6‑month, endline | Program evaluator |
| Attendance rate | Attendance logs | Weekly | Program manager |
| Mentor satisfaction | Survey of mentors | Quarterly | HR/Training lead |
| Community perception | Focus groups with parents | End of year | Communications coordinator |
| Retention rate | School district records | Annual (post‑grant) | Data analyst |

### 8.3 Data Collection Methods  

- **Surveys** – Short, Likert‑scale questionnaires.  
- **Tests** – Standardized assessments (e.g., DIBELS, Fountas & Pinnell).  
- **Observations** – Structured rubrics during workshops.  
- **Administrative data** – Attendance, budget spend, participant demographics.  
- **Qualitative** – Interviews, focus groups, case studies.  

### 8.4 Sample Evaluation Plan  

> **Objective:** Demonstrate a 20 % improvement in literacy scores among participants.  
>   
> **Design:** Quasi‑experimental pre‑post design with a comparison group (waiting list).  
>   
> **Sample Size:** 150 participants (target).  
>   
> **Data Collection:**  
> - **Baseline (Month 2):** DIBELS assessment.  
> - **Mid‑point (Month 6):** Short reading comprehension quiz.  
> - **Endline (Month 12):** Full DIBELS battery.  
>   
> **Analysis:** Paired t‑tests to compare pre/post scores; effect size (Cohen’s d) calculated.  
>   
> **Reporting:** Quarterly progress reports to funder; final evaluation report (PDF) within 60 days of grant end.  

### 8.5 Evaluation Checklist  

- [ ] **Evaluation question** directly aligns with program goals.  
- [ ] **Methodology** is appropriate for the question and resources.  
- [ ] **Data sources** are credible and accessible.  
- [ ] **Timeline** for data collection and analysis is realistic.  
- [ ] **Budget** for evaluation is included and justified.  
- [ ] **Reporting plan** matches funder’s requirements (format, frequency).

<a name="post-award-management--reporting"></a>  
## 9. Post‑Award Management & Reporting  

Successful implementation does **not** end with the first disbursement. Ongoing compliance and communication are critical.  

### 9.1 Grant Agreement Essentials  

- **Scope of Work** – What the grant funds (activities, deliverables).  
- **Reporting Requirements** – Frequency, format, content.  
- **Budget Rules** – Allowable vs. unallowable expenses, audit requirements.  
- **Intellectual Property & Publication** – Any restrictions on disseminating results.  
- **Compliance** – Federal regulations (e.g., 2 CFR Part 200), state laws, local policies.  

### 9.2 Reporting Requirements  

| Report Type | When Due | Key Elements |
|-------------|----------|--------------|
| **Quarterly Narrative Report** | End of each fiscal quarter | Progress against milestones, challenges, upcoming activities. |
| **Financial Report** | Usually with quarterly narrative | Expenditures vs. budget, receipted invoices, bank reconciliations. |
| **Mid‑Year Progress Report** | 6 months into grant | Updated logic model, revised timeline if needed. |
| **Final Report** | Within 60‑90 days of grant end | Full evaluation results, lessons learned, sustainability plan. |
| **Special Reports** | As requested (e.g., audit, site visit) | Specific data sets, compliance documentation. |

### 9.3 Financial Management Basics  

- **Separate Grant Bank Account** – Avoid mixing funds with operating account.  
- **Expense Documentation** – Keep original receipts, invoices, and supporting statements.  
- **Budget Transfer Process** – Obtain funder approval before re‑allocating >10 % of a line item.  
- **Monitoring** – Use a spreadsheet to track actuals vs. budget monthly.  
- **Audit Trail** – Maintain a chronological file of all grant‑related documents.  

### 9.4 Communication Best Practices  

- **Proactive Updates** – Send a brief email summarizing quarterly achievements before formal report due date.  
- **Clarify Requests** – If a funder asks for additional data, respond within 5 business days.  
- **Document Decisions** – Keep a log of communications regarding scope changes, budget adjustments, or reporting delays.  

### 9.5 Sample Reporting Calendar (12‑Month Grant)  

| Month | Activity |
|-------|----------|
| 1 | Grant award notification; set up separate bank account. |
| 2 | Submit **Month 1** progress & financial report. |
| 3 | Conduct **Mid‑Year** needs assessment update. |
| 4 | Submit **Quarter 2** reports (narrative + financial). |
| 5 | Prepare **Evaluation Mid‑point** data set. |
| 6 | Submit **Mid‑Year Progress Report**; request any funder feedback. |
| 7 | Submit **Quarter 3** reports. |
| 8 | Conduct **Process Evaluation** (focus groups). |
| 9 | Submit **Quarter 4** reports. |
| 10 | Compile **Final Evaluation** results; draft final narrative. |
| 11 | Finalize **Financial Close‑out** (reconcile final expenditures). |
| 12 | Submit **Final Report** + **Financial Close‑out**; schedule exit interview if required. |

### 9.6 Post‑Award Checklist  

- [ ] **Grant account set up** (bank, sub‑ledger).  
- [ ] **Document repository** created (shared drive, cloud folder).  
- [ ] **Quarterly reporting schedule** established with due dates.  
- [ ] **Budget monitoring** spreadsheet updated monthly.  
- [ ] **Compliance policies** reviewed (e.g., conflict of interest, procurement).  
- [ ] **Stakeholder communications** log maintained.  
- [ ] **Evaluation data** collected and stored securely.  
- [ ] **Final close‑out** tasks completed (final report, financial reconciliation).

## 10. Continuous Improvement & Future Grants  

Even after a grant ends, the work continues through reflection and relationship building.  

### 10.1 Lessons‑Learned Review Process  

1. **Gather Data** – Quantitative results, qualitative feedback, financial variance.  
2. **Facilitate a Workshop** – Involve program staff, evaluators, and senior leadership.  
3. **Identify Successes & Gaps** – Use a “What worked well / What could improve” matrix.  
4. **Create Action Items** – Assign owners, deadlines, and success metrics.  
5. **Integrate Findings** – Update logic model, budget, and timeline for next funding cycle.  

### 10.2 Building Long‑Term Funder Relationships  

- **Thank‑You Notes** – Send personalized letters within 48 hours of award notification.  
- **Progress Updates** – Share newsletters, photos, and impact stories quarterly.  
- **Invite to Events** – Offer funder site visits or program observation sessions.  
- **Co‑Create Future Projects** – Ask if they want to partner on expansion or replication.  
- **Recognition** – Feature funder logo on publications, acknowledge contributions in annual reports.  

### 10.3 Continuous Improvement Checklist  

- [ ] **Post‑grant review** completed within 90 days of close‑out.  
- [ ] **Action plan** documented and shared with team.  
- [ ] **Data repository** archived for future reference.  
- [ ] **Funder relationship** refreshed (thank‑you, updates).  
- [ ] **Program refinements** incorporated into next grant proposal.  
- [ ] **Staff training** conducted on lessons learned.

## 11. Final Checklist – Before You Submit  

| Item | Completed? |
|------|------------|
| [ ] **Funder research** – Mission alignment, deadline, required docs confirmed. |
| [ ] **Needs statement** – Data + story + urgency + vision present. |
| [ ] **Narrative sections** – Program description, goals/objectives, approach, partnerships, sustainability. |
