### Section 1 – Foundations of Grant Compliance & Acquittal Reporting  

1. **Introduction**  
2. Why grant compliance matters for NGOs, researchers, and community groups.  
3. The cost of non‑compliance: funding loss, reputational damage, legal penalties.  
4. Defining “acquittal” in the grant‑making context.  
5. Acquittal = the formal demonstration that funds were spent according to the agreement.  
6. Core purpose: accountability, transparency, and evidence‑based impact reporting.  
7. Two‑step view: (1) financial acquittal, (2) programmatic/outcome acquittal.  
8. Financial acquittal focuses on receipts, invoices, payroll, and bank reconciliations.  
9. Programmatic acquittal ties expenditures to activities, outputs, and outcomes.  
10. Typical acquittal package: financial statements, narrative report, supporting docs.  
11. Who reviews acquittals? Funders’ finance teams, auditors, grant officers.  
12. Timing: interim acquittals (quarterly/bi‑annual) vs. final acquittal (project close).  
13. Interim reports help funders monitor cash flow and adjust future disbursements.  
14. Final acquittal releases any remaining funds and closes the grant record.  
15. Legal basis: grant agreements, terms & conditions, and applicable statutes.  
16. Government funders often reference the Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200) in the U.S.  
17. In the EU, the Financial Regulation and Horizon Europe rules apply.  
18. Philanthropic foundations may follow their own policies but often align with GAAP/IFRS.  
19. Common language: “allowable costs,” “unallowable costs,” “matching funds,” “indirect costs.”  
20. Allowable costs must be necessary, reasonable, and allocable to the project.  
21. Unallowable costs include entertainment, alcohol, fines, and lobbying (unless permitted).  
22. Matching funds demonstrate grantee commitment; they must be verifiable.  
23. Indirect cost rates (facilities & administration) are negotiated or use a de minimis rate.  
24. Record‑keeping is the backbone of a successful acquittal.  
25. Best practice: maintain a dedicated grant folder (physical or digital).  
26. Sub‑folders: budgets, contracts, invoices, payroll, timesheets, correspondence.  
27. Digital naming convention: YYYYMMDD_GrantID_DocumentType_Description.pdf.  
28. Example: 20240315_GRANT123_Invoice_VendorABC_Supplies.pdf.  
29. Retention period: typically 3‑7 years after grant close, per funder policy.  
30. Some federal awards require 3 years; state awards may require 5 years.  
31. Philanthropic funders often ask for 5 years, but check each agreement.  
32. Backup strategy: encrypted cloud storage + offline external drive.  
33. Access controls: limit editing rights to finance officer; grant manager reads‑only.  
34. Audit trail: who uploaded, modified, or deleted each file (system logs).  
35. Regular reconciliation: monthly bank statement vs. grant ledger.  
36. Variance analysis: compare budgeted vs. actual expenses each month.  
37. Document explanations for variances (> 10 % or funder‑specified threshold).  
38. Timesheet best practice: daily entry, supervisor approval, project code linkage.  
39. Use of electronic time‑tracking tools reduces errors and provides audit logs.  
40. Invoices must show: vendor name, date, description, amount, grant reference.

42. Travel claims: itinerary, boarding passes, mileage log, per‑diem rates.  
43. Consultant contracts: scope, deliverables, rate, payment schedule, and IRS Form 1099‑NEC (if applicable).

45. Sub‑recipient risk assessment before issuing funds.  
46. Required sub‑recipient reports: financial statements, progress reports, certifications.  
47. Flow‑down clauses: pass‑through of funder requirements to sub‑awardees.

67. Attach annexes for detailed supporting documents; reference them in the narrative.
