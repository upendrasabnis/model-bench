### 2. Researching Funders – Building a Targeted Funder List  
2.1. Identify mission‑aligned funders  
  - Use GrantStation, Foundation Directory Online, GuideStar, and state grant databases.  
  - Example: “The XYZ Foundation funds youth arts programs in the Midwest.”  
2.2. Map funder priorities to your programs  
  - Create a matrix: Program → Funder Interest → Fit Score (1‑5).  
2.3. Assess funder health and giving history  
  - Check recent awards, grant size, and application success rate.  
2.4. Create a “Funder Scorecard”  
  - Columns: Alignment, Deadline, Required Attachments, Contact Person, Success Tips.  
2.5. Set up a tracking spreadsheet  
  - Columns: Funder Name, Deadline, Status, Notes, Link to RFP.

### 3. Decoding the RFP (Request for Proposal)  
3.1. Read the RFP **twice** – first for overall fit, second for technical requirements.  
3.2. Highlight mandatory vs. optional elements.  
3.3. Create a compliance checklist (see Checklist 3‑A).  
3.3‑A. **Compliance Checklist (RFP)**  
  - ☐ Funding limit requested ≤ allowed amount  
  - ☐ Project period within fiscal year of funder  
  - ☐ Required narrative sections present (e.g., Needs Statement, Budget Narrative)  
  - ☐ Attachments (IRS 501(c)(3) status, audited financials) included  
  - ☐ Page/word limits respected  
  - ☐ Submission portal functional test completed  
3.4. Identify the “story” the funder wants to hear.  
3.5. Draft a “RFP‑to‑Prospect” memo summarizing key requirements.

### 4. Crafting a Compelling **Needs Statement**  
4.1. Why the needs statement matters – it is the “hook.”  
4.2. Structure:  
  - **Context** (broad community data)  
  - **Gap** (what’s missing)  
  - **Impact** (what happens without intervention)  
4.3. Data sources:  
  - Census statistics, local health department reports, school test scores, GIS mapping.  
4.4. Concrete example (Youth After‑School Program):  
  - *“In 2023, 38 % of 8th‑grade students in County X scored below grade‑level in math, a 12 % increase from 2019. Meanwhile, the county’s after‑school program capacity covers only 15 % of eligible students, leaving 2,300 children without structured support.”*  
4.5. Avoid jargon; use vivid, quantifiable language.  
4.6. Checklist for Needs Statement (Checklist 4‑A)  
  - ☐ Starts with a compelling, specific statistic  
  - ☐ Cites at least two credible data sources  
  - ☐ Describes the gap in clear, relatable terms  
  - ☐ Explains the consequences of inaction  
  - ☐ Connects directly to the proposed solution  
  - ☐ Is no longer than the funder’s page limit  

---

### 5. Developing a Logic Model (or Theory of Change)  
5.1. Purpose: Visual roadmap linking inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes.  
5.2. Core components:  
  - **Inputs** (resources, staff, partners)  
  - **Activities** (what you will do)  
  - **Outputs** (tangible deliverables)  
  - **Short‑term outcomes** (knowledge, behavior change)  
  - **Intermediate outcomes** (skill development)  
  - **Long‑term impact** (community health, economic uplift)  
5.3. Example Logic Model (Community Food Bank):  

| Component | Example |
|-----------|---------|
| **Inputs** | $150k grant, 10 staff, 5 volunteer groups, 2 partner farms |
| **Activities** | Purchase bulk produce, run weekly distribution, nutrition education workshops |
| **Outputs** | 12,000 meals distributed, 8 workshops delivered |
| **Short‑term outcomes** | 85 % of participants report increased knowledge of healthy eating |
| **Intermediate outcomes** | 30 % reduction in household food insecurity after 6 months |
| **Long‑term impact** | Improved health indices for 5,000 residents over 3 years |

5.4. Checklist for Logic Model (Checklist 5‑A)  
  - ☐ All components present and labeled clearly  
  - ☐ Flow direction is logical (left‑to‑right or top‑to‑bottom)  
  - ☐ Uses simple, non‑technical language  
  - ☐ Includes measurable indicators for each outcome  
  - ☐ Fits on a single page (or two if required)  
  - ☐ Aligns with funder’s evaluation expectations

### 6. Writing the **Program Description** / Project Narrative  
6.1. Narrative sections most funders require:  
  - **Project Overview** (one‑sentence elevator pitch)  
  - **Goals & Objectives** (SMART)  
  - **Methods/Activities** (step‑by‑step plan)  
  - **Staffing & Governance** (who does what)  
  - **Evaluation Plan** (how you’ll measure success)  
6.2. Writing tips:  
  - Use active verbs (deliver, train, implement).  
  - Keep paragraphs ≤ 3 sentences.  
  - Mirror the funder’s language from the RFP.  
6.3. Example paragraph (Youth Mentoring Initiative):  
  - *“The ‘Future Leaders’ program will pair 150 at‑risk middle‑school students with trained community mentors for a 12‑month curriculum focused on STEM exploration, career readiness, and personal development. Mentors will meet bi‑weekly, delivering hands‑on workshops, job‑shadowing experiences, and monthly progress check‑ins. The program’s primary goal is to increase participants’ high‑school graduation rate by 15 % within two years.”*  
6.4. Checklist for Narrative (Checklist 6‑A)  
  - ☐ Aligns every sentence with a requirement from the RFP  
  - ☐ Includes at least one measurable objective per goal  
  - ☐ Describes each major activity with responsible staff name  
  - ☐ Explains how activities lead to intended outputs and outcomes  
  - ☐ Uses funder‑specific terminology (e.g., “capacity building,” “systems change”)  
  - ☐ Stays within page/word limits

### 7. Budget Development – The Financial Blueprint  
7.1. Budget types most funders accept:  
  - **Line‑item budget** (detailed expenses)  
  - **Summary budget** (high‑level totals)  
7.2. Core budget categories:  
  - Personnel (salaries, benefits)  
  - Supplies & Materials  
  - Equipment (if > $5k, justify)  
  - Travel (purpose‑specific)  
  - Contractual Services (consultants, contractors)  
  - Indirect Costs (overhead, if allowed)  
7.3. Steps to build a budget:  
  - Step 1: List all activities from the narrative.  
  - Step 2: Assign cost drivers (e.g., $45k for 2 full‑time staff).  
  - Step 3: Research market rates (salary surveys, vendor quotes).  
  - Step 4: Add a 5‑10 % contingency for unforeseen costs.  
  - Step 5: Total direct costs, then apply indirect cost rate (if allowed).  
7.4. Budget Narrative Checklist (Checklist 7‑A)  
  - ☐ Each expense line matches a narrative activity  
  - ☐ Costs are realistic and justified with market data  
  - ☐ Personnel costs include salary + fringe rate breakdown  
  - ☐ Travel budget includes purpose, dates, and cost per trip  
  - ☐ Equipment purchases meet funder policy (e.g., < $5k may be exempt)  
  - ☐ Contingency line is clearly labeled and justified  
  - ☐ Indirect cost rate is documented and approved by the board

### 8. Post‑Award Management – From Funding to Reporting  
8.1. Key post‑award tasks:  
  - Execute grant agreement (signatures, attachments).  
  - Set up a grant‑specific accounting code in your financial system.  
  - Establish a monitoring schedule (monthly check‑ins, quarterly reports).  
8.2. Common reporting requirements:  
  - **Progress Reports** (quarterly or semi‑annual)  
  - **Financial Reports** (actual vs. budget)  
  - **Outcome/Evaluation Reports** (data on outcomes)  
  - **Site Visits** (if funder requests)  
8.3. Best‑practice template for a **Quarterly Progress Report**:  

| Section | Content | Frequency |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Executive Summary | Brief overview of activities & milestones | Quarterly |
| Financial Summary | Actual spend vs. budget line items | Quarterly |
| Narrative Update | What was accomplished, any deviations | Quarterly |
| Outcomes & Metrics | Data on short‑term outcomes, any changes | Quarterly |
| Challenges & Solutions | Problems encountered + mitigation steps | Quarterly |
| Upcoming Activities | Planned work for next quarter | Quarterly |
| Attachments | Photos, newsletters, participant stories | Quarterly |

8.4. Checklist for Post‑Award Management (Checklist 8‑A)  
  - ☐ Signed grant agreement filed in a secure, searchable folder  
  - ☐ Accounting code created and mapped to budget categories  
  - ☐ Monthly expense tracking reconciled with budget narrative  
  - ☐ Quarterly report template customized to funder’s specifications  
  - ☐ Data collection tools (surveys, logs) are active and archived  
  - ☐ Evaluation results are documented and shared with the board  
  - ☐ Any required site visits scheduled and documented

### 9. Common Pitfalls & How to Avoid Them  
9.1. Submitting after the deadline (even by a minute).  
9.2. Ignoring “required attachments” – they are often disqualifying.  
9.3. Over‑promising outcomes – funders value realistic, evidence‑based goals.  
9.4. Using generic language that does not tie back to the funder’s priorities.  
9.5. Failing to proofread – typos can appear unprofessional.  
9.6. Neglecting to thank the funder in the acknowledgments section.  
9.7. Skipping the logic model – reviewers often use it to assess feasibility.  
9.8. Budget mismatches – indirect costs not allowed when prohibited.

### 12. Final Tips for a Winning Grant Application  
12.1. Start early – give yourself at least **90 days** before the deadline.  
12.2. Build a “grant team” – assign a lead writer, a finance liaison, and a reviewer.  
12.3. Leverage storytelling – weave a short anecdote that humanizes the data.  
12.4. Align language – mirror the funder’s own words from their mission statement.  
12.5. Proofread twice – once for content, once for grammar/spelling.  
12.6. Submit a test upload – verify formatting and file size.  
12.7. Follow up politely if you haven’t heard back within the stated review period.  
12.8. Maintain relationships – even declined proposals can lead to future opportunities.
