## Why Grant Writing Matters  

Grant writing is far more than a “write‑up” of a project; it is the **primary vehicle** through which nonprofits secure the resources needed to fulfill their missions. A well‑crafted proposal can:  

- **Unlock funding** that fuels program expansion, capacity building, or emergency relief.  
- **Build credibility** with funders, demonstrating that the organization is professional, strategic, and accountable.  
- **Create a roadmap** for the organization, forcing clarity on goals, outcomes, and resource needs.  
- **Facilitate learning** through the evaluation and reporting processes that follow.  

In short, grant writing is the bridge between a community’s needs and the financial support required to address them.

## Phase 1 – Researching Funders  

### Understanding the Funding Landscape  

Before you even draft a single sentence, you must know **who** you are writing to. Funders fall into several categories:  

| Category | Typical Sources | Funding Focus |
|----------|----------------|---------------|
| **Private Foundations** | Ford Foundation, Kellogg Foundation | Mission‑driven, often thematic |
| **Corporate Foundations** | Coca‑Cola HBC Foundation, Google.org | Community investment, CSR |
| **Federal Agencies** | NIH, Department of Education, HUD | Large‑scale, evidence‑based programs |
| **State/Local Governments** | State Arts Councils, City Parks Departments | Regional priorities |
| **Individual Philanthropists** | High‑net‑worth donors, crowdfunding platforms | Personal interests, cause‑specific |

### How to Research Effectively  

1. **Create a Funding Matrix** – List potential funders, their typical grant sizes, eligibility, and deadlines.  
2. **Read the Guidelines Carefully** – Highlight required elements (e.g., “must serve low‑income families,” “requires a matching contribution”).  
3. **Check the Foundation’s Logic** – Look at recent grants they awarded; note trends in program areas, geographies, and approaches.  
4. **Explore the Application Timeline** – Some funders have rolling deadlines; others have a single annual window.  
5. **Identify Contacts** – Many foundations list program officers; reaching out for a brief “pre‑application” conversation can be invaluable.  

### Checklist: Funders Research  

- [ ] **Funding Matrix Completed** – Columns: Funder, Grant Size, Eligibility, Deadline, Contact, Recent Focus Areas.  
- [ ] **Guidelines Summary** – Capture all mandatory and recommended requirements.  
- [ ] **Competitive Landscape Reviewed** – At least 3–5 recent grants from the same funder.  
- [ ] **Mission Alignment Confirmed** – Your organization’s mission matches the funder’s stated priorities.  
- [ ] **Application Timeline Mapped** – Note internal deadlines for drafts, revisions, and final submission.  
- [ ] **Contact Information Collected** – Email/phone of program officer (if available).

## Phase 2 – Crafting the Core Proposal Components  

### 2.1 Needs Statement  

A compelling needs statement does more than list problems; it **tells a story** that positions your organization as the solution.  

#### Key Elements  

| Element | What to Include | Tips |
|---------|----------------|------|
| **Context** | Demographic data, community statistics, trends | Use recent (within 2‑3 years) data from census, health departments, or local surveys. |
| **Gap Analysis** | What currently exists vs. what is needed | Highlight unmet need quantitatively (e.g., “30% of families lack access to after‑school tutoring”). |
| **Impact** | Short‑ and long‑term consequences of the gap | Connect to broader social issues (education, health, economic stability). |
| **Call to Action** | Why your program is the right response | Emphasize your organization’s experience, community trust, and capacity. |
| **Geographic Scope** | Specific area served (city, zip codes, rural vs. urban) | Be precise; funders love clear boundaries. |

#### Checklist: Needs Statement  

- [ ] **Data Sources Cited** – At least two reputable sources (census, local health dept., nonprofit partners). |
- [ ] **Quantifiable Metrics** – Include numbers, percentages, or rates. |
- [ ] **Community Voice** – Quote from stakeholders (parents, teachers, clients) if possible. |
- [ ] **Alignment with Funder Priorities** – Show how the need matches the funder’s focus. |
- [ ] **Clear Geographic Boundaries** – Define service area in map or description. |
- [ ] **Concise & Persuasive** – No more than 1 page; read aloud to ensure flow. |

#### Example Needs Statement  

> *In the city of Riverside, 42% of children in low‑income households score below grade level in reading by third grade, a rate 15% higher than the state average. This literacy gap predicts higher dropout rates, limited college attendance, and reduced lifetime earnings. Without early intervention, these children face a cycle of academic underachievement that undermines community economic stability. Our organization, Riverside Literacy Partners, has demonstrated success in raising reading proficiency by 30% in similar neighborhoods through our after‑school tutoring program. We propose expanding this proven model to serve an additional 150 at‑risk students, directly addressing the city’s identified need for early literacy support.*

### 2.2 Logic Model  

A logic model is the **blueprint** that links resources, activities, outputs, and outcomes. It answers: “If we do X, what will happen?”  

#### Core Components  

1. **Inputs** – Resources (staff, money, materials, partnerships).  
2. **Activities/Strategies** – What the organization will do (e.g., program sessions, outreach).  
3. **Outputs** – Immediate, tangible results (e.g., number of participants, workshops delivered).  
4. **Outcomes** – Short‑term changes (knowledge gained, attitudes shifted).  
5. **Impact** – Long‑term effects (improved graduation rates, reduced poverty).  

#### Checklist: Logic Model  

- [ ] **Inputs Identified** – List all personnel, budget line items, facilities, and partnerships. |
- [ ] **Activities Described** – Specific, measurable actions (e.g., “conduct 12 weekly tutoring sessions”). |
- [ ] **Outputs Quantified** – Numbers (e.g., “serve 150 students”). |
- [ ] **Outcomes Defined** – Use SMART criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound). |
- [ ] **Impact Statement** – Articulate the broader community change. |
- [ ] **Visual Diagram** – Create a simple flowchart for reviewers. |
- [ ] **Alignment with Funder Priorities** – Ensure each outcome maps to a funder’s goal. |

#### Example Logic Model (Narrative)  

> **Inputs:** 2 full‑time tutors, $15,000 budget, partnership with Riverside Public Library.  
>   
> **Activities:** Conduct 12 weekly after‑school reading clubs (2 hours each), administer pre‑/post‑ assessments, provide parental workshops.  
>   
> **Outputs:** 150 student participants, 12 clubs held, 4 parental workshops.  
>   
> **Outcomes:** 30% increase in reading proficiency scores among participants within 6 months. |
>   
> **Impact:** Reduced likelihood of grade‑level retention, increased high‑school graduation rates, and improved long‑term economic prospects for participating families.

### 2.3 Budget Development  

A budget is **more than a spreadsheet**; it tells funders you have realistic expectations about costs and can manage resources responsibly.  

#### Budget Categories to Include  

| Category | Description | Tips |
|----------|-------------|------|
| **Personnel** | Salaries, benefits, stipends | Use market salary surveys; include fringe benefits. |
| **Travel** | Participant or staff travel, mileage | Keep receipts; note purpose. |
| **Materials/Supplies** | Books, art supplies, printed handouts | Itemize; include unit costs. |
| **Facilities** | Rent, utilities, equipment | If in‑kind, note value and justification. |
| **Direct Program Costs** | Costs directly tied to service delivery | Separate from overhead. |
| **Indirect Costs (Overhead)** | Organizational overhead (rent, admin) | Use the organization’s established indirect rate if allowed. |
| **Evaluation & Reporting** | Surveys, data analysis, reporting software | Show commitment to measurement. |
| **Contingency** | 5‑10% of total budget for unexpected expenses | Explain why it’s needed. |

#### Checklist: Budget Development  

- [ ] **All Costs Itemized** – Include line items, unit quantities, unit costs. |
- [ ] **Personnel Salaries Realistic** – Use Form W‑2 data or nonprofit salary surveys. |
- [ ] **In‑Kind Contributions Valued** – Provide documentation (appraisal, receipts). |
- [ ] **Indirect Cost Rate Applied** – If using a federal indirect rate, cite OMB A‑122. |
- [ ] **Budget Narrative** – Explain each line item, why it’s needed, and how it aligns with activities. |
- [ ] **Consistency with Logic Model** – Ensure each activity has a corresponding cost. |
- [ ] **Budget Review** – Have finance director or accountant sign off. |
- [ ] **Formatting** – Use the funder’s template; align columns; include totals. |

#### Example Budget (Simplified)  

| Category | Item | Quantity | Unit Cost | Total |
|----------|------|----------|-----------|-------|
| Personnel | Program Director (0.5 FTE) | 0.5 | $35,000 | $17,500 |
| Personnel | Tutor (2 FTE) | 2 | $22,000 | $44,000 |
| Materials | Books & Reading Packs | 150 kits | $30 | $4,500 |
| Supplies | Art Supplies (for after‑school club) | 1 set | $200 | $200 |
| Facilities | Library Rental (12 sessions) | 12 days | $150 | $1,800 |
| Travel | Participant Mileage | 500 miles | $0.55 | $275 |
| Evaluation | Survey Software License | 1 yr | $120 | $120 |
| Indirect | 10% Overhead | — | — | $6,395 |
| **Total** | | | | **$75,790** |

*Narrative*: “The budget reflects the minimal staff needed to deliver high‑quality tutoring while leveraging the existing library space. All personnel costs are market‑based, and the $75,790 total includes a 10% contingency for unforeseen expenses such as additional materials or emergency facility repairs.”

## Phase 3 – Evaluation & Impact Measurement  

### Why Evaluation Matters  

Funders invest in **evidence**, not just ideas. A robust evaluation plan demonstrates:  

- **Accountability** – You can prove you delivered what you promised.  
- **Learning** – Data informs program improvements.  
- **Scaleability** – Results can be used to attract additional funding.  

### Evaluation Plan Components  

| Component | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| **Logic Model Alignment** | Tie each outcome back to your logic model. | “Increase reading proficiency by 30%” → outcome metric. |
| **Indicators** | What you will measure (quantitative or qualitative). | Pre/post reading scores, participant satisfaction surveys. |
| **Data Sources** | Where data comes from (tests, surveys, administrative records). | Standardized reading assessments, parent interviews. |
| **Timeline** | When data will be collected (baseline, mid‑point, end‑point). | Baseline (Month 0), 6‑month follow‑up, 12‑month follow‑up. |
| **Sample Size & Selection** | Who will be measured; statistical significance. | 150 students, 80% participation rate. |
| **Analysis Plan** | How data will be analyzed (statistical tests, thematic coding). | Paired t‑test for reading scores; content analysis for open‑ended responses. |
| **Reporting Schedule** | Frequency of reports to funder (progress, final). | Quarterly progress report, final impact report. |
| **Evaluation Budget** | Costs for data collection, analysis, external evaluator. | $2,500 for survey platform, $3,000 for external consultant. |

#### Checklist: Evaluation Plan  

- [ ] **Alignment Confirmed** – Each indicator maps to a logic‑model outcome. |
- [ ] **SMART Indicators Defined** – Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time‑bound. |
- [ ] **Data Sources Identified** – Include primary and secondary sources. |
- [ ] **Timeline Documented** – Include collection dates and report due dates. |
- [ ] **Sample Size & Selection Plan** – Include inclusion/exclusion criteria. |
- [ ] **Analysis Methods Outlined** – Statistical or qualitative methods. |
- [ ] **Evaluation Budget Included** – Separate line in overall budget. |
- [ ] **Stakeholder Input Gathered** – Consult program staff, participants, and external experts. |
- [ ] **Compliance Checked** – Ensure IRB approval for human subjects if needed. |
- [ ] **Reporting Templates Prepared** – Draft formats for progress and final reports. |

#### Example Evaluation Plan  

> **Program:** Riverside After‑School Literacy Program  
>   
> **Goal:** Increase reading proficiency among at‑risk elementary students by 30% within 12 months.  
>   
> **Indicators:**  
> 1. **Reading Proficiency Score** – Measured via standardized DIBELS test.  
> 2. **Participant Satisfaction** – 5‑point Likert survey for students and parents.  
> 3. **Attendance Rate** – Number of sessions attended vs. total possible.  
>   
> **Data Sources:**  
> - Pre‑ and post‑DIBELS scores (primary).  
> - End‑of‑program student/parent surveys (secondary).  
> - Program attendance logs (secondary).  
>   
> **Timeline:**  
> - Baseline: Month 0 (before program start).  
> - Mid‑point: Month 6 (progress check).  
> - End‑point: Month 12 (final impact).  
>   
> **Sample Size:** 150 students; target 80% participation (120 students).  
>   
> **Analysis:**  
> - Paired t‑test for reading scores (pre vs. post).  
> - Descriptive statistics for satisfaction (mean, SD).  
> - Correlation analysis between attendance and score improvement.  
>   
> **Reporting:**  
> - Quarterly progress report (attendance, mid‑point scores).  
> - Final impact report (full dataset, lessons learned).  
>   
> **Evaluation Budget:** $2,500 for DIBELS licensing, $1,200 for survey platform, $3,000 for external evaluator → **$6,700** (included in overall budget).

## Phase 4 – Narrative Writing & Positioning  

### Storytelling Techniques  

1. **Open with a Hook** – Start with a vivid anecdote or startling statistic that draws the reader in.  
2. **Establish Credibility Early** – Highlight years of experience, staff qualifications, past successes.  
3. **Use Simple Language** – Avoid jargon; write as if you’re explaining the project to a neighbor.  
4. **Show, Don’t Just Tell** – Provide concrete examples of past impact (“In 2022, we served 200 families, reducing housing instability by 25%”).  
5. **Create a Clear Timeline** – Show when activities will occur, linking to funder’s reporting calendar.  

### Aligning with Funder Priorities  

- **Read the Request for Proposals (RFP) twice.** Underline keywords such as “early childhood,” “equity,” “data‑driven,” “community engagement.”  
- **Map your proposal sections to the RFP sections.** If the RFP has a “Program Design” section, dedicate that part of your narrative to the logic model.  
- **Reference the funder’s mission in your narrative.** “The XYZ Foundation’s commitment to educational equity aligns directly with our goal to…”  

### Checklist: Narrative Writing  

- [ ] **Hook Present** – Opening sentence captures attention. |
- [ ] **Credibility Established** – Include staff qualifications, years in service, prior grants. |
- [ ] **Clear Problem Statement** – Concise, data‑driven needs statement. |
- [ ] **Solution Articulated** – Describe program model and why it’s unique. |
- [ ] **Alignment Highlighted** – Explicitly connect to funder’s priorities. |
- [ ] **Storytelling Elements** – Include client testimonial or case study. |
- [ ] **Logical Flow** – Sections follow RFP order; transitions smooth. |
- [ ] **Tone Consistent** – Professional yet personable. |
- [ ] **Proofreading Done** – Spell check, grammar check, readability score (aim for 8th‑grade level). |
- [ ] **Compliance Verified** – All required attachments included (budget, logic model, evaluation plan). |

## Phase 5 – Review, Edit & Submission  

### Internal Review Process  

1. **Draft Review** – Program staff read for content accuracy.  
2. **Financial Review** – Finance team checks budget calculations and footnotes.  
3. **Legal/Compliance Review** – Ensure EEOC, IRS status, and any required certifications are included.  
4. **Copy‑edit** – Professional editor or senior staff for grammar, style, and formatting.  
5. **Final Sign‑off** – Executive director or board chair signs the cover letter.  

### Compliance Checklist  

- [ ] **Organizational Documents Attached** – IRS 501(c)(3) letter, most recent audited financial statements, conflict of interest policy. |
- [ ] **Required Certifications Included** – Lobbying, single audit (if applicable), drug‑free workplace. |
- [ ] **Budget Narrative Aligned** – Each line item explained. |
- [ ] **Evaluation Plan Meets Funders’ Specs** – Sample size, timeline, reporting format. |
- [ ] **Letters of Support** – At least 3–5 letters from community partners, clients, or stakeholders. |
- [ ] **Organizational Capacity** – Demonstrate staff, facilities, and systems to implement. |
- [ ] **Sustainability Plan** – Post‑grant strategy (e.g., diversification, earned income). |
- [ ] **Timeline & Milestones** – Clearly marked start/end dates and deliverables. |
- [ ] **Contact Information** – Accurate email, phone, and mailing address for the grants team. |
- [ ] **Submission Portal Completion** – Upload all required documents before deadline. |

### Checklist: Submission Preparation  

- [ ] **All Documents Compiled** – PDFs named consistently (e.g., “OrganizationName_Budget.pdf”). |
- [ ] **Cover Letter Personalized** – Address program officer by name, reference specific RFP. |
- [ ] **Deadline Confirmed** – Set calendar reminders for internal due dates. |
- [ ] **Proofreading Completed** – Read aloud; ask a colleague to review. |
- [ ] **Formatting Checked** – Font size 11‑12, 1‑inch margins, page numbers. |
- [ ] **File Size Limits Respected** – If upload limit is 10 MB, compress images if needed. |
- [ ] **Email Confirmation Received** – Save receipt of submission for records. |
- [ ] **Follow‑up Plan Set** – Note when to expect notification (usually 6‑12 weeks). |
- [ ] **Budget Tracking Spreadsheet Updated** – Log each funder’s deadline, amount, and status. |

## Phase 6 – Post‑Award Management & Reporting  

### Reporting Requirements Overview  

| Report Type | Frequency | Key Content |
|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Progress Report** | Quarterly or semi‑annual | Activities completed, budget expenditures, mid‑term outcomes. |
| **Financial Report** | Monthly or quarterly (as per grant) | Expenditures vs. budget, supporting receipts, cash reconciliations. |
| **Evaluation Report** | Mid‑term and final | Full dataset, analysis, lessons learned, recommendations. |
| **Compliance Report** | As required | Documentation of any policy changes, audit results, risk assessments. |
| **Close‑out Report** | At project end | Final expenditures, unused funds, deliverables, sustainability plan. |

### Financial Reporting Basics  

1. **Maintain Separate Grant Accounts** – Use a distinct chart of accounts code for each grant.  
2. **Track Expenditures Accurately** – Reconcile bank statements weekly; use accounting software (e.g., QuickBooks, NetSuite).  
3. **Document In‑Kind Contributions** – Provide valuation and supporting documentation.  
4. **Reimburse Staff Properly** – If staff work partial time on the grant, use time sheets and allocate salary proportionally.  
5. **Prepare Quarterly Reports** – Include a budget vs. actual variance analysis with explanations.  

### Monitoring & Compliance  

- **Risk Assessment** – Identify potential compliance risks (e.g., subgrant agreements, procurement thresholds).  
- **Internal Controls** – Segregation of duties, approval hierarchies, and regular audits.  
- **Grant Management Software** – Tools like GrantManagement, Logos, or Fluxx help track deadlines, reports, and budgets.  
- **Communication with Funder** – Schedule a mid‑project call; keep the funder informed of challenges and successes.  

### Checklist: Post‑Award Reporting  

- [ ] **Report Due Dates Logged** – Calendar alerts set for each report. |
- [ ] **Data Collected** – Ensure all required metrics are captured (activities, outcomes, expenditures). |
- [ ] **Budget Reconciliation Completed** – Compare actual spending to approved budget; note variances. |
- [ ] **Supporting Documents Attached** – Receipts, invoices, signatures, letters. |
- [ ] **Narrative Written** – Clear description of progress, challenges, and next steps. |
- [ ] **Quality Review** – Have a second set of eyes (e.g., finance director) check numbers. |
- [ ] **Timely Submission** – Upload or mail before the deadline; keep proof of delivery. |
- [ ] **Follow‑up** – Respond to funder questions promptly; schedule any required meetings. |
- [ ] **Lessons Learned Documented** – Capture insights for future proposals. |
- [ ] **Close‑out Activities Completed** – Return any unused funds, provide final report, and close the grant file. |

<a name="conclusion--continuous-improvement"></a>  

## Conclusion & Continuous Improvement  

Grant writing is a **cycle of learning and refinement**. Each proposal—whether successful or not—provides data that can improve your organization’s capacity to secure funding.  

- **Capture Feedback** – Keep a log of funder comments, both positive and critical.  
- **Iterate Your Materials** – Update your needs statement, logic model, and budget based on lessons learned.  
- **Build Relationships** – Nurture contacts with program officers; attend funder conferences and webinars.  
- **Diversify Funding Sources** – Use the insights from each grant to broaden your fundraising portfolio.  

By mastering the components outlined above, you’ll be well‑positioned to craft compelling, fundable proposals that drive real change in your community.
