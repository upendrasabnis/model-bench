## 2. What Is Acquittal Reporting?

Acquittal reporting is the final submission that a grantor receives from a grantee to verify that the grant was expended in accordance with its terms and that all required deliverables were met. In many contexts, the term “acquittal” refers specifically to the **final financial report**; in others, it also includes programmatic outcomes and supporting documentation.

### 2.1 Key Components of an Acquittal Report

| Component | Typical Content | Example |
|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| **Financial Statement** | Total expenditures, categorized by cost type (direct, indirect, overhead), and comparison to budget | $1,200,000 spent on direct program costs vs. $1,250,000 budget |
| **Cost Breakdown** | Detailed line items for staff salaries, materials, travel, sub‑awards, equipment, and indirect costs | 10 FTE staff, $600ეგმ |
| **Narrative Summary** | Explanation of any variances, costжа, and how budget constraints were managed | “Travel costs were higher due to increased field visits.” |
| **Deliverables & Outcomes** | Quantitative and qualitative data on project outputs, outcomes, and impact | 1,200 individuals served, 75% satisfaction rate |
| **Compliance Verification** | Confirmation that all contractual obligations were met (e.g., IRB approval, data security) | “All data were stored on encrypted servers.” |
| **Attachments** | Supporting documents: invoices, receipts, contracts, sub‑award agreements, approvals | Copies of all invoices, sub‑award agreements with vendors |

### 2.2 When Is an Acquittal Due?

- **Federal Grants:** Usually at the end of the fiscal year or the grant period.
- **State Grants:** Follow state guidelines—often quarterly or semi‑annual.
- **Philanthropic Grants:** Dependent on the foundation’s reporting schedule; some require annual, others quarterly, and many require a “final” report upon grant completion.

## 3. Common Requirements Across Funders

While each funder has its own nuances, several core requirements recur stocks:

1. **Budget Adherence**: No disbursement outside the approved budget unless formally modified.
2. **Cost Principles**: Adherence to the Uniform Guidance (U.S. federal), the Cost Principles for State Grants, or the foundation’s policy.
3. **Indirect Cost Rate**: Must be negotiated and approved; cannot exceed the negotiated rate.
4. **Sub‑award Oversight**: Must ensure sub‑awards meet all cost principles and reporting requirements.
5. **Data Security & Privacy**: Compliance with HIPAA, FERPA, or other relevant regulations.
6. **Timeliness**: Reports submitted by the required deadline; penalties for late submission.
7. **Documentation**: Receipts, contracts, and other documents must support every dollar reported.
8. **Audit Trail**: A clear, traceable chain of evidence from budget to expenditure to reporting.

## 6. Common Compliance Failures (and How to Avoid Them)

Below are the most frequent compliance failures seen across government and philanthropic grants, with real‑world case studies and actionable avoidance strategies.

### 6.1 Failure #1: Budget Variance Without Formal Modification

**Case Study:** *一定 Non‑Profit* (NYC) received a $500,000 state grant. During the second year, they spent $550,000Bath due to an unforeseen equipment upgrade. The organization did not submit a budget modification, leading to a $50,000 over‑expenditure that the state required them to repay.

**Avoidance Strategy:**
- **Action:** Submit a formal *Budget Modification Request* (BMR) within 30 days of the variance.  
- **Tool:** Use the funder’s online portal (e.g., NYS Office of the Comptroller) to upload the revised budget.  
- **Check:** Include a justification narrative for the variance and a revised cost breakdown.

### 6.2 Failure #2: Misclassification of Indirect Costs

**Case Study:** *Global Health Alliance* (USA) accrued $120,000 in indirect costs on a $1,000,000 grant. The negotiated indirect cost rate wasENTRY 55%, but the organization used a 60% rate. Auditors flagged the over‑recovery. The organization had to pay $6,000 back.

**Avoidance Strategy:**
- **Action:** Review the negotiated IRR in the grant agreement; use a spreadsheet to calculate indirect cost automatically.  
- **Tool:** Implement an automatic IRR calculator in the financial system.  
- **Check:** Conduct quarterly IRR reconciliation.

### 6.3 Failure #3: Inadequate Sub‑award Oversight

**Case Study:** *Youth Empowerment Center* (Illinois) awarded a sub‑grant to a local community group. The sub‑grant agreement lacked a cost‑control clause. The sub‑awarded entity spent $30,000 on personal travel and the parent organization was required to reimburse the state.

**Avoidance Strategy:**
- **Action:** Standardize sub‑award agreements to include cost‑control, reporting, and audit rights.  
- **Tool:** Use a template sub‑award agreement with built‑in cost categories.  
- **Check:** Require the sub‑awarded entity to submit a quarterly cost report.

### 6.5 Failure #5: Failure to Maintain an Audit Trail

### sung 6.6 Failure #6: Data Security Non‑Compliance

**Case Study:** *Education Equity Foundation* (Washington) stored participant data on a shared Google Drive with no encryption. During a federal audit, the auditor flagged a data security breach. The foundation had to implement a data management plan and pay a $10,000 penalty.

**Case Study:** *Community Health Initiative* (Colorado) had no written sub‑award agreements with its partner clinics. When the state requested documentation during the audit, the organization could not provide evidence of the sub‑awards, leading to a $15,000 penalty.

**Avoidance Strategy:**
- **Action:** Require a signed sub‑award agreement for all sub‑contractors.  
- **Tool:** Use a sub‑award agreement template that includes cost‑type definitions.  
- **Check:** Maintain a master ledger that tracks all sub‑awards.

### 6.9 Failure #9: Non‑Compliance With Cost‑Principles

**Case Study:** *Sustainable Agriculture Fund* (Texas) expended $80,000 on a supplier who was not a qualified vendor per the federal cost‑principles. The audit flagged the cost as unallowable, resulting in a $10,000 repayment.

- **Tool:** Use a sub‑award management module that tracks changes.  
- **Check:** Reconcile sub‑award amounts quarterly.
