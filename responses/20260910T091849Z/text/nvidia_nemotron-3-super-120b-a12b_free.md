**Grant Compliance and Acquittals: A Comprehensive Guide**

### Introduction  
Grant compliance ensures that funds are used according to the terms set by the funder.  
Acquittal reporting is the process by which recipients demonstrate that they have spent the money correctly.  
Failure to meet acquittal requirements can lead to funding withdrawal, reputational damage, or legal penalties.  
This article explains what acquittal reporting entails, outlines common requirements across government and philanthropic funders, discusses record‑keeping and audits, and highlights frequent compliance failures with practical avoidance strategies.  

---  

### What Is Acquittal Reporting?  
Acquittal reporting is a formal statement submitted to a funder after a grant period ends.  
It details how the awarded funds were allocated, spent, and the outcomes achieved.  
The report typically includes financial statements, narrative descriptions of activities, and evidence of results.  
Acquittals serve both accountability and learning purposes for funders and recipients.  
They differ from interim progress reports, which are submitted during the grant term.  
A successful acquittal demonstrates that the recipient adhered to the budget, complied with restrictions, and achieved agreed‑upon outputs.

### Common Requirements Across Government and Philanthropic Funders  

#### Government Funders  
1. **Standardised Financial Forms** – Many agencies require specific templates (e.g., SF‑425 for U.S. federal grants).  
2. **Cost‑Principle Documentation** – Expenses must conform to OMB Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200) or equivalent national regulations.  
3. **Matching Funds Verification** – If the grant requires cost‑sharing, evidence of matching contributions must be provided.  
4. **Indirect Cost Rate Approval** – Recipients must submit an approved indirect cost rate agreement or use the de minimis rate.  
5. **Program‑Specific Metrics** – Agencies often demand quantitative outcomes (e.g., number of beneficiaries served, jobs created).  
6. **Compliance with Laws** – Reporting must attest to adherence to statutes such as the Davis‑Bacon Act, Buy American provisions, or environmental regulations.  

#### Philanthropic Funders  
1. **Narrative‑Heavy Reports** – Foundations often prioritize storytelling over strict financial forms.  
2. **Flexible Budget Categories** – While line‑item detail is required, foundations may allow reallocation within approved ranges.  
3. **Impact Metrics** – Emphasis on qualitative and quantitative impact (e.g., testimonials, case studies, longitudinal data).  
4. **Financial Statements** – Audited or reviewed financial statements are commonly requested for larger grants.  
5. **Governance Documentation** – Proof of board oversight, conflict‑of‑interest policies, and ethical standards.  
6. **Acknowledgement and Publicity** – Some foundations require acknowledgment in publications, websites, or event materials.  

#### Overlapping Elements  
- Clear separation of restricted vs. unrestricted funds.  
- Timely submission (usually within 30–90 days after grant end).  
- Certification by an authorized official (e.g., Executive Director or CFO).  
- Retention of supporting documentation for a defined period (often 3–7 years).

### Record‑Keeping Best Practices  

#### 1. Establish a Grant Management System  
- Use software that links budget lines to transactions (e.g., Blackbaud Financial Edge, QuickBooks Nonprofit).  
- Assign a unique grant identifier to all related documents and transactions.  

#### 2. Maintain Source Documentation  
- Keep original invoices, receipts, payroll records, and bank statements.  
- Scan paper documents and store them in a secure, backed‑up cloud folder with access controls.  

#### 3. Separate Funds by Grant  
- Create distinct sub‑accounts or cost centers for each award.  
- Avoid commingling grant money with general operating funds unless explicitly permitted.  

#### 4. Track Time and Effort  
- For personnel costs, maintain timesheets that reflect the percentage of time spent on grant activities.  
- Use periodic certifications (e.g., semi‑annual) to confirm accuracy.  

#### 5. Document Indirect Cost Allocation  
- If using an approved indirect cost rate, retain the rate agreement and the basis of allocation (e.g., square footage, salary base).  
- For de minimis rates, keep a simple calculation showing the modified total direct costs multiplied by the rate.  

#### 6. Implement Internal Controls  
- Segregate duties: the person who approves expenses should not be the one who records them.  
- Conduct monthly reconciliations of grant accounts to the general ledger.  
- Perform surprise audits or spot checks on a sample of transactions.  

#### 7. Retention Policy  
- Follow the funder’s retention requirement; if unspecified, retain records for at least three years after final expenditure report.  
- Store records in a format that ensures readability (PDF/A for scanned documents).

### Audits and Monitoring  

#### Types of Audits  
- **Desk Audit** – Funder reviews submitted acquittal documents without visiting the recipient.  
- **Site Visit Audit** – Funder staff or contracted auditors examine operations, interview staff, and verify records on‑site.  
- **Single Audit** – Required for U.S. non‑federal entities expending $750,000 or more in federal funds in a year (OMB Circular A‑133, now Subpart F of 2 CFR 200).  
- **Financial Statement Audit** – Independent CPA examines the organization’s overall financial statements, often required by large foundations.  

#### What Auditors Look For  
- Agreement between reported expenditures and supporting documentation.  
- Proper classification of costs (direct vs. indirect, allowable vs. unallowable).  
- Compliance with grant‑specific restrictions (e.g., no lobbying, no religious instruction).  
- Accuracy of performance metrics and outcome reporting.  
- Evidence of internal controls and segregation of duties.  

#### Preparing for an Audit  
- Perform a internal pre‑audit using the same checklist the funder will apply.  
- Ensure all grant files are complete, labeled, and accessible.  
- Have key personnel (grant manager, finance officer, program director) available for interviews.  
- Reconcile grant accounts monthly and resolve discrepancies promptly.  
- Maintain a log of any budget modifications and obtain funder approval where required.

### Frequent Compliance Failures and How to Avoid Them  

#### 1. Misclassification of Expenses  
- **Problem:** Charging unallowable costs (e.g., entertainment, alcohol) to the grant.  
- **Avoid:** Maintain an updated list of allowable/unallowable items per funder guidance; run expenses through a compliance checklist before posting.  

#### 2. Inadequate Time‑and‑Effort Documentation  
- **Problem:** Estimating staff time without contemporaneous records, leading to disallowed personnel costs.  
- **Avoid:** Use electronic time‑tracking systems that require daily sign‑off; conduct quarterly reviews of timesheets against project plans.  

#### 3. Failure to Obtain Prior Approval for Budget Changes  
- **Problem:** Reallocating funds beyond the permitted variance without funder consent, resulting in questioned costs.  
- **Avoid:** Know the funder’s revision thresholds (often 10% of a budget line); submit formal amendment requests before making changes.  

#### 4. Missing or Incomplete Supporting Documentation  
- **Problem:** Submitting acquittals without invoices, contracts, or payroll registers.  
- **Avoid:** Create a document‑request checklist for each expense type; attach scanned copies to the accounting transaction in real time.  

#### 5. Incorrect Indirect Cost Application  
- **Problem:** Applying the indirect rate to unallowable bases or using an outdated rate.  
- **Avoid:** Verify the approved indirect cost rate agreement each fiscal year; maintain a spreadsheet that calculates indirect costs automatically.  

#### 6. Lack of Matching Funds Verification  
- **Problem:** Reporting matching contributions that cannot be traced to source funds.  
- **Avoid:** Keep a separate ledger for matching funds; retain bank statements, donation letters, or in‑kind valuation records.  

#### 7. Inaccurate Performance Reporting  
- **Problem:** Overstating outcomes or failing to provide baseline data.  
- **Avoid:** Develop a logic model at grant onset; collect baseline data; use validated survey tools; have an external evaluator review the report before submission.  

#### 8. Missed Submission Deadlines  
- **Problem:** Late acquittals trigger automatic penalties or funding suspension.  
- **Avoid:** Set internal deadlines 15 days before the funder’s due date; use calendar reminders and assign a compliance officer to oversee timing.  

#### 9. Poor Communication with the Funder  
- **Problem:** Not seeking clarification on ambiguous guidelines, leading to assumptions that later prove incorrect.  
- **Avoid:** Establish a single point of contact; schedule quarterly check‑ins; document all written communications.  

#### 10. Insufficient Training of Staff  
- **Problem:** New grant administrators unaware of compliance nuances.  
- **Avoid:** Conduct mandatory onboarding training; provide refresher workshops annually; maintain a compliance manual accessible to all staff.

### Real‑World Examples  

#### Example 1: Federal Research Grant Misclassification  
A university received a $2 million NIH grant for a clinical trial. The finance team inadvertently charged $45 000 of catering expenses for advisory board meetings to the grant. NIH’s policy classifies meals as unallowable unless directly tied to a specific activity. During a desk audit, the expense was flagged, resulting in a $45 000 disallowance and a required refund.  
*Lesson:* Maintain a searchable database of allowable costs per agency; run all expense codes through a compliance validator before posting.  

#### Example 2: Foundation Grant Time‑and‑Effort Shortfall  
A mid‑size nonprofit secured a $500 000 grant from a private foundation to run an after‑school program. The program director estimated that 30 % of her time was spent on the grant but kept no timesheets. The foundation’s site visit auditor requested documentation; the nonprofit could only provide a retroactive affidavit, which the auditor deemed insufficient. The foundation reduced the final payment by 15 %.  
*Lesson:* Implement a simple daily log tool (e.g., Toggl) and require weekly sign‑offs by supervisors.  

#### Example 3: Philanthropic Fund Matching‑Funds Error  
A community foundation awarded a $250 000 matching grant to a local arts organization, requiring a 1:1 match. The organization reported $250 000 in matching funds, but the supporting documents included pledges that had not yet been collected. During the foundation’s audit, the unverified pledges were excluded, leaving a shortfall of $125 000. The foundation demanded repayment of the unmatched portion.  
*Lesson:* Only count matching funds when cash is received or an irrevocable pledge is documented; keep a separate matching‑funds ledger with receipt dates.  

#### Example 4: State‑Level Infrastructure Grant Indirect Cost Misapplication  
A city received a $5 million state DOT grant for road repairs. The city applied its 15 % indirect cost rate to the entire award, including equipment purchases that the state deemed “direct costs only.” The state audit recovered $750 000 in indirect costs that were incorrectly charged.  
*Lesson:* Verify which cost categories are eligible for indirect cost recovery; maintain a cost‑allocation matrix that flags non‑eligible items.
