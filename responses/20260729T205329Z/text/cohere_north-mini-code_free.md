# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  
*An In‑Depth Guide from Fund‑Research to Post‑Award Reporting*  

---  

## Introduction  
Grant writing is both an art and a science. Successful proposals combine rigorous research, clear storytelling, accurate budgeting, logical frameworks, and disciplined follow‑up. This article walks you through the entire grant‑writing cycle, offering concrete examples, practical checklists, and tips you can apply to any funding opportunity. Whether you are a solo program director or part of a large nonprofit, mastering these best practices will increase your chances of securing the resources you need to advance your mission.  

---  

## 1. Researching Funders  

### 1.1 Identify Potential Funders  
- **Foundation databases** – Candid (formerly Foundation Center), GrantStation, Foundation Directory Online.  
- **Government grants** – Grants.gov, SAM.gov, state‑specific grant portals.  
- **Corporate giving** – Company CSR pages, local business alliances.  
- **Community sources** – United Way, local chambers of commerce, community foundations.  

### 1.2 Understand Funding Priorities  
- Review each funder’s **mission statement** and **grant guidelines**.  
- Look for **priority program areas**, **geographic focus**, **audience**, and **funding cycles**.  
- Note any **special requirements** (e.g., matching gifts, capacity‑building grants).  

### 1.3 Review Guidelines and Deadlines  
| Funder | Deadline | Application Type | Required Attachments |
|--------|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| The Ford Foundation | Jan 15, 2025 | Full proposal | Budget, logic model, letters of support |
| Local Community Foundation | Oct 31, 2024 | Letter of intent | Project summary, org chart |
| NIH (National Institutes of Health) | Rolling | Full proposal | Human subjects IRB approval, budget justification |

- **Create a master spreadsheet** to track deadlines, required documents, and contact persons.  
- **Set calendar alerts** at least 30 days before each deadline to allow time for revisions.  

### 1.4 Track Requirements and Restrictions  
- **Funding restrictions** – e.g., “no indirect costs,” “must be used for direct services only.”  
- **Reporting frequency** – quarterly, semi‑annual, or annual.  
- **Compliance requirements** – audit standards, grant‑making policies, ethical guidelines.  

### 1.5 Checklist for Funder Research  

- [ ] Confirm funder’s **mission** aligns with your organization’s mission.  
- [ ] Verify **eligibility** (organization type, geographic area, program focus).  
- [ ] Record **deadline** and **application method** (online portal, email, postal mail).  
- [ ] List **required documents** (budget, logic model, letters of support, evaluation plan).  
- [ ] Note any **special instructions** (e.g., formatting, page limits, required signatures).  
- [ ] Identify **contact person** for questions and any pre‑submission consultations.

## 2. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

### 2.1 Define the Problem  
- **Be specific** – “Children in XYZ county lack access to after‑school tutoring.”  
- **Use data** – cite local school performance reports, census data, or health statistics.  

### 2.2 Provide Evidence and Context  
- **Statistics**: “In 2023, 42 % of students in XYZ scored below grade level in math.”  
- **Quotes**: “Parents tell us they cannot afford private tutoring, leaving gaps that affect academic confidence.”  
- **Trend analysis**: “Over the past five years, the dropout rate has risen 12 % in our target community.”  

### 2.3 Articulate the Vision of Change  
- **Outcome focus**: “We envision a community where every child achieves grade‑level proficiency by age 12.”  
- **Connection to mission**: “This aligns directly with our mission to eliminate educational inequities.”  

### 2.4 Example Needs Statement  

> **Needs Statement**  
> In XYZ County, 38 % of elementary students in low‑income neighborhoods score below grade level in reading, according to the 2022 State Assessment. These students face limited access to affordable, high‑quality after‑school tutoring, which correlates with lower high‑school graduation rates. Our organization will address this gap by providing free, evidence‑based tutoring services that improve reading proficiency and increase graduation rates by 15 % within three years.  

### 2.5 Checklist for a Strong Needs Statement  

- [ ] Clearly state **who is affected** and **how many**.  
- [ ] Include **current data** (year, source, relevance).  
- [ ] Explain **why the problem matters** to the community and the funder.  
- [ ] Highlight **existing gaps** or unmet needs.  
- [ ] Define **desired outcomes** and their impact.

## 3. Developing the Proposal Narrative  

### 3.1 Executive Summary (1‑2 pages)  
- Summarize **problem, solution, approach, goals, budget, and impact**.  
- Keep it **concise**; funders often read this first and decide whether to continue.  

### 3.2 Organizational Overview  
- **History**: years in operation, key milestones.  
- **Credentials**: staff expertise, partnerships, certifications.  
- **Governance**: board composition, conflict‑of‑interest policies.  

### 3.3 Program Description  
- **What** you will do (services, activities).  
- **Who** will receive them (target population, demographics).  
- **Where** (geographic focus).  

### 3.4 Goals, Objectives, and Outcomes  
| Goal | Objective (SMART) | Outcome (measurable) |
|------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Increase reading proficiency | Improve 30 students’ reading scores by 1.5 grade levels within 12 months | 30 students achieve ≥1.5‑grade‑level gain (measured by standardized test) |
| Build parent engagement | Conduct monthly workshops for 50 families | 80 % of participating families report increased involvement in homework support |

- Use **SMART** criteria (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound).  

### 3.5 Implementation Strategy  
- **Timeline**: Gantt chart or bullet‑point schedule.  
- **Staffing**: roles, responsibilities, qualifications.  
- **Partnerships**: collaborations that add credibility or capacity.  

### 3.6 Evaluation Plan  
- **Logic model** (see Section 5).  
- **Data collection methods**: surveys, tests, focus groups, administrative records.  
- **Metrics**: quantitative (percent change) and qualitative (stories of impact).  
- **Reporting schedule**: how often you’ll share results with the funder.  

### 3.7 Example Narrative Section – “Program Description”  

> **Program Description**  
> Our “Read‑Ready After‑School Program” will operate in three elementary schools within XYZ County. For a total of 150 students per year, we will deliver twice‑weekly, 90‑minute tutoring sessions led by certified teachers and trained volunteers. Sessions will follow the Evidence-Based Reading Intervention (EBRI) framework, which has demonstrated a 1.8‑grade‑level gain in reading proficiency in independent evaluations. The program will be facilitated in partnership with the XYZ School District, which will provide classroom space and student referrals.  

### 3.8 Checklist for Narrative Development  

- [ ] Executive summary captures key points in ≤2 pages.  
- [ ] Organization’s mission and track record are clearly linked to the project.  
- [ ] Program description is specific, realistic, and replicable.  
- [ ] Goals and objectives are SMART and aligned with funder priorities.  
- [ ] Implementation timeline includes realistic milestones and responsible parties.  
- [ ] Evaluation plan outlines data sources, analysis methods, and reporting cadence.  
- [ ] All sections are proofread for grammar, clarity, and consistency.

## 4. Budgeting and Financial Projections  

### 4.1 Direct vs. Indirect Costs  
- **Direct costs**: salaries, supplies, travel, equipment directly tied to the project.  
- **Indirect costs** (overhead): rent, utilities, administrative salaries—often capped by funders.  

### 4.2 Budget Categories (Typical)  
1. Personnel (Salaries, Benefits, Fringe)  
2. Supplies & Materials  
3. Travel & Lodging  
4. Equipment & Software  
5. Rent & Facility Costs  
6. Indirect Costs (if allowable)  
7. Evaluation & Reporting Expenses  

### 4.3 Reasonable and Allowable Costs  
- **Allowable**: costs necessary and reasonable for the grant’s purpose.  
- **Unallowable**: alcohol, fines, lobbying, personal expenses.  

### 4.4 Example Budget Table  

| Category | Year 1 | Year 2 | Total |
|----------|--------|--------|-------|
| Personnel (Program Director – 0.5 FTE) | $30,000 | $30,000 | $60,000 |
| Tutors (3 part‑time) | $15,000 | $15,000 | $30,000 |
| Supplies (books, workbooks) | $5,000 | $3,000 | $8,000 |
| Evaluation (external evaluator) | $8,000 | $4,000 | $12,000 |
| Travel (conferences) | $2,000 | $1,000 | $3,000 |
| Indirect Costs (10% of direct) | $6,600 | $4,400 | $11,000 |
| **Total** | **$66,600** | **$47,400** | **$114,000** |

### 4.5 Budget Narrative  
- Explain **why each line item is necessary** and **how it supports the program**.  
- Provide **justification** for salaries (e.g., “Program Director’s 0.5 FTE salary reflects the time commitment required to oversee 150 students annually”).  

> **Budget Narrative – Personnel**  
> The Program Director will dedicate 0.5 FTE to this grant, managing all aspects of the Read‑Ready Program. This role includes curriculum coordination, staff supervision, and reporting to the funder. The salary of $30,000 per year is market‑based and aligns with comparable positions in the region.  

### 4.6 Checklist for Budget Development  

- [ ] Separate direct and indirect costs clearly.  
- [ ] Use **realistic, market‑based figures**; avoid under‑ or over‑budgeting.  
- [ ] Include a **budget narrative** that justifies each line item.  
- [ ] Ensure **budget alignment** with the timeline and scope of work.  
- [ ] Verify **funder’s cost policies** (e.g., indirect cost caps, allowable categories).  
- [ ] Conduct a **budget vs. actual** comparison after project completion.

## 5. Logic Model Development  

### 5.1 Core Components  
- **Inputs** – resources (funding, staff, materials).  
- **Activities** – actions taken (tutoring sessions, workshops).  
- **Outputs** – immediate deliverables (number of sessions delivered, participants served).  
- **Outcomes** – short‑ and long‑term effects (reading score gains, increased graduation rates).  
- **Impact** – broader societal changes (reduced educational inequity).  

### 5.2 Example Logic Model  

```
Inputs                         Activities                     Outputs                         Outcomes                              Impact
$114,000 grant                 • Hire 3 part‑time tutors        • 150 students served per year   • 30 students gain 1.5‑grade‑level reading gain   • Improved high‑school graduation rates
Staff expertise                • Conduct bi‑weekly 90‑min sessions   • 600 tutoring hours delivered   • Increased parent engagement (80% report higher involvement)   • Reduced achievement gap in XYZ County
Partnership with XYZ School District   • Provide sessions at 3 schools   • 3 school sites utilized   • Students attend ≥80% of sessions   • Long‑term reduction in dropout rates
Evaluation resources           • Administer pre/post assessments   • Data collected for 30 students   • Evidence of program effectiveness   • Scalable model for other districts
```

### 5.3 Aligning Logic Model with Funder Requirements  
- Many funders **require a logic model** as a separate attachment.  
- Use the **same language** as the funder’s own theory of change when possible.  
- Ensure each **outcome** directly answers the funder’s performance indicators.  

### 5.4 Checklist for Logic Model Creation  

- [ ] List **all inputs** (funding, staff, in‑kind).  
- [ ] Define **specific activities** (what you will do).  
- [ ] Quantify **outputs** (numbers, hours, deliverables).  
- [ ] Identify **desired outcomes** (short‑term, intermediate, long‑term).  
- [ ] Connect each **outcome** to the **impact** you aim to achieve.  
- [ ] Review with **stakeholders** (staff, partners, beneficiaries) for realism.  
- [ ] Ensure the model **matches the funder’s format** (if specified).

## 6. Writing the Grant Application  

### 6.1 Follow Instructions Exactly  
- **Page limits**, **font type/size**, **margin requirements**.  
- **Naming conventions** for files (e.g., “Grant_Proposal_2025.pdf”).  
- **Submission portal** (online system vs. postal mail).  

### 6.2 Tailor Each Proposal  
- **Mirror the funder’s language** in the needs statement and objectives.  
- **Highlight** how your project addresses the **specific priorities** listed in the RFP.  

### 6.3 Edit and Proofread  
- **First draft** – focus on content.  
- **Second draft** – tighten language, improve flow.  
- **Third draft** – grammar, spelling, formatting.  
- **Peer review** – ask a colleague to check consistency and compliance.  

### 6.4 Formatting and Layout  
- Use **headings** (e.g., “Executive Summary”, “Budget”) for easy navigation.  
- Insert **page numbers** and **your organization’s footer**.  
- Include a **cover letter** that references the RFP number and deadline.  

### 6.5 Example Cover Letter  

> **Cover Letter**  
> Date: November 5, 2024  
> Ms. Laura Smith  
> Program Officer, Education Initiative  
> The Community Foundation of XYZ  
> Re: 2025 After‑School Tutoring Grant – Proposal for “Read‑Ready Program”  
> Dear Ms. Smith,  
> Thank you for considering our application for the 2025 Education Initiative grant. Enclosed is our comprehensive proposal, which outlines how we will address the reading achievement gap in XYZ County through evidence‑based tutoring. We look forward to discussing this partnership and answering any questions you may have.  
> Sincerely,  
> Jane Doe, Executive Director, XYZ Youth Services  

### 6.6 Checklist for Final Application  

- [ ] All required attachments are included (budget, logic model, letters of support).  
- [ ] Application follows **funder’s formatting guidelines** exactly.  
- [ ] Each section **answers the questions** posed in the RFP.  
- [ ] Budget aligns with **program narrative** and **logic model**.  
- [ ] No spelling or grammatical errors remain.  
- [ ] Contact information and **organizational details** are current.  
- [ ] Submitted **on time** and through the correct channel.

## 7. Post‑Award Management  

### 7.1 Understand the Award Agreement  
- **Scope of work** – what you are funded to do.  
- **Reporting obligations** – frequency, format, required documents.  
- **Budget restrictions** – allowed vs. unallowed expenditures.  
- **Compliance requirements** – audits, procurement rules, sub‑award policies.  

### 7.2 Set Up a Monitoring System  
| Task | Frequency | Responsible Person | Tool |
|------|-----------|--------------------|------|
| Review grant calendar | Monthly | Executive Director | Google Calendar |
| Track expenditures vs. budget | Weekly | Finance Manager | QuickBooks |
| Collect program data | After each session | Program Manager | SurveyMonkey + Excel |
| Draft quarterly reports | Every 3 months | Reporting Coordinator | Word template |
| Conduct internal audits | Annually | Board Audit Committee | Audit software |

### 7.3 Reporting Requirements  
- **Narrative report** – progress toward goals, challenges, successes.  
- **Financial report** – actual expenditures, budget variance explanation.  
- **Evaluation report** – outcome data, lessons learned, recommendations.  

#### Example Quarterly Narrative Report Structure  

1. **Project Overview** – brief recap of activities completed.  
2. **Milestones Achieved** – e.g., “Delivered 12 tutoring sessions, served 48 students.”  
3. **Challenges & Mitigations** – e.g., “Supply chain delay resolved by alternative vendor.”  
4. **Budget Status** – “90 % of Year 1 budget spent; remaining $6,600 allocated to evaluation.”  
5. **Next Quarter Priorities** – “Begin parent workshop series, finalize evaluation report.”  

### 7.4 Financial Reporting  
- Use **standard financial templates** provided by the funder or adapted from your accounting system.  
- Include **bank statements**, **receipts**, **invoices** as supporting documents.  

### 7.5 Sub‑Award Management (if applicable)  
- **Identify sub‑recipients** early.  
- **Develop sub‑award agreements** that mirror the primary grant’s terms.  
- **Monitor sub‑recipient compliance** through progress reports and site visits.  

### 7.6 Continuous Improvement  
- **Conduct a mid‑project review** (6‑month checkpoint).  
- **Gather stakeholder feedback** (beneficiaries, partners, staff).  
- **Adjust activities** if outcomes are not on track.  

### 7.7 Example Reporting Calendar  

| Month | Deadline | Report Type | Deliverable |
|-------|----------|-------------|-------------|
| Jan 2025 | Feb 1 | Quarterly Narrative & Financial | PDF + Excel |
| Apr 2025 | May 1 | Mid‑Year Progress Review | Word doc + presentation |
| Jul 2025 | Aug 1 | Second Quarterly Report | PDF + Excel |
| Oct 2025 | Nov 1 | Final Narrative & Financial | Full impact report |
| Dec 2025 | Jan 15 (following year) | Final Evaluation Summary | PDF + data sets |

### 7.8 Checklist for Post‑Award Management  

- [ ] Read and store the **award agreement** in a secure, searchable location.  
- [ ] Set up **milestone reminders** in your project management tool.  
- [ ] Maintain **receipts and documentation** for all expenditures.  
- [ ] Submit **all required reports** on time; keep copies for your records.  
- [ ] Conduct **internal budget reconciliations** monthly.  
- [ ] Hold **quarterly team meetings** to discuss progress and obstacles.  
- [ ] Document **lessons learned** and incorporate them into future proposals.  
- [ ] Prepare for **audit readiness** (organized files, clear policies).

## Conclusion  
Grant writing is a cyclical process that begins with thorough research, flows through strategic storytelling, meticulous budgeting, and logical planning, and ends with disciplined post‑award management. By following the best practices outlined above—using checklists, concrete examples, and clear templates—you can produce proposals that not only meet funder requirements but also demonstrate your organization’s capacity to deliver real impact.  

Remember: **Consistency, attention to detail, and a focus on outcomes** are the hallmarks of successful grant professionals. Invest time in each phase, seek feedback from peers, and continuously refine your approach. The result is a stronger organization, more funded programs, and a brighter future for the communities you serve.
