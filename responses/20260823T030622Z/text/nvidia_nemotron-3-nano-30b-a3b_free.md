## 2. What Is an Acquittal Report? (Lines 71‑120)

**2.1 Formal Definition**  
An **acquittal report** (sometimes called a **final report**, **expenditure report**, or **close‑out report**) is the **official documentation** submitted to the funder that confirms how the granted funds were spent, what outcomes were achieved, and whether the funded project has been completed in accordance with the grant’s terms and conditions.

**2.2 Core Components**  
1. **Narrative Summary** – Description of activities, milestones reached, and outcomes.  
2. **Financial Reconciliation** – Detailed breakdown of all expenses charged to the grant, including any unspent balances.  
3. **Compliance Statements** – Confirmation that all matching‑fund requirements, cost‑allowability rules, and reporting timelines were met.  
4. **Supporting Documentation** – Receipts, invoices, bank statements, sub‑award agreements, and any other evidentiary material required by the funder.

**2.3 Typical Submission Timeline**  
- **Federal Grants:** Usually due **30 days after the project end date** (or as specified in the award notice).  
- **State Grants:** Varies; many require submission **within 60 days** of project completion.  
- **Private Foundations:** Often have a **90‑day** or **180‑day** window, but some foundations request a **final report** only at the end of the grant cycle.

**2.4 Legal Implications**  
Failure to submit an accurate acquittal can trigger:  
- **Funding claw‑backs** (the funder may reclaim disbursed amounts).  
- **Ineligibility** for future awards.  
- **Criminal or civil penalties** in cases of fraud or willful misrepresentation.

## 3. Why Acquittal Reporting Matters (Lines 121‑170)

**3.1 Accountability to Stakeholders**  
- **Taxpayers** expect that public money is spent wisely.  
- **Donors and board members** want assurance that their philanthropic dollars are achieving intended impact.  
- **Regulators** monitor compliance to enforce public policy objectives.

**3.2 Financial Integrity**  
Accurate acquittals protect the organization’s **audit trail**, ensuring that every dollar can be traced from award to expenditure. This is critical for:  
- **Internal control** over cash flow.  
- **Transparent financial statements** for auditors and internal review.

**3.3 Future Funding Opportunities**  
A clean acquittal record:  
- Demonstrates **reliability**, increasing the likelihood of renewed or expanded funding.  
- Serves as a **positive reference** when applying for competitive grants.

**3.4 Risk Management**  
Proper documentation reduces the risk of:  
- **Misallocation accusations** (e.g., “double‑dipping” or “unauthorized use”).  
- **Audit findings** that could lead to corrective actions or financial penalties.

## 4. Common Elements Across Funders (Lines 171‑220)

**4.1 Eligibility Verification**  
All funders require proof that the project was **eligible** under the original award terms (e.g., purpose, duration, budget ceiling).

**4.2 Expense Allowability**  
- **Direct costs** (salaries, supplies, equipment) must be **allowable** under the funder’s cost principles.  
- **Indirect costs** (overhead) are subject to a **negotiated rate** or a **fixed percentage** stipulated in the award.

**4.3 Matching‑Fund Requirements**  
- Many government grants demand a **cost‑share** contribution (often 25‑50 % of total project costs).  
- Private foundations may waive this requirement but still ask for **in‑kind contributions** or **volunteer hours**.

**4.4 Outcome Reporting**  
- **Performance metrics** (e.g., number of beneficiaries served, outcomes achieved) must be reported, often using the funder’s prescribed indicator set.

**4.5 Documentation Retention**  
- **Minimum retention periods** typically range from **3 to 7 years** after the final close‑out, depending on the funder and jurisdiction.

**4.6 Signature & Certification**  
- The report must be signed by an authorized official (e.g., Executive Director, CFO) who **certifies** that the information is true, complete, and accurate to the best of their knowledge.
