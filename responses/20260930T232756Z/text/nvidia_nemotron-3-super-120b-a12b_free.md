## 1. Introduction  
- Grant writing blends storytelling, data, and strategy.  
- Success hinges on funder alignment, clear needs, and realistic budgets.  
- This guide walks you through each step, from research to post‑award reporting.  
- Use the checklists at the end of each section to self‑audit your draft.  
- Keep a master folder (digital or paper) for templates, funder profiles, and past proposals.

## 2. Researching Funders  
### 2.1 Identify Potential Funders  
- Scan foundations’ websites for mission statements.  
- Use databases: Foundation Directory Online, GrantWatch, Candid.  
- Review IRS Form 990s to see giving patterns and average award size.  
- Look at recent grantees to gauge fit and competition.  
- Note application deadlines, reporting requirements, and eligibility rules.  

### 2.2 Prioritize Fit Over Funding Amount  
- Score each prospect on mission alignment (0‑3), funding range (0‑2), and relationship potential (0‑1).  
- Total score ≥ 5 = high‑priority target.  
- Example: A youth‑arts nonprofit scores 3 (mission) + 2 (range $25‑50k) + 1 (past contact) = 6 → pursue.  

### 2.3 Build a Funder Profile Sheet  
- **Funder Name** – one line.  
- **Focus Areas** – bullet list.  
- **Average Award** – dollar range.  
- **Application Cycle** – monthly/quarterly/annual.  
- **Key Contact** – name, title, email.  
- **Notes** – any insider tips or past interactions.  

### 2.4 Checklist: Funder Research  
- [ ] Mission alignment verified.  
- [ ] Funding range matches project budget.  
- [ ] Eligibility criteria satisfied (geography, organization type).  
- [ ] Deadline recorded in calendar with reminders.  
- [ ] Contact person identified for pre‑submission questions.

## 3. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  
### 3.1 Start with Data, Not Anecdotes  
- Cite recent statistics (cite source, year).  
- Example: “In 2023, 22 % of children in County X lacked access to after‑school STEM programs (County Health Dept., 2024).”  
- Use local data when possible; supplement with state/national benchmarks.  

### 3.2 Humanize the Numbers  
- Follow each statistic with a brief vignette.  
- Example: “Maria, a 12‑year‑old Latina, walks two miles each day to the nearest library because her school lacks a computer lab.”  
- Keep vignettes ≤ 2 sentences; ensure confidentiality.  

### 3.3 Show the Gap, Not Just the Problem  
- Describe what exists (assets) and what is missing (gap).  
- Example: “While three community centers offer tutoring, none provide hands‑on robotics kits, leaving a gap for 150 interested middle‑schoolers.”  

### 3.4 Align the Need with the Funder’s Priorities  
- Mirror language from the funder’s RFP or guidelines.  
- If funder emphasizes “equity,” explicitly tie the gap to inequitable access.  

### 3.5 Checklist: Needs Statement  
- [ ] At least two credible data sources cited.  
- [ ] Data are recent (within last 3 years).  
- [ ] One human‑interest vignette included.  
- [ ] Gap clearly articulated (assets vs. missing services).  
- [ ] Language matches funder’s stated priorities.  
- [ ] No jargon; understandable to a lay reviewer.

## 4. Setting SMART Goals and Objectives  
### 4.1 Define the Overarching Goal  
- Broad, outcome‑oriented statement.  
- Example: “Increase STEM proficiency among underserved youth in County X.”  

### 4.2 Break Into Specific Objectives  
- Use the SMART framework: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound.  
- Objective 1: “By month 12, deliver 30 hours of robotics instruction to 150 students.”  
- Objective 2: “Achieve a 20 % increase in participants’ scores on a standardized STEM assessment from pre‑ to post‑test.”  

### 4.3 Limit Number of Objectives  
- 3‑5 objectives per proposal keep focus clear.  
- Each objective should map to a distinct activity or output.  

### 4.4 Checklist: Goals & Objectives  
- [ ] One overarching goal statement present.  
- [ ] Each objective follows SMART criteria.  
- [ ] Objectives are numbered and distinct.  
- [ ] Total number of objectives ≤ 5.  
- [ ] Objectives directly address the needs statement gap.

## 5. Developing a Logic Model  
### 5.1 Components  
- **Inputs** – resources (staff, funding, materials).  
- **Activities** – what you will do (workshops, mentorship).  
- **Outputs** – tangible products (number of sessions, kits distributed).  
- **Outcomes** – short‑term (knowledge gain), medium‑term (behavior change), long‑term (academic improvement).  
- **Impact** – ultimate societal change (reduced STEM achievement gap).  

### 5.2 Visual Layout Tips  
- Use a simple table or flowchart.  
- Keep each cell to one short phrase.  
- Color‑code: Inputs (blue), Activities (green), Outputs (orange), Outcomes (purple), Impact (red).  

### 5.3 Example Logic Model (Condensed)  
| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Inputs | $50k grant, 2 part‑time instructors, 15 robotics kits |
| Activities | Weekly 2‑hour robotics labs, quarterly family showcase |
| Outputs | 30 lab sessions delivered, 450 student‑hours, 3 showcase events |
| Short‑Term Outcome | 80 % of participants demonstrate basic programming competence |
| Medium‑Term Outcome | 60 % increase in interest in pursuing STEM courses |
| Long‑Term Outcome | Improved math and science GPA among participants |
| Impact | Narrowed STEM achievement gap in County X by 2028 |

### 5.4 Checklist: Logic Model  
- [ ] All five components present.  
- [ ] Inputs realistic and tied to budget.  
- [ ] Activities directly lead to outputs.  
- [ ] Outputs quantified (numbers, frequencies).  
- [ ] Outcomes progress logically from short‑ to long‑term.  
- [ ] Impact aligns with overarching goal.

## 6. Building a Realistic Budget  
### 6.1 Budget Categories  
- Personnel (salaries, fringe benefits).  
- Direct program costs (materials, equipment, participant stipends).  
- Indirect/overhead (admin, utilities, rent – usually a % of direct costs).  
- Contingency (typically 5 % of total).  

### 6.2 Costing Best Practices  
- Use actual quotes or vendor estimates for equipment.  
- Calculate fringe benefits as a percentage of salary (e.g., 25 %).  
- Document assumptions in a budget narrative.  
- Example: “Robotics kits @ $120 each × 15 = $1,800 (quote from Supplier XYZ, 03/2025).”  

### 6.3 Matching Funds & In‑Kind Contributions  
- List any cash match or in‑kind donations (volunteer hours, donated space).  
- Show funder that you have skin in the game.  

### 6.4 Checklist: Budget  
- [ ] All line items categorized correctly.  
- [ ] Personnel costs include salary + fringe.  
- [ ] Direct costs backed by quotes or internal cost sheets.  
- [ ] Indirect cost rate justified (if applicable).  
- [ ] Contingency line present and ≤ 5 %.  
- [ ] Total matches requested amount.  
- [ ] Budget narrative explains each major item.

## 7. Writing an Evaluation Plan  
### 7.1 Types of Evaluation  
- **Formative** – ongoing feedback to improve implementation.  
- **Summative** – final assessment of outcomes against objectives.  

### 7.2 Data Collection Methods  
- Surveys (pre/post), focus groups, observation checklists, administrative records.  
- Choose methods that match resources and desired rigor.  

### 7.3 Indicators & Targets  
- Link each objective to at least one indicator.  
- Example: Objective 2 → Indicator: average STEM assessment score; Target: +20 % gain.  

### 7.4 Analysis Plan  
- Describe statistical tests (t‑score, ANOVA) or qualitative coding approach.  
- Note who will conduct analysis (internal staff, external evaluator).  

### 7.5 Checklist: Evaluation Plan  
- [ ] Formative and summative components described.  
- [ ] Data collection tools listed (survey, interview guide, etc.).  
- [ ] Each objective tied to a measurable indicator.  
- [ ] Targets for each indicator stated.  
- [ ] Analysis methodology outlined.  
- [ ] Responsible party for data management identified.

## 8. Ensuring Sustainability & Scalability  
### 8.1 Post‑Grant Funding Strategies  
- Diversify revenue: individual donors, corporate sponsorships, fee‑for‑service.  
- Develop a fundraising calendar aligned with program cycles.  

### 8.2 Capacity Building  
- Train staff to maintain activities after grant ends.  
- Document standard operating procedures (SOPs) for replication.  

### 8.3 Community Ownership  
- Involve beneficiaries in advisory boards or volunteer roles.  
- Show letters of support from partners committing to continue resources.  

### 8.4 Checklist: Sustainability  
- [ ] At least two post‑grant revenue sources identified.  
- [ ] Staff training plan described.  
- [ ] SOPs or manuals referenced.  
- [ ] Letters of support from partner organizations attached.  
- [ ] Scalability note (e.g., “model can be replicated in neighboring districts”).

## 9. Assembling the Full Proposal  
### 9.1 Standard Sections (in order)  
1. Cover Letter  
2. Executive Summary (1‑page)  
3. Needs Statement  
4. Goals & Objectives  
5. Program Description (including logic model)  
6. Evaluation Plan  
7. Budget & Budget Narrative  
8. Sustainability Plan  
9. Organizational Background & Capacity  
10. Appendices (letters of support, IRS 501(c)(3) determination, resumes)  

### 9.2 Writing Tips  
- Use active voice: “We will deliver” not “It will be delivered.”  
- Keep sentences ≤ 20 words for readability.  
- Avoid acronyms on first use; spell out then provide abbreviation.  
- Maintain consistent font (e.g., 11‑pt Calibri) and 1‑inch margins.  

### 9.3 Checklist: Full Proposal Assembly  
- [ ] All required sections present per RFP.  
- [ ] Page limits respected (including appendices).  
- [ ] Font, spacing, and margins comply.  
- [ ] Each section starts on a new page (if required).  
- [ ] All attachments labeled and referenced in the main text.  
- [ ] Final PDF compiled and bookmarked for easy navigation.

## 10. Review, Edit, and Proofread  
### 10.1 Internal Review Cycle  
- **Draft 1** – content focus (needs, objectives, logic).  
- **Draft 2** – budget and evaluation alignment.  
- **Draft 3** – language, tone, and compliance.  

### 10.2 External Peer Review  
- Ask a colleague unfamiliar with the project to read for clarity.  
- Incorporate feedback but preserve your voice.  

### 10.3 Final Proofread  
- Read aloud to catch awkward phrasing.  
- Use spell‑check, then manually verify proper nouns and numbers.  

### 10.4 Checklist: Review & Editing  
- [ ] Three internal review cycles completed.  
- [ ] External reviewer feedback incorporated.  
- [ ] No spelling or grammar errors (verified via two tools).  
- [ ] All numbers consistent across narrative, budget, and logic model.  
- [ ] Page numbers and table of contents accurate.

## 11. Submission Procedures  
### 11.1 Pre‑Submission Checklist  
- Confirm deadline (time zone).  
- Verify required file format (PDF, Word).  
- Ensure all signatures (if required) are present.  
- Test upload portal or email attachment size limits.  

### 11.2 Submission Log  
- Record: Funder, Date submitted, Method (portal/email), Confirmation ID, Contact person.  

### 11.3 Checklist: Submission  
- [ ] Deadline noted in calendar with 24‑hour reminder.  
- [ ] File naming convention followed (e.g., OrgName_ProjectName_Date.pdf).  
- [ ] All required documents attached.  
- [ ] Submission confirmation saved.  
- [ ] Log entry created immediately after submission.

## 12. Post‑Award Management  
### 12.1 Award Acceptance  
- Review award letter for special conditions (matching funds, reporting timelines).  
- Sign and return any required agreements within the stipulated window.  

### 12.2 Setting Up Financial Tracking  
- Create a unique grant code in your accounting system.  
- Track expenditures against budget line items monthly.  

### 12.3 Program Kick‑Off  
- Hold a launch meeting with staff, partners, and beneficiaries.  
- Distribute copies of the logic model and evaluation tools.  

### 12.4 Monitoring & Reporting  
- Submit progress reports as required (quarterly, semi‑annual).  
- Include: accomplishments vs. objectives, financial statement, challenges, and mitigation steps.  

### 12.5 Checklist: Post‑Award  
- [ ] Award letter reviewed and signed.  
- [ ] Grant code established in accounting software.  
- [ ] Kick‑off meeting minutes filed.  
- [ ] Reporting schedule entered into project calendar.  
- [ ] Financial tracking spreadsheet updated monthly.

## 13. Post‑Award Reporting Best Practices  
### 13.1 Narrative Reporting  
- Use the same headings as the proposal for consistency.  
- Highlight successes, unexpected outcomes, and lessons learned.  

### 13.2 Financial Reporting  
- Provide a detailed ledger showing actual vs. budgeted amounts.  
- Explain any variances (> 10 %) with justification.  

### 13.3 Impact Storytelling  
- Include at least one beneficiary quote or case study per report.  
- Pair quantitative data with qualitative illustration.  

### 13.4 Checklist: Post‑Award Reporting  
- [ ] Report submitted by the deadline.  
- [ ] Narrative follows proposal section headings.  
- [ ] Financial statement includes variance explanations.  
- [ ] At least one impact story or quote included.  
- [ ] Attachments (photos, survey results) labeled and referenced.

## 14. Common Pitfalls & How to Avoid Them  
| Pitfall | Consequence | Prevention |
|---------|-------------|------------|
| Vague needs statement | Reviewers cannot gauge urgency | Use concrete data + vignette |
| Overly ambitious budget | Funders doubt feasibility | Get quotes, show cost‑saving measures |
| Missing logic model | Proposal appears disjointed | Draft model early, align each section |
| Ignoring funder’s guidelines | Automatic rejection | Create a checklist of RFP requirements |
| Weak evaluation plan | Cannot prove impact | Tie each objective to measurable indicator |
| Late submission | Disqualified | Set internal deadline 5 days before actual |
| Poor financial tracking | Audit findings | Use grant‑specific code, reconcile monthly |
| No sustainability plan | Funders worry about permanence | Outline post‑grant revenue and capacity steps |

## 16. Final Thoughts  
Grant writing is both an art and a science. By systematically researching funders, grounding your narrative in solid data, aligning every component with a clear logic model, and maintaining rigorous financial and evaluation practices, you increase the likelihood of securing funding—and, more importantly, delivering meaningful change. Use the checklists throughout this guide as living documents; update them as you learn from each proposal and award.
