# Best Practices in Nonprofit Grant Writing  

## Introduction  

Grant writing is both an art and a science. It requires a deep understanding of funder priorities, a crystal‑clear articulation of community need, and meticulous attention to detail in budgets, logic models, and reporting. Whether you are a seasoned development director or a first‑time grant writer, mastering these best practices will increase your chances of securing funding and, more importantly, of delivering successful programs that align with funder goals.  

This guide walks you through the entire grant‑writing lifecycle—from researching potential funders to post‑award reporting—providing concrete examples, practical checklists, and tips you can apply to any grant proposal.  

---  

## 1. Researching Funders  

### 1.1 Types of Funders  

| Funder Type | Typical Funding Sources | Example Organizations |
|-------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Foundation** | Endowments, individual donations | Ford Foundation, Robert Wood Johnson Foundation |
| **Government Agency** | Taxpayer dollars, legislative allocations | NIH, Department of Education, HUD |
| **Corporate** | CSR budgets, marketing initiatives | Coca‑Cola Foundation, Google.org |
| **International/Global** | Foreign government aid, multilateral institutions | World Bank, UNICEF |
| **Community/Local** | Local business leagues, charitable trusts | Local United Way, regional foundations |

### 1.2 How to Conduct Effective Funder Research  

1. **Create a Funder Database** – Use tools like *Foundation Directory Online*, *GrantStation*, or *Guidestar* to log funders, grant deadlines, funding priorities, and contact persons.  
2. **Read Annual Reports & Priority Statements** – These documents reveal strategic themes, geographic focus, and any “strategic priorities” for the upcoming year.  
3. **Review Past Grantees** – Look at the funder’s *grantee reports* or *publicized success stories* to understand what types of proposals get funded.  
4. **Network with Peers** – Attend nonprofit conferences, join LinkedIn groups, and ask other grantees about their experiences.  
5. **Set Up Alerts** – Use *Google Alerts* or funder email newsletters to stay updated on new requests for proposals (RFPs) and deadlines.  

### 1.3 Practical Checklist for Funder Research  

- [ ] **Log each funder** in a spreadsheet with columns: Name, Website, Mission, Funding Focus, Typical Grant Size, Deadline, Contact, Status (Open/Closed/Upcoming).  
- [ ] **Read the latest request for proposals** (RFP) or guidelines. Highlight key eligibility, geographic, and programmatic criteria.  
- [ ] **Identify 3–5 recent grantees** and note the grant amount, project title, and outcome metrics they reported.  
- [ ] **Note any “strategic priorities”** (e.g., climate resilience, youth STEM, health equity) and align your organization’s mission accordingly.  
- [ ] **Set calendar reminders** for deadline dates, proposal submission windows, and funder reporting due dates.  
- [ ] **Document contact information** for program officers and keep a relationship log (date of contact, outcome, next steps).  

### 1.4 Example: Funder Research Summary  

> **Funder:** The **Kresge Foundation** – Health Program  
> **Mission:** Improve health outcomes in underserved communities.  
> **Recent Grants:** $2.5M to “Community Health Hubs” (2022), $1.8M to “Youth Nutrition Initiatives” (2023).  
> **Priority Areas:** Preventive care, mental health, healthy food access.  
> **Eligibility:** 501(c)(3) nonprofits serving low‑income neighborhoods.  
> **Next Steps:** Draft a proposal aligning with “healthy food access” and submit by **Oct 15, 2024**.

## 2. Crafting a Compelling Needs Statement  

### 2.1 What Makes a Needs Statement Powerful?  

- **Data‑Driven:** Use credible statistics (census data, health department reports, academic studies).  
- **Specific:** Identify who is affected, how many, and where.  
- **Urgent:** Highlight the immediacy of the problem (e.g., rising homelessness, increasing dropout rates).  
- **Local Context:** Explain how the issue uniquely impacts your community.  
- **Solution‑Oriented:** While stating the need, hint at the proposed solution (your program).  

### 2.2 Step‑by‑Step Process  

1. **Gather Evidence** – Compile local, state, and national data sets.  
2. **Define the Population** – Age, ethnicity, income level, geography.  
3. **Quantify the Gap** – Show current service capacity vs. need.  
4. **Tell the Story** – Use a narrative that humanizes the data (quotes, case studies).  
5. **Link to Your Mission** – Demonstrate why addressing this need aligns with your organization’s purpose.  

### 2.3 Example Needs Statement  

> **Community:** Downtown Minneapolis, MN (population 45,000; 22% below poverty line).  
> **Issue:** The **City Health Department** reports a **40% increase** in reported asthma attacks among children ages 5‑12 over the past three years.  
> **Current Services:** The local clinic provides inhalers and occasional education, but there is no systematic program for environmental trigger mitigation or family education.  
> **Gap:** Only 30% of affected families receive comprehensive asthma management support.  
> **Why It Matters:** Uncontrolled asthma leads to missed school days, emergency room visits, and long‑term health complications.  
> **Our Solution:** Implement a **“Healthy Lungs, Happy Kids”** program that combines home environmental assessments, staff training, and family workshops.  

### 2.4 Practical Checklist for a Strong Needs Statement  

- [ ] **Include at least two credible data sources** (e.g., city health department, peer‑reviewed study).  
- [ ] **Specify the exact number of individuals affected** (or a clear estimate).  
- [ ] **Identify the geographic boundaries** (city, neighborhood, zip codes).  
- [ ] **Explain the consequences** of not addressing the need (health, economic, social).  
- [ ] **Reference any existing programs** and why they are insufficient.  
- [ ] **Connect the need to your organization’s mission** and values.  
- [ ] **Use plain language** while maintaining professionalism.

## 3. Developing the Project Narrative  

### 3.1 Core Components of the Narrative  

1. **Goals & Objectives** – Broad, long‑term vision vs. measurable, short‑term objectives.  
2. **Program Description** – What you will do, who you will serve, how you will do it.  
3. **Logic Model** – Visual representation of inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes.  
4. **Implementation Timeline** – Gantt‑style or milestone chart.  
5. **Sustainability Plan** – How the program will continue after grant funding ends.  

### 3.2 Logic Model Development  

A logic model is a roadmap that links resources to impact. It helps funders see *why* your program should work.  

#### Example Logic Model (Sample)  

```
[Inputs]
- Staff: 2 FTE program managers
- Funding: $150,000 grant
- Partnerships: Local school district, community health clinic

[Activities]
- Conduct needs assessments in 5 schools
- Deliver 12 weekly after‑school workshops (focus on nutrition & exercise)
- Provide parent education sessions (monthly)
- Distribute resource kits to families

[Outputs]
- 150 children enrolled
- 60 parent education sessions held
- 120 resource kits distributed

[Outcomes (Short‑term)]
- 80% of participants report increased knowledge of healthy eating
- 70% report willingness to try new physical activities

[Outcomes (Long‑term)]
- 20% reduction in BMI percentiles among participants after 12 months
- Improved academic performance (average GPA increase of 0.3)

[Impact]
- Healthier children, reduced healthcare costs, stronger community partnerships
```

### 3.3 Practical Checklist for the Project Narrative  

- [ ] **Write clear, SMART objectives** (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound).  
- [ ] **Describe each activity in concrete terms** (who does what, when, where).  
- [ ] **Include a visual logic model** (Excel table, PowerPoint slide, or infographic).  
- [ ] **Provide a timeline** with milestones and responsible parties.  
- [ ] **Explain how you will measure success** (indicators, data collection methods).  
- [ ] **Address potential risks** and mitigation strategies.  
- [ ] **Demonstrate alignment** between each activity and the overall goals.

## 4. Budget Development  

### 4.1 Budget Categories  

| Category | Description | Tips |
|----------|-------------|------|
| **Personnel** | Salaries, benefits, fringe costs | Use market salary surveys; include indirect costs if applicable. |
| **Travel** | Participant or staff travel for program delivery | Keep receipts; justify necessity. |
| **Materials & Supplies** | Books, kits, food, equipment | Itemize each line; include unit cost and quantity. |
| **Overhead/Indirect Costs** | Rent, utilities, admin expenses | Use a reasonable indirect cost rate (e.g., 10‑15%). |
| **Evaluation** | Surveys, data analysis, external evaluator | Show how evaluation data will be used. |

### 4.2 Budget Narrative  

The budget narrative explains each line item, justifies costs, and shows how they support program activities.  

**Example Budget Narrative Excerpt:**  

> *Personnel: Two program managers ($85,000 each) will lead curriculum development, staff training, and participant outreach. Benefits (20% of salaries) are included to reflect true labor costs.*  
> *Materials & Supplies: 150 resource kits at $30 each ($4,500) will be distributed to families to support behavior change at home.*  

### 4.3 Practical Checklist for Budget Creation  

- [ ] **Create a line‑item budget** with a clear heading (e.g., “Personnel – Program Manager”).  
- [ ] **Attach a narrative** that explains each line item, referencing the logic model.  
- [ ] **Include indirect cost rate** if your organization negotiates an indirect cost agreement.  
- [ ] **Use realistic cost figures** (research market rates, obtain quotes).  
- [ ] **Ensure totals balance** with the requested grant amount.  
- [ ] **Highlight any in‑kind contributions** (donated services, volunteer hours).  
- [ ] **Proofread for formatting consistency** (fonts, numbers, currency symbols).

## 5. Evaluation & Metrics  

### 5.1 Why Evaluation Matters  

- Demonstrates accountability to funders.  
- Provides data for program improvement.  
- Builds evidence for future funding.  

### 5.2 Key Evaluation Components  

1. **Logic‑Based Indicators** – Tie each outcome back to the logic model.  
2. **Data Collection Methods** – Surveys, interviews, focus groups, administrative records.  
3. **Baseline & Follow‑up Data** – Measure change over time.  
4. **Evaluation Plan Timeline** – When data will be collected, analyzed, and reported.  

### 5.3 Example Evaluation Plan  

> **Program:** “Healthy Lungs, Happy Kids”  
> **Evaluation Questions:**  
> - Q1: How much does children’s knowledge of asthma triggers increase after the workshop series?  
> - Q2: What is the change in participants’ BMI percentiles after 12 months?  
> - Q3: How satisfied are parents with the resources provided?  
>   
> **Indicators:**  
> - Knowledge gain (pre‑post survey scores)  
> - BMI percentile change (medical records)  
> - Parent satisfaction (Likert‑scale survey)  
>   
> **Data Collection:**  
> - Baseline survey (Week 0)  
> - Mid‑program survey (Week 6)  
> - End‑of‑program survey (Week 12)  
> - 12‑month follow‑up health data (clinic records)  
>   
> **Analysis:** Descriptive statistics, paired t‑tests for knowledge and BMI changes, thematic analysis for open‑ended feedback.  

### 5.4 Practical Checklist for Evaluation  

- [ ] **Define clear evaluation questions** that align with program goals.  
- [ ] **Select appropriate indicators** (quantitative and qualitative).  
> **Plan data collection methods** (surveys, interviews, observations).  
- [ ] **Obtain IRB or ethical approval** if human subjects are involved.  
- [ ] **Create a data management plan** (storage, confidentiality, backup).  
- [ ] **Schedule data collection points** in the implementation timeline.  
- [ ] **Draft reporting templates** for interim and final reports.

## 6. Post‑Award Management  

### 6.1 Reporting Requirements  

- **Progress Reports** (often quarterly) – Describe activities completed, challenges, and next steps.  
- **Financial Reports** – Provide an accounting of grant expenditures, supporting receipts, and budget variance explanations.  
- **Final Report** – Summarize outcomes, lessons learned, and recommendations for scaling.  

### 6.2 Financial Management Best Practices  

1. **Separate Grant Accounts** – Keep a distinct ledger for each grant to avoid commingling.  
2. **Track Expenditures in Real‑Time** – Use accounting software (e.g., QuickBooks, Xero) with project codes.  
3. **Maintain Documentation** – Receipts, invoices, approved purchase orders, and approval signatures.  
4. **Reconciliation** – Monthly reconciliation of grant balances vs. ledger entries.  

### 6.3 Communication with Funder  

- **Prompt Responses:** Reply to funder inquiries within 48‑72 hours.  
- **Transparency:** Share both successes and challenges openly.  
- **Relationship Building:** Schedule a brief check‑in call mid‑project to reinforce partnership.  

### 6.4 Practical Checklist for Post‑Award Management  

- [ ] **Create a project timeline** with reporting deadlines and deliverable dates.  
- [ ] **Assign a grant manager** responsible for all communications and documentation.  
- [ ] **Set up a tracking spreadsheet** for budget expenditures, burn‑down, and variance.  
- [ ] **Prepare draft reports** at least two weeks before the due date for internal review.  
- [ ] **Proofread all reports** for accuracy, grammar, and compliance with funder guidelines.  
- [ ] **Maintain a “grant folder”** (digital and physical) containing all required documents.  
- [ ] **Conduct a mid‑term review** (internal) to identify risks and adjust activities.

## 7. Continuous Improvement & Future Grant Strategies  

### 7.1 Lessons Learned Workshop  

- **Gather Data:** Use evaluation results, financial reports, and stakeholder feedback.  
- **Identify Successes & Gaps:** What worked well? Where did you encounter obstacles?  
- **Document Best Practices:** Create a “Grant Writing Playbook” for your organization.  
- **Update Logic Models:** Refine based on new insights or changing community needs.  

### 7.2 Building a Sustainable Grant Pipeline  

- **Diversify Funder Portfolio:** Aim for at least 30% government, 30% foundations, 20% corporate, 20% individual donors.  
- **Cultivate Relationships:** Attend funder conferences, share success stories, and invite program officers to site visits.  
- **Leverage Past Successes:** Use case studies and testimonials in future proposals.  
- **Invest in Capacity Building:** Provide staff training on grant writing, budgeting, and evaluation.  

### 7.3 Practical Checklist for Future Grant Development  

- [ ] **Review past grant cycles** (what was funded, what was not).  
- [ ] **Identify new funding opportunities** (set up alerts, subscribe to newsletters).  
- [ ] **Update your organization’s logic model** to reflect lessons learned.  
- [ ] **Create a grant calendar** for the next 12 months with deadlines and internal milestones.  
- [ ] **Develop a “grant story”** (short video, infographic) to attach to proposals.  
- [ ] **Seek feedback** from colleagues or mentors on draft proposals.  
- [ ] **Maintain a grant writing toolkit** (templates, guidelines, contact lists).

## Conclusion  

Grant writing is a cyclical process of research, storytelling, planning, execution, and learning. By following the best practices outlined above—rigorously researching funders, crafting data‑driven needs statements, building robust project narratives with clear logic models, preparing meticulous budgets, implementing rigorous evaluation, and managing post‑award responsibilities—you position your nonprofit for sustained funding success.  

Remember: each grant is an opportunity to demonstrate your organization’s capacity, impact, and commitment to the communities you serve. Treat every proposal as a partnership, and let transparency, accountability, and continuous improvement be the hallmarks of your grant‑writing practice.
