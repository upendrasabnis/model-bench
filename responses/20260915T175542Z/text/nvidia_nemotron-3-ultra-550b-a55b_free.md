NONPROFIT GRANT WRITING BEST PRACTICES  
A COMPREHENSIVE GUIDE FROM RESEARCH TO POST‑AWARD REPORTING  

INTRODUCTION  
Grant writing is a strategic process that aligns organizational mission with funder priorities.  
Success depends on thorough research, clear storytelling, realistic budgets, and rigorous follow‑up.  
This guide walks through each phase with concrete examples and practical checklists.  

UNDERSTANDING THE GRANT LANDSCAPE  
Identify the types of funders: foundations, government agencies, corporations, and individual donors.  
Recognize funding cycles: annual, rolling, and emergency deadlines.  
Differentiate between restricted, unrestricted, and capacity‑building grants.  
Track trends such as increased emphasis on equity, data‑driven outcomes, and collaborative models.  

RESEARCHING FUNDERS  
Create a master funder spreadsheet with columns: name, focus areas, geographic limits, average award size, deadline, contact person, and application format.  
Use databases like Foundation Directory Online, GrantStation, and USAspending.gov.  
Review the funder’s 990‑PF or annual report to understand giving history.  
Search for “request for proposals” (RFPs) and “funding priorities” on the funder’s website.  
Set up Google Alerts for the funder’s name and key program officers.  

CHECKLIST: FUNDER RESEARCH  
- Funder name and contact entered  
- Mission alignment confirmed  
- Geographic eligibility verified  
- Average grant size recorded  
- Deadline noted with time zone  
- Application format identified (online portal, PDF, email)  
- Recent grantees listed for reference  

BUILDING RELATIONSHIPS WITH FUNDERS  
Attend funder‑hosted webinars, site visits, and networking events.  
Schedule a brief introductory call with the program officer before writing.  
Ask clarifying questions about evaluation expectations and reporting templates.  
Share a one‑page concept note to gauge interest without a full proposal.  
Maintain a contact log with date, discussion points, and follow‑up actions.  

CHECKLIST: RELATIONSHIP BUILDING  
- Introductory email sent within 48 hours of event  
- Call scheduled and prepared agenda  
- Concept note drafted and reviewed internally  
- Follow‑up thank‑you note sent  
- Contact log updated  

CRAFTING A COMPELLING NEEDS STATEMENT  
Start with data: cite local statistics, census data, or community health assessments.  
Tell a human story: include a brief vignette of a beneficiary.  
Connect the need to the funder’s stated priorities.  
Avoid jargon; use clear, concise language.  
Limit the needs statement to 300‑500 words for most applications.  

EXAMPLE NEEDS STATEMENT (EXCERPT)  
“In the Riverside neighborhood, 38 % of children under five live below the federal poverty line (U.S. Census 2023).  
Maria, a single mother of two, reports that her children miss an average of 12 school days per year due to untreated asthma.  
The Riverside Health Initiative seeks $150,000 to expand mobile clinic visits, directly addressing the funder’s focus on reducing health disparities in underserved urban communities.”  

CHECKLIST: NEEDS STATEMENT  
- Quantitative data sourced and cited  
- Qualitative story included  
- Direct link to funder priority demonstrated  
- Word count within limits  
- Reviewed for clarity and impact  

DEVELOPING GOALS, OBJECTIVES, AND OUTCOMES  
Write one overarching goal that reflects the mission.  
Break the goal into 3‑5 SMART objectives (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound).  
Define short‑term outputs (activities completed) and long‑term outcomes (changes in condition).  
Align each objective with a logic model component.  

EXAMPLE GOAL AND OBJECTIVES  
Goal: Reduce childhood asthma exacerbations in Riverside by 25 % within three years.  
Objective 1: Conduct 120 mobile clinic visits in Year 1 (output).  
Objective 2: Achieve a 15 % increase in asthma action plan adoption among enrolled families by end of Year 2 (outcome).  
Objective 3: Decrease emergency department visits for asthma by 10 % by Year 3 (impact).  

CHECKLIST: GOALS & OBJECTIVES  
- Goal statement concise and mission‑aligned  
- Each objective SMART  
- Outputs and outcomes distinguished  
- Objectives mapped to logic model  

BUILDING A LOGIC MODEL  
Components: Inputs, Activities, Outputs, Short‑term Outcomes, Long‑term Outcomes, Impact.  
Use a visual table or flowchart; many funders require a one‑page logic model.  
Include assumptions and external factors that could affect results.  

LOGIC MODEL TEMPLATE (TEXT VERSION)  
Inputs: Funding $150,000; staff 2 FTE; mobile van; medical supplies.  
Activities: Schedule visits; provide screenings; educate families; refer to primary care.  
Outputs: 120 visits; 300 screenings; 250 education sessions; 180 referrals.  
Short‑term Outcomes: Increased knowledge of asthma triggers; action plans created.  
Long‑term Outcomes: Reduced symptom days; improved school attendance.  
Impact: 25 % reduction in asthma exacerbations community‑wide.  

CHECKLIST: LOGIC MODEL  
- All six components populated  
- Assumptions listed  
- External factors noted  
- One‑page format ready for attachment  

DESIGNING THE PROGRAM NARRATIVE  
Follow the funder’s required sections: background, approach, timeline, staffing, partnerships, evaluation.  
Use headings that mirror the RFP.  
Write in active voice; keep paragraphs under 150 words.  
Integrate evidence‑based practices and cite relevant research.  
Highlight organizational capacity: past successes, staff expertise, governance.  

EXAMPLE NARRATIVE OPENING  
“The Riverside Health Initiative (RHI) has operated a community health worker program since 2015, serving over 2,000 families annually.  
Our recent evaluation (2022) demonstrated a 12 % reduction in missed school days among participants.  
Building on this track record, RHI proposes the Mobile Asthma Clinic to extend services directly into neighborhoods with the highest need.”  

CHECKLIST: PROGRAM NARRATIVE  
- All RFP sections addressed  
- Evidence citations included  
- Organizational capacity highlighted  
- Language clear, active, and jargon‑free  
- Word count within limits  

DEVELOPING A DETAILED BUDGET  
Separate budget into personnel, fringe benefits, travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, indirect costs, and other.  
Justify each line item in a budget narrative.  
Use the funder’s budget template if provided; otherwise follow standard nonprofit budgeting guidelines.  
Include a contingency line (typically 3‑5 % of total direct costs).  
Show cost‑share or in‑kind contributions if required.  

BUDGET EXAMPLE (YEAR 1)  
Personnel: Program Director 0.5 FTE $35,000; Community Health Worker 1 FTE $45,000.  
Fringe Benefits (25 %): $20,000.  
Travel: Mobile van fuel $8,000; staff mileage $2,000.  
Equipment: Portable spirometer $12,000.  
Supplies: Asthma education kits $5,000.  
Contractual: Data analyst $10,000.  
Indirect Costs (10 % MTDC): $13,500.  
Total Direct Costs: $137,000.  
Total Requested: $150,500 (includes $13,500 indirect).  

CHECKLIST: BUDGET  
- All categories completed  
- Narrative explains each cost  
- Indirect rate matches funder policy  
- Contingency included  
- Cost‑share documented  
- Totals reconcile with request  

CREATING AN EVALUATION PLAN  
Define evaluation questions linked to objectives.  
Select methods: quantitative (pre/post surveys, health records), qualitative (focus groups, interviews).  
Specify data sources, collection timeline, and responsible staff.  
Describe data analysis plan and how results will inform program improvement.  
Include a dissemination strategy for findings.  

EVALUATION PLAN OUTLINE  
Question 1: Did mobile clinic visits increase asthma action plan adoption?  
Method: Pre‑visit survey vs. 6‑month follow‑up survey.  
Data Source: Electronic health record, family questionnaire.  
Timeline: Baseline at enrollment; follow‑up at 6 months.  
Analysis: Paired t‑test for proportion change.  
Reporting: Quarterly internal brief; final report to funder.  

CHECKLIST: EVALUATION PLAN  
- Evaluation questions mapped to objectives  
- Methods appropriate and feasible  
- Data collection tools identified  
- Timeline realistic  
- Analysis plan described  
- Dissemination plan included  

ADDRESSING SUSTAINABILITY  
Explain how the program will continue after grant funds end.  
Identify revenue streams: fee‑for‑service, Medicaid reimbursement, corporate sponsorships, individual giving.  
Describe capacity‑building activities: staff training, volunteer recruitment, policy advocacy.  
Provide a sustainability timeline with milestones.  

SUSTAINABILITY EXAMPLE  
Year 1‑2: Grant‑funded pilot; collect outcome data.  
Year 3: Apply for Medicaid reimbursement for home‑based asthma education.  
Year 4: Launch community fundraising campaign targeting $50,000.  
Year 5: Integrate mobile clinic into county health department budget.  

CHECKLIST: SUSTAINABILITY  
- Multiple revenue sources identified  
- Timeline with concrete milestones  
- Organizational commitment documented  
- Policy or systemic change strategies noted  

PRE‑SUBMISSION CHECKLIST  
- All required documents compiled (cover letter, narrative, budget, logic model, letters of support, IRS determination letter).  
- Formatting follows funder specifications (font, margins, file type).  
- Page limits respected.  
- Internal review completed by at least two staff members.  
- Budget totals match narrative.  
- Contact information current.  
- Submission portal tested (login, upload).  
- Confirmation receipt saved.  

SUBMISSION BEST PRACTICES  
Submit at least 48 hours before deadline to avoid technical issues.  
Use a dedicated email address for grant correspondence.  
Keep a copy of the full submitted package in a secure folder.  
Notify key stakeholders (board, program staff) of submission.  

POST‑AWARD MANAGEMENT  
Set up a grant management calendar with all reporting deadlines.  
Assign a grant manager responsible for compliance.  
Establish financial tracking: separate grant code in accounting system.  
Conduct a kickoff meeting with program team to review deliverables.  

CHECKLIST: POST‑AWARD SETUP  
- Grant calendar created  
- Grant manager designated  
- Accounting code established  
- Kickoff meeting held  
- Document repository organized  

FINANCIAL REPORTING  
Follow funder’s financial report template (usually quarterly or semi‑annual).  
Reconcile expenditures to budget line items monthly.  
Document any budget modifications with prior approval.  
Maintain receipts and invoices for audit readiness.  

PROGRAMMATIC REPORTING  
Report on outputs, outcomes, and any deviations from the logic model.  
Use data visualizations (charts, tables) to illustrate progress.  
Include success stories and lessons learned.  
Address challenges and corrective actions taken.  

EXAMPLE QUARTERLY REPORT SNAPSHOT  
Outputs Q1: 30 mobile visits completed (target 30).  
Outcome: 18 families received asthma action plans (target 20).  
Challenge: Van breakdown delayed two visits; repaired within 48 hours.  
Corrective Action: Added backup vehicle contract.  

CHECKLIST: REPORTING  
- Financial report matches accounting records  
- Programmatic report aligns with logic model  
- Data visualizations clear and labeled  
- Narrative includes qualitative insights  
- Submitted before deadline  
- Confirmation receipt archived  

COMPLIANCE AND AUDIT PREPARATION  
Retain all grant documents for the period required by the funder (typically 3‑5 years).  
Conduct internal audit annually; address findings promptly.  
Stay current on federal regulations (e.g., 2 CFR 200) if receiving federal funds.  
Train staff on allowable vs. unallowable costs.  

CHECKLIST: COMPLIANCE  
- Document retention schedule posted  
- Internal audit completed  
- Staff training records up to date  
- Allowable cost policy reviewed  

COMMON PITFALLS AND HOW TO AVOID THEM  
1. Misaligned mission – always map need to funder priority.  
2. Over‑promising outcomes – set realistic, measurable targets.  
3. Incomplete budgets – double‑check every line item and narrative.  
4. Ignoring reporting requirements – build reporting into project plan from day one.  
5. Last‑minute submission – adopt a 48‑hour buffer rule.

FINAL THOUGHTS  
Grant writing is an iterative cycle of research, relationship‑building, storytelling, and accountability.  
Embedding best practices at each stage increases win rates and strengthens organizational capacity.  
Use the checklists in this guide as living documents; update them after each grant cycle.
