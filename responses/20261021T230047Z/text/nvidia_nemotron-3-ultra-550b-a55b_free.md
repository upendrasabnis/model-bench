I. INTRODUCTION
Nonprofit grant writing is a disciplined process that blends research, storytelling, data analysis, and project management.
A well‑crafted proposal not only secures funding but also builds credibility with funders and strengthens organizational capacity.
This guide walks through every major phase — from identifying the right funders to post‑award reporting — with concrete examples and practical checklists you can copy into your workflow.

II. RESEARCHING FUNDERS
A. Define Your Funding Landscape
1. List program areas that align with your mission (e.g., youth literacy, climate resilience, health equity).
2. Identify geographic focus (local, regional, national, international).
3. Determine funding types needed (project grants, capacity‑building, general operating, capital).

B. Build a Funder Database
- Use Foundation Directory Online, GrantStation, Guidestar, and local community foundation portals.
- Track: Funder name, mission, giving history, average grant size, application deadlines, contact person, eligibility restrictions.
- Example entry:
  Funder: Greenfield Family Foundation
  Mission: Environmental education for underserved youth
  Avg. Grant: $25,000‑$75,000
  Deadline: March 15 (annual)
  Contact: Maria Lopez, Program Officer (mlopez@greenfield.org)
  Eligibility: 501(c)(3) nonprofits serving grades 6‑12 in the Midwest

C. Prioritize Prospects
- Score each funder on alignment (0‑5), capacity (0‑5), and likelihood of success (0‑5).
- Focus first on high‑alignment, high‑capacity funders with a history of funding similar projects.

D. Funder Research Checklist
- [ ] Mission match ≥ 4/5
- [ ] Geographic eligibility confirmed
- [ ] Grant size fits project budget
- [ ] Deadline allows adequate preparation time (≥ 8 weeks)
- [ ] Contact person identified for relationship building
- [ ] Past grantees reviewed for fit and reporting expectations

III. CRAFTING A COMPELLING NEEDS STATEMENT
A. Structure of a Strong Needs Statement
1. Problem Definition – quantitative and qualitative evidence.
2. Target Population – demographics, size, specific vulnerabilities.
3. Gap Analysis – what existing services miss.
4. Urgency – why funding now matters.
5. Connection to Funder Priorities – explicit linkage.

B. Data Sources
- Census Bureau, American Community Survey, state health departments, school district reports, peer‑reviewed studies.
- Local anecdotal evidence: client testimonials, case manager notes, community focus groups.

C. Example Needs Statement (Youth Literacy Program)
"In the Riverbend School District, 38 % of third‑grade students read below grade level (State Dept. of Education, 2023). Low‑income families constitute 62 % of the student body, and only 12 % have access to after‑school tutoring. Existing school‑based interventions serve 150 students annually, leaving an estimated 1,200 eligible children without support. The COVID‑19 pandemic widened this gap by an additional 15 % (National Assessment of Educational Progress, 2022). The Greenfield Family Foundation’s focus on environmental education for underserved youth aligns directly with our plan to integrate literacy with outdoor learning modules."

D. Needs Statement Checklist
- [ ] Cite at least three recent data points (≤ 3 years old)
- [ ] Define target population with numbers
- [ ] Identify service gaps quantitatively
- [ ] State urgency with a time‑sensitive factor
- [ ] Explicitly reference funder’s stated priorities
- [ ] Keep length ≤ 350 words for most applications

IV. DEVELOPING GOALS, OBJECTIVES, AND OUTCOMES
A. SMART Goals Framework
- Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time‑bound.
- Example Goal: "Increase third‑grade reading proficiency in Riverbend District by 12 percentage points within 24 months."

B. Objectives vs. Outcomes
- Objectives = activities/outputs (e.g., "Deliver 48 tutoring sessions per semester").
- Outcomes = changes in knowledge, behavior, condition (e.g., "Students improve oral reading fluency by 20 words per minute").

C. Logic Model Overview
- Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Short‑term Outcomes → Long‑term Impact.
- Visual logic models help funders see the theory of change at a glance.

D. Logic Model Checklist
- [ ] All inputs listed (staff, volunteers, curriculum, space, technology)
- [ ] Activities linked to each objective
- [ ] Outputs quantified (sessions, participants, materials distributed)
- [ ] Short‑term outcomes measurable within grant period
- [ ] Long‑term impact aligned with organizational mission
- [ ] Assumptions and external factors documented

V. DESIGNING THE PROGRAM PLAN
A. Program Narrative Components
1. Program Description – what, where, who, how.
2. Staffing Plan – roles, qualifications, % FTE dedicated.
3. Timeline – Gantt‑style milestones with responsible parties.
4. Partnerships – MOUs, letters of support, shared resources.
5. Sustainability – plan for continuation after grant ends.

B. Sample Timeline (12‑Month Grant)
Month 1‑2: Hire program coordinator; finalize curriculum; sign MOUs.
Month 3‑4: Recruit 30 volunteer tutors; conduct background checks.
Month 5‑6: Launch pilot with 60 students; collect baseline literacy data.
Month 7‑9: Expand to 120 students; mid‑term evaluation.
Month 10‑12: Full cohort 180 students; final data collection; prepare report.

C. Program Plan Checklist
- [ ] Narrative ≤ 2 pages (or funder limit)
- [ ] Staff bios attached (≤ 150 words each)
- [ ] Timeline includes start/end dates, milestones, responsible staff
- [ ] Partnership letters signed and dated
- [ ] Sustainability paragraph addresses diversified funding, earned income, or policy change

VI. BUDGET DEVELOPMENT
A. Budget Categories
1. Personnel (salaries, benefits, payroll taxes)
2. Fringe Benefits (health, retirement)
3. Travel (local mileage, conference attendance)
4. Equipment (computers, tablets > $5,000 each)
5. Supplies (books, art materials, printing)
6. Contractual (evaluators, consultants)
7. Indirect Costs (per funder’s approved rate or de minimis 10 %)
8. In‑Kind Contributions (volunteer hours, donated space)

B. Budget Narrative
- Explain each line item, justification, and calculation method.
- Example: "Personnel – Program Coordinator 0.75 FTE @ $48,000/yr = $36,000. Includes 15 % fringe = $5,400."

D. Budget Checklist
- [ ] All line items match narrative descriptions
- [ ] Salaries reflect current organizational pay scale
- [ ] Fringe rate documented (e.g., 15 % of salaries)
- [ ] Travel rates follow federal GSA per diem or funder policy
- [ ] Equipment > $5,000 justified with depreciation schedule
- [ ] Indirect cost rate approved or de minimis applied
- [ ] In‑kind valued using Independent Sector volunteer rate
- [ ] Totals reconcile across summary and detail sheets
- [ ] Budget fits within funder’s maximum award

VII. EVALUATION PLAN
A. Types of Evaluation
1. Process Evaluation – fidelity of implementation.
2. Outcome Evaluation – achievement of short‑term outcomes.
3. Impact Evaluation – long‑term change (often beyond grant period).

B. Data Collection Methods
- Pre‑/post‑tests, attendance logs, surveys, focus groups, administrative records.
- Use validated instruments when possible (e.g., DIBELS for reading fluency).

C. Sample Evaluation Matrix
Outcome                     Indicator                     Data Source          Frequency   Responsible
Improved reading fluency    +20 wpm on DIBELS            Student assessments  Quarterly   Program Coordinator
Increased library visits    ≥2 visits/month per child    Library sign‑in      Monthly     Volunteer Lead
Parent engagement           75 % attend ≥1 workshop      Sign‑in sheets       Per event   Family Liaison

D. Evaluation Checklist
- [ ] Each objective linked to at least one measurable indicator
- [ ] Data collection tools identified and budgeted
- [ ] Baseline data plan described
- [ ] Analysis plan (statistical tests, qualitative coding) outlined
- [ ] Reporting schedule matches funder requirements
- [ ] IRB considerations addressed if human subjects involved

VIII. WRITING THE PROPOSAL NARRATIVE
A. General Writing Principles
- Use active voice, plain language, and concise sentences.
- Avoid jargon; define acronyms on first use.
- Tailor tone to funder (formal for government, conversational for family foundations).

B. Section‑by‑Section Tips
1. Cover Letter – one page, reference funder’s mission, summarize request, name contact.
2. Executive Summary – written last; 250‑350 words; includes need, solution, budget, impact.
3. Organizational Background – mission, history, key achievements, capacity.
4. Needs Statement – as crafted in Section III.
5. Program Description – goals, objectives, logic model, timeline.
6. Evaluation – matrix, methods, responsible staff.
7. Sustainability – diversification, earned revenue, policy advocacy.
8. Budget & Narrative – attached as separate files per funder guidelines.

C. Narrative Checklist
- [ ] All funder‑required sections present and in order
- [ ] Page limits respected (use 12‑pt Times New Roman, 1‑in margins)
- [ ] Consistent terminology throughout
- [ ] No orphaned references (every citation appears in bibliography)
- [ ] Proofread by at least two reviewers (one external)
- [ ] PDF/A format if electronic submission required

IX. SUBMISSION PROCESS
A. Pre‑Submission Review
- Verify eligibility, required attachments, formatting, and deadline time zone.
- Conduct a “mock review” using funder’s scoring rubric if available.

B. Electronic Portals
- Create account early; test upload of a dummy PDF.
- Name files per convention: OrgName_ProjectName_Budget.pdf

C. Hard‑Copy Submissions (if required)
- Print on high‑quality paper; bind with clips (no staples).
- Include a table of contents and tabbed dividers.

D. Submission Checklist
- [ ] All required documents compiled
- [ ] File naming convention followed
- [ ] Confirmation receipt saved (email or portal screenshot)
- [ ] Internal copy archived with version control
- [ ] Follow‑up calendar reminder set for 2 weeks post‑deadline

X. POST‑AWARD MANAGEMENT
A. Grant Agreement Review
- Note reporting dates, allowable cost changes, prior‑approval thresholds.
- Assign a Grant Manager (often the Program Coordinator).

B. Financial Tracking
- Set up separate cost center in accounting software.
- Reconcile monthly; flag variances >10 % for explanation.

C. Program Monitoring
- Weekly staff check‑ins; monthly dashboard updates (outputs, outcomes, budget burn rate).
- Adjust work plan with funder approval when scope changes.

D. Reporting Requirements
1. Progress Reports – narrative + quantitative metrics (usually quarterly).
2. Financial Reports – actual vs. budgeted expenditures.
3. Final Report – outcomes, lessons learned, sustainability plan, audited financials if required.

E. Sample Quarterly Report Outline
1. Executive Summary (½ page)
2. Activities Completed (table with dates, outputs)
3. Outcome Data (charts, statistical significance)
4. Challenges & Mitigation
5. Budget Status (percent spent, variance notes)
6. Upcoming Activities
7. Attachments (photos, testimonials, evaluation instruments)

F. Post‑Award Checklist
- [ ] Grant agreement signed and filed
- [ ] Cost center created; budget loaded
- [ ] Reporting calendar populated with due dates
- [ ] Staff trained on data collection tools
- [ ] Communication plan for funder updates (e.g., brief email at 30‑day mark)
- [ ] Audit preparation timeline if required

XI. BUILDING LONG‑TERM FUNDER RELATIONSHIPS
A. Stewardship Activities
- Thank‑you letter within 48 hours of award.
- Quarterly impact snapshots (one‑page infographic).
- Invite funder staff to site visits or program events.
- Share success stories in organizational newsletter and social media (with permission).

B. Renewal Strategy
- Begin renewal conversation 6‑9 months before grant ends.
- Present outcome data, participant testimonials, and a refined logic model for next phase.
- Align next proposal with any shifts in funder’s strategic plan.

C. Relationship Checklist
- [ ] Thank‑you letter sent
- [ ] Quarterly impact brief delivered
- [ ] Site visit scheduled (if feasible)
- [ ] Funder added to mailing list for annual report
- [ ] Renewal discussion calendar invite set

XII. COMMON PITFALLS AND HOW TO AVOID THEM
1. Misaligned Funder Fit – use the scoring matrix in Section II.
2. Vague Needs Statement – require at least three data citations.
3. Over‑ambitious Objectives – apply SMART test; pilot first.
4. Budget Inconsistencies – cross‑check narrative vs. spreadsheet line by line.
5. Weak Evaluation – embed evaluation design early; budget for evaluator.
6. Missed Deadlines – internal deadline 5 business days before funder deadline.
7. Poor Stewardship – assign a stewardship lead; schedule touchpoints.
