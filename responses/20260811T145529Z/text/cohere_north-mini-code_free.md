## 1. Introduction  

Grant compliance is the backbone of any successful funded project. Whether the money comes from a federal agency, a state government, or a private foundation, recipients must demonstrate that they are using the resources as intended, on time, and in accordance with the law. One of the most critical, yet often misunderstood, components of compliance is **acquittal reporting**—the formal process by which a grantee confirms that it has satisfied all obligations, returned unspent funds, or otherwise resolved the grant.  

Failure to comply can result in repayment demands, loss of future funding, legal action, or even criminal prosecution. This article walks you through what acquittal reporting is, the common requirements that both government and philanthropic funders impose, the record‑keeping and audit expectations, and—most importantly—how to avoid the most frequent compliance failures. Real‑world examples are woven throughout to illustrate best practices and pitfalls.

## 2. Understanding Acquittal Reporting  

### 2.1 Definition  

Acquittal reporting is the formal documentation submitted by a grantee to a funder that confirms the grant’s status at its conclusion. It typically includes:  

1. **Final financial status** – a statement of all expenditures, revenues, and any remaining balances.  
2. **Fund reconciliation** – a clear explanation of how the grant funds were allocated, including any over‑ or under‑expenditures.  
3. **Unspent funds** – either a plan to return surplus money or a justification for retaining it (if allowed).  
4. **Compliance certification** – a signed statement that the grantee has met all programmatic, reporting, and regulatory requirements.  

The term “acquittal” originates from the Latin *acquiescere* (to satisfy), and it is used in both public and private sector contexts.  

### 2.2 Why It Matters  

- **Legal obligation** – Many grant agreements explicitly require an acquittal report before the funder will release the grantee from liability.  
- **Fiscal closure** – Without an acquittal, the funder may hold the grantee responsible for future audit findings or cost recoveries.  
- **Future funding** – Demonstrating a clean acquittal can enhance a grantee’s reputation and improve odds of receiving subsequent grants.  

### 2.3 Real‑World Example: The “Healthy Communities” Grant  

The **U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS)** awarded a $5 million grant to the *Midwest Public Health Alliance* (MPHA) in 2018 to expand diabetes prevention programs. In 2022, MPHA submitted its acquittal report, which included:  

- A **final expenditure report** showing $4.8 million spent on community workshops, staff salaries, and evaluation tools.  
- A **$200,000 surplus** that was returned to HHS via a Treasury check.  
- A **compliance certification** signed by MPHA’s CEO, confirming that all program outcomes (e.g., 3,200 participants screened) were met.  

HHS accepted the report, closed the grant, and MPHA’s leadership cited the acquittal as a key factor in securing a $7 million continuation grant in 2023.

## 3. Common Requirements Across Government and Philanthropic Funders  

While each funder has its own set of rules, there are several **core compliance elements** that appear in most grant agreements, whether the source is federal, state, local, or private.  

### 3.1 Federal Grants  

| Requirement | Typical Source | Key Points |
|-------------|----------------|------------|
| **SAM.gov registration** | Federal Funding Accountability and Transparency Act (FFATA) | Grantees must be registered in the System for Award Management (SAM) and maintain active status throughout the award period. |
| **OMB A‑133 audit** | Office of Management and Budget | Grants over $750,000 require an independent audit performed according to Government Auditing Standards (GAGAS). |
| **Cost principles** | OMB Circular A‑87 (for states) / FAR (for contractors) | Only allowable, reasonable, and allocable costs may be charged. |
| **Matching or cost‑share** | Varies by program | Grantees must contribute a defined percentage of project costs unless waived. |
| **Reporting schedules** | Grant terms | Quarterly financial reports, progress reports, and final acquittal reports are usually required. |
| **Sub‑grant compliance** | If the grantee sub‑awards funds | Must ensure sub‑grantees also meet federal requirements. |

**Example:** The **National Science Foundation (NSF) Grant No. 1846572** required the University of XYZ to submit a **final performance report** (including acquittal) within 90 days of project end, provide a **public abstract**, and make all deliverables publicly available.  

### 3.2 State and Local Grants  

- **Uniform Grant Management System (UGMS)** – Many states use a centralized portal for registration, reporting, and acquittal.  
- **Local audit requirements** – Smaller jurisdictions often waive the OMB A‑133 audit but still require **internal audit certifications**.  
- **Programmatic outcomes** – Emphasis on measurable results, similar to federal models.  

**Real‑World Example:** The **California Department of Transportation (Caltrans)** awarded a $12 million Safe Streets grant to the *San Diego Transit Authority*. The grant required quarterly progress reports, a **final acquittal report** confirming that all pedestrian safety improvements were completed, and a **return of any unspent funds** within 30 days of project closeout.  

### 3.3 Philanthropic Foundations  

| Requirement | Typical Source | Key Points |
|-------------|----------------|------------|
| **Grant agreement clauses** | Foundation’s standard form | Usually include acquittal reporting, program reporting, and audit provisions. |
| **Audit thresholds** | Often lower than government | Many foundations require an audit if the grant exceeds $250,000 (or even $100,000). |
| **Use of funds** | Narrative focus | Emphasis on program impact rather than strict cost allocation. |
| **Reporting frequency** | Flexible | Can be annual or project‑specific; acquittal often tied to final report. |
| **Compliance certifications** | Legal sign‑off | May include a **certification of compliance** similar to federal forms. |

**Example:** The **Ford Foundation** awarded a $3 million grant to the *Global Education Initiative* (GEI) to support STEM education in underserved schools. The grant agreement required:  

- **Semi‑annual progress reports** with metrics on student enrollment and test scores.  
- **A final acquittal report** confirming that all curriculum materials were delivered and that the program achieved its stated learning outcomes.  
- **An independent audit** (conducted by a CPA firm) verifying that 90 % of funds were spent on curriculum development and teacher training, with the remaining 10 % covering administrative costs (within the foundation’s allowable limit).

## 4. Record‑Keeping: The Foundation of Compliance  

### 4.1 Documentation Hierarchy  

| Level | What It Is | Why It Matters |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| **Source documents** | Receipts, invoices, contracts, payroll registers | Provide the raw evidence for every transaction. |
| **Supporting schedules** | Detailed breakdowns of expenditures, time sheets, grant budgets | Enable auditors to trace amounts to source documents. |
| **Ledger entries** | General ledger, grant‑specific sub‑ledgers | Summarize financial activity for reporting. |
| **Management reports** | Quarterly financial statements, program status reports | Facilitate internal oversight and external reporting. |
| **Acquittal package** | Compiled set of all above plus certifications | The final deliverable to the funder. |

### 4.2 Digital vs. Physical  

- **Digital**: Faster retrieval, easier version control, searchable, but requires robust backup and security.  
- **Physical**: Tangible, acceptable for some auditors, but risk of loss, damage, or inefficient access.  

**Best practice:** Use a **cloud‑based grant management system (GMS)** that stores all source documents, automatically generates required reports, and enforces retention policies (e.g., keep records for at least 7 years).  

### 4.3 Real‑World Example: City Health Department  

The *Chicago Department of Public Health* (CDPH) received a **$15 million CDC grant** to expand vaccination outreach. CDPH adopted a **Microsoft Dynamics GP** GMS that:  

- Scanned every receipt into the system using mobile apps.  
- Linked each expense to the grant’s cost code.  
- Generated **monthly financial extracts** for CDC reporting.  
- Produced an **acquittal package** with a single click, including a **certified statement** signed electronically by the health commissioner.  

When CDC’s external auditors arrived in 2021, they praised CDPH’s **complete audit trail** and **timely acquittal submission**, noting that the department had “exemplary documentation practices.”

## 5. Audits: Types, Expectations, and How to Prepare  

### 5.1 Internal Audits  

- **Purpose:** Detect compliance gaps before an external auditor arrives.  
- **Frequency:** Often quarterly or semi‑annual for high‑risk grants.  
- **Scope:** Financial accuracy, program outcomes, sub‑grant compliance.  

**Tip:** Conduct **risk‑based internal audits**—focus on areas with higher dollar amounts, recent changes, or prior findings.  

### 5.2 External Audits  

| Auditor Type | Who Performs | Typical Scope |
|--------------|--------------|---------------|
| **Government external audit** | GAO, DCAA, state audit agencies | Compliance with federal statutes, cost principles, grant terms. |
| **Philanthropic audit** | Independent CPA firms (often engaged by the foundation) | Financial statements, program expenditures, compliance with grant agreement. |
| **Single‑audit (OMB A‑133)** | Independent certified public accountants (CPAs) | Uniform guidance (UG) compliance for entities receiving federal funds >$750k. |

**Key expectations:**  

1. **Audit trail** – All transactions must be traceable to source documents.  
2. **Compliance testing** – Verify that expenditures are allowable, that matching contributions are documented, and that reporting deadlines were met.  
3. **Materiality** – Auditors focus on items that could affect the fairness of financial statements.  

### 5.3 Real‑World Example: Nonprofit A’s Audit Experience  

*Nonprofit A* received a **$500,000 grant** from the **Robert Wood Johnson Foundation** to implement a community health program. The foundation required an audit because the grant exceeded the $250,000 threshold.  

- **Pre‑audit preparation:** Nonprofit A’s finance team performed an internal audit, discovered a **$12,000 over‑charge** for consulting services that was not allowable under the grant’s cost principles.  
- **Corrective action:** They returned the $12,000 to the grant account and updated the financial records.  
- **External audit outcome:** The independent CPA firm issued a **clean opinion**, noting that the over‑charge had been promptly corrected and the organization’s documentation was “exceptional.”  

Nonprofit A’s proactive internal audit not only avoided a potential audit finding but also strengthened its relationship with the foundation.

## 6. Most Frequent Compliance Failures and How to Avoid Them  

| Failure | Why It Happens | Consequence | Prevention Strategy |
|---------|----------------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Failure to report acquittals on time** | Overlooking final reporting deadlines, assuming “no news is good news.” | Funders may impose penalties, withhold future funding, or initiate recoupment. | Implement a **grant calendar** with automated reminders; assign a dedicated compliance officer. |
| **Misclassification of expenses** | Lack of clear cost‑allocation policies; confusing allowable vs. unallowable costs. | Audit findings, required repayment, possible legal action. | Use **grant‑specific cost codes**; train staff on OMB/A‑87 principles; perform monthly expense reviews. |
| **Inadequate documentation** | Relying on informal notes, missing supporting receipts, or failing to retain records long enough. | Auditors cannot verify transactions; may lead to disallowed costs. | Adopt a **document‑by‑exception** approach; store all source documents in a secure, searchable system; enforce a **7‑year retention policy**. |
| **Non‑compliance with federal registration (SAM.gov)** | Forgetting to renew registration or missing updates. | Grant may be deemed non‑compliant; potential de‑obligation. | Set **SAM renewal alerts**; integrate SAM status checks into the GMS. |
| **Improper handling of sub‑grants** | Not verifying sub‑grantee compliance, especially for pass‑through grants. | Primary grantee held liable for sub‑grantee failures. | Conduct **sub‑grantee due diligence**; require sub‑grantees to provide their own compliance certifications; perform periodic monitoring. |
| **Failure to meet matching or cost‑share requirements** | Underestimating the required contribution or misallocating funds. | Grant may be considered incomplete; funder may demand repayment. | Calculate matching requirements **up‑front**; track contributions in a separate ledger; obtain **third‑party verification** (e.g., bank statements, board resolutions). |
| **Late or incomplete progress reports** | Overloading staff with programmatic work; missing reporting deadlines. | Funders may impose liquidated damages; loss of confidence. | Use **template-based reporting**; schedule report writing as part of project milestones; automate data extraction from GMS. |
| **Ignoring program outcome data** | Focusing only on financials, neglecting the “what works” narrative. | Funders may deem the grant unsuccessful despite financial compliance. | Implement **monitoring and evaluation (M&E)** plans from day one; collect quantitative and qualitative data; report results in acquittal narrative. |

### 6.1 Real‑World Case Study: The “GrantGate” Scandal  

In 2018, the **U.S. Department of Education** discovered that a consortium of three universities had **failed to report acquittals** for a $30 million Title I grant. The consortium had instead **retained $4.5 million** for administrative overhead, citing “unforeseen expenses.”  

- **Findings:** The grant agreement required a final acquittal report within 60 days of project end, with any surplus returned. The consortium missed the deadline, continued to spend grant funds on unrelated research, and falsified progress reports.  
- **Consequences:** The Department initiated **cost recovery** of $4.5 million, imposed **civil penalties** of $1 million, and barred the universities from future federal grants for three years.  
- **Lessons learned:** The scandal highlighted the importance of **timely acquittal reporting**, **strict segregation of grant funds**, and **independent oversight** of sub‑awards.  

### 6.2 Proactive Checklist (Monthly)  

1. **Verify SAM registration** status.  
2. **Reconcile grant accounts** – compare budgeted vs. actual expenditures.  
3. **Update sub‑grantee compliance** records.  
4. **Generate draft progress report** and circulate for review.  
5. **Archive source documents** for the prior month.  
6. **Review matching contributions** – ensure they are properly documented.  
7. **Test the GMS reporting templates** for accuracy.

## 7. Strategies for Ongoing Compliance  

### 7.1 Policies and Procedures  

- **Write a Grant Compliance Manual** that outlines:  
  - Cost allowability rules (OMB A‑87, FAR, foundation guidelines).  
  - Reporting calendars (quarterly, annual, acquittal).  
  - Document retention schedule.  
  - Sub‑grant monitoring protocol.  
- **Assign a Grant Compliance Officer (GCO)** – ideally a senior accountant or program manager with authority to enforce standards.  

### 7.2 Training and Capacity Building  

- Conduct **annual compliance workshops** for finance staff, program managers, and principal investigators.  
- Use **scenario‑based e‑learning** modules that simulate audit situations and illustrate proper documentation.  
- Keep staff updated on **regulatory changes** (e.g., new OMB guidance, foundation policy updates).  

### 7.3 Technology Solutions  

| Tool | Function | Real‑World Success |
|------|----------|--------------------|
| **Grant Management System (GMS)** | Central repository for budgets, expenditures, reporting, and document storage. | *TechCorp* adopted *GrantFlow* and reduced audit preparation time from 6 weeks to 3 days. |
| **Automated reporting software** | Pulls data from accounting systems and populates funder templates. | *University of Michigan* used *Sentinel* to generate quarterly reports, cutting manual entry by 80 %. |
| **Document management platform** | Secure cloud storage with version control and audit trails. | *San Francisco Public Library* migrated all grant receipts to *Box*, enabling instant retrieval during a city audit. |
| **Compliance dashboards** | Real‑time monitoring of key indicators (e.g., spend vs. budget, matching status). | *Global Health Initiative* used a custom dashboard to flag a $200k overspend 2 months before the audit. |

### 7.4 Real‑World Example: TechCorp’s Grant Compliance System  

TechCorp, a mid‑size software firm, manages **over 30 active grants** from the **National Institutes of Health (NIH)** and several private foundations. Their compliance framework includes:  

- **GrantFlow GMS**: Tracks each grant’s budget, allowable costs, and reporting milestones.

- **Integrated SAM checker**: Runs a daily script that flags expired registrations.  
- **Quarterly internal audit module**: Generates a risk score based on spend variance, documentation completeness, and matching status.  

After implementing this system, TechCorp **reduced audit findings by 75 %** over two years and **achieved a 100 % acquittal acceptance rate** with NIH and all foundation partners.
